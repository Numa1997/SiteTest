5 A whole population: the collection equation

Keep a ledger with one line per size. When a 10 µm and a 20 µm droplet merge, cross out one entry on each of their lines and write one on the line of 20.8 µm. Number falls by one per merger; mass is moved, never made.

5.1 Gains and losses

Let $n(m,t)\,dm$ be the number of drops per m³ of air with mass between $m$ and $m + dm$, and $K(m, m')$ the volume of air one drop of mass $m$ clears of partners of mass $m'$ per second, the kernel of chapters 1–4: $K = \pi(R + r)^2\,|v(R) - v(r)|\,E\,E_{\rm coal}$. Pairs meet at the rate $K n(m) n(m')\,dm\,dm'$ per m³. Add every way to make a drop of mass $m$, subtract every way to lose one:

$$\frac{\partial n(m)}{\partial t} = \frac12\int_0^m K(m', m - m')\,n(m')\,n(m - m')\,dm' - n(m)\int_0^\infty K(m, m')\,n(m')\,dm'. \tag{5.1}$$

The ½ counts each pair once: as $m'$ runs from 0 to $m$, the pair ($m'$, $m - m'$) is met twice. This is Smoluchowski's coagulation equation; in cloud physics it is called the stochastic collection equation (SCE). It is an equation for the mean: it assumes that the numbers of drops in different size classes are uncorrelated (Gillespie 1972). Chapter 6 tests that.

Multiply (5.1) by any $\phi(m)$ and integrate over $m$. In the gain term, name the second incoming mass $m'' = m - m'$: as $m$ runs over all masses and $m'$ from 0 to $m$, the pair $(m', m'')$ runs over all positive pairs, and $\phi(m)$ becomes $\phi(m' + m'')$. The gain is then $\tfrac12\iint K\,n(m')\,n(m'')\,\phi(m' + m'')\,dm'\,dm''$. The loss term is $\iint K\,n(m)\,n(m')\,\phi(m)\,dm\,dm'$; because $K$ is the same whichever drop is named first, this equals the same integral with $\phi(m')$, so it is half the integral with $\phi(m) + \phi(m')$. Renaming the variables of the gain term and subtracting:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int\phi\,n\,dm = \frac12\iint K\,n(m)\,n(m')\,\big[\phi(m + m') - \phi(m) - \phi(m')\big]\,dm\,dm'. \tag{5.2}$$

Each merger replaces $\phi(m) + \phi(m')$ by $\phi(m + m')$. With $\phi = m$ the bracket is zero: mass is conserved exactly. With $\phi = 1$ it is $-1$: number falls. With $\phi = m^2$ it is $2mm' > 0$: merging always builds bigger drops.

5.2 The thesis's bookkeeping as a special case

The thesis kept two fixed groups: collectors that grow smoothly by (1.1) and never meet each other, and droplets that only disappear. We call this the gravitational collection equation (GCE). It follows from (5.1) if only collector–droplet pairs are allowed and each collector's jumps (one droplet at a time) are replaced by their average drift: a collector of mass $M$ then grows at $A(M) = \int m\,K(M,m)\,n(m)\,dm$, which is (1.1) summed over droplet sizes. What it leaves out: collectors meeting collectors, droplets meeting droplets, and the spread between collectors that are lucky or unlucky in their captures.

Method · one time step of the thesis's bookkeeping, as the model takes it
  1. Each bin of the collector mode is a collector class: its number $N_c$ never changes, its drop mass $m_c$ grows. Each bin of the droplet mode is a pool: its drop mass never changes, its number $n_p$ falls.
  2. A droplet of pool $p$ is caught at the rate $\Lambda_p = \sum_c N_cK_{cp}$, summed over the collector classes larger than it. Over the step the pool shrinks exactly as $n_p \to n_p\,e^{-\Lambda_p\Delta t}$.
  3. The water the pool lost is shared among the collector classes in proportion to $N_cK_{cp}$, and each class's drops grow by their share: $m_c \to m_c + (\text{share})/N_c$.
The kernel is evaluated with the collectors' present radius in every step, so the collectors speed up as they grow, as in chapter 1.

5.3 Solving it on a grid

Sizes span more than three decades of radius (1 µm to 4 mm) and eleven of mass, so the bins are spaced logarithmically: bin $k$ holds drops of mass $x_k = x_0\,2^{k/s}$, with $x_0$ the mass of a 1 µm sphere and $s$ bins per doubling of mass. In a time step $\Delta t$, bins $i < j$ produce $C_{ij} = K_{ij}N_iN_j\Delta t$ mergers per m³, and a bin with itself $C_{ii} = \tfrac12 K_{ii}N_i^2\Delta t$ (each pair counted once). Each product has mass $x^* = x_i + x_j$, which almost never falls on a grid mass; it lies between $x_k$ and $x_{k+1}$. Put the fraction $f$ of the products in bin $k + 1$ and $1 - f$ in bin $k$, and demand that both their number and their mass come out right:

$$q_k + q_{k+1} = C_{ij},\quad x_k q_k + x_{k+1} q_{k+1} = x^*C_{ij}\quad\Rightarrow\quad f = \frac{x^* - x_k}{x_{k+1} - x_k}. \tag{5.3}$$

A lever rule. A step may never take more drops from a bin than it holds (counting what the product returns to the same bin), and products above the last bin (4 mm) go to an overflow store that keeps their mass. The cost of (5.3): a product is smeared over two neighbouring bins, a numerical diffusion that only an exact test can measure.

Method · one time step of the collection equation, as the model takes it

Before the run: the grid masses $x_k$; the kernel $K_{ij}$ for every pair of bins, computed once (289 × 289 at 8 bins per doubling); and the starting numbers $N_k$, the lognormal density of section 5.1 at $r_k$ times the bin's width in $\ln r$. Then, in every step of $\Delta t$ = 1 s, the model goes through all pairs of bins $i \le j$, the smaller bin first, and for each pair does five things.

  1. Count the mergers. $C = K_{ij}N_iN_j\Delta t$, or $\tfrac12K_{ii}N_i^2\Delta t$ for a bin with itself.
  2. Find where the product goes. Its mass is $x^* = x_i + x_j$. Find the bin $k$ with $x_k \le x^* < x_{k+1}$ and the share $f$ from (5.3).
  3. Do not overdraw. $C$ may not exceed $N_i$. It may not exceed $N_j$ either, with one refinement: if the product lands back in bin $j$ ($k = j$), bin $j$ loses only the share $f$ of the mergers, so the limit is $N_j/f$. For a bin with itself the limit is $N_i/2$.
  4. Take out. Subtract $C$ from $N_i$ and from $N_j$ (or $2C$ from $N_i$ when $i = j$).
  5. Put in. Add $(1 - f)\,C$ to $N_k$ and $f\,C$ to $N_{k+1}$. If $x^*$ lies beyond the last bin, add $C$ and $Cx^*$ to the overflow store's number and mass.

The bins are changed in place: a later pair in the same step already sees what the earlier pairs left. Step 5 puts in exactly the mass step 4 took out, because $(1 - f)\,x_k + f\,x_{k+1} = x^*$; that is why the water books close to rounding error in every run. An hour is 3600 such steps.

Example 5.1 · one pair of bins in the first second
  1. The pair. At 8 bins per doubling neighbouring bins differ by a factor $2^{1/24}$ = 1.0293 in radius. Bin 72 is $r$ = 8.000 µm and holds $N_i = 1.549\times10^{7}$ droplets per m³ at the start. Bin 128 is $r$ = 40.317 µm and holds $N_j = 6.314\times10^{4}$ collectors per m³. Their kernel is $K_{ij} = \pi(R + r)^2\,\Delta v\,E\,E_{\rm coal} = 4.353\times10^{-10}$ m³/s, with $\Delta v$ = 0.1716 m/s and $E$ = 0.3459.
  2. Mergers. $C = K_{ij}N_iN_j\Delta t$ = 425.7 per m³ in the first second: each of these collectors catches 0.0067 droplets of that one bin per second, and 0.674 % of them catch one this second.
  3. The product. A 40.317 µm drop plus an 8 µm droplet is a 40.422 µm drop. That lies between bin 128 (40.317 µm) and bin 129 (41.499 µm).
  4. The lever. $f = (x^* - x_{128})/(x_{129} - x_{128})$ = 0.0863. In words: the droplet adds $x_i/x_j$ = 0.78 % to the collector's mass, and the next bin is 9.05 % heavier.
  5. The books. Bin 72 loses 425.7 droplets per m³. Bin 128 loses 425.7 collectors and gets $(1 - f)\times425.7$ back: a net loss of 36.7. Bin 129 gains 36.7.
Here the smearing of (5.3) can be seen at work. In truth every one of these collectors must catch about twelve droplets ($1/f$ = 11.6) before it has grown by one bin. The scheme instead lets one collector in twelve jump a whole bin at its first capture. The mean is right; the spread is too wide, and that is the numerical diffusion the Golovin test below measures.
Check: Golovin's exact solution
For the artificial kernel $K = b(m + m')$ and an exponential start (5.1) has an exact solution (Golovin 1963; the Checks tab shows how a Laplace transform turns (5.1) into a solvable equation for this kernel, and gives the solution). With $b$ = 1.5 m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹, a mean drop mass equal to that of a 10 µm sphere, and 1 g/m³, after 60 min the scheme's spectrum error falls from 0.428 at 2 bins per doubling to 0.150, 0.051 and 0.018 at 4, 8 and 16: an observed order of about 1.5. At 8 bins per doubling its mass error is $-4.58\times10^{-7}$, which is exactly the water the exponential puts below the grid's first bin; the scheme itself changes the mass by $2\times10^{-13}$.

5.4 The warm cloud

Now the real kernel. The thesis kernel uses Stokes speeds, E = 1 and (4.2) with Stokes speeds; the extended kernel uses the drag balance, the efficiencies of chapter 3 and (4.2) with the drag-balance speeds. Both run on 8 bins per doubling with $\Delta t$ = 1 s. These numerical choices matter little: halving the time step twice moves $t_{90}$ of the extended run by 0.005 min; going from 4 to 8 to 16 bins per doubling moves it from 13.35 to 13.47 to 13.53 min. The grid matters more than the clock, and both far less than the physics below: the numerical answer is good to about 0.1 min.

The kernel decides the speed more than the bookkeeping. Changing the kernel moves $t_{90}$ from 4.5 to 13.5 min. Changing the bookkeeping moves it by 2.3 min (thesis) or 5.1 min (extended). Within the kernel, the collision efficiency matters most; the fall speed matters in the population more than for one drop (chapter 2); coalescence hardly at all (chapter 4). The honest uncertainty of observable 1 within this model is the collision efficiency's: moving Langmuir's crossover (chapter 3) from 60 down to 2, where our E(40, 8) meets Hall's, moves $t_{90}$ from 13.5 down to 10.5 min, and Hall's table gives 11.4 min. So $t_{90}$ = 10.5 to 13.5 min, and the minutes after the decimal point are not physics.
