2 How fast a drop falls

Every rate in these notes is a difference of fall speeds, so the fall speed has to be right. Chapter 1 used Stokes' law. Here we derive it, see where it fails, and replace it by a drag balance that holds at every size in the study.

Draw a teaspoon slowly through honey. The honey closes in behind it at once, and doubling the speed doubles the push you feel. Swish the same spoon through water: the water piles up in front and swirls away behind, and doubling the speed roughly quadruples the push. In honey the fluid's viscosity sets the push; in water the momentum of the fluid thrown aside sets it. A cloud droplet lives in the honey world. A growing collector leaves it.

Stokes' drag, derived

Hold a sphere of radius $a$ at rest and let air stream past it at speed $U$. When the air's inertia is negligible (we will check when), the flow obeys the creeping-flow equations $\nabla p = \mu\nabla^2\mathbf u$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0$. For a flow symmetric about the stream direction, a stream function $\psi(r,\theta)$ (with $\theta$ measured from the stream direction) gives the velocity, $u_r = \partial_\theta\psi/(r^2\sin\theta)$, $u_\theta = -\partial_r\psi/(r\sin\theta)$, and conserves volume automatically. Taking the curl of the momentum equation removes the pressure and leaves $E^2(E^2\psi) = 0$, where $E^2 = \partial_r^2 + \frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\partial_\theta\big(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta\big)$.

Try $\psi = \sin^2\theta\,f(r)$: then $E^2\psi = \sin^2\theta\,(f'' - 2f/r^2)$, and the equation becomes an ordinary one whose solutions are powers, $f = Ar^4 + Br^2 + Cr + D/r$. Far away the flow must be uniform, $\psi \to \tfrac12 U r^2\sin^2\theta$, so $A = 0$ and $B = U/2$. On the surface the air must not slip, $u_r = u_\theta = 0$ at $r = a$, so $f(a) = f'(a) = 0$. Two conditions fix $C$ and $D$:

$$\psi = \tfrac12 U\sin^2\theta\Big(r^2 - \tfrac32 a r + \frac{a^3}{2r}\Big). \tag{2.1}$$

The momentum equation then gives the pressure, $p - p_\infty = -\tfrac32\mu U a\cos\theta/r^2$, higher on the upstream face; on the surface the normal viscous stress vanishes and the shear stress is $\tau_{r\theta} = -\tfrac32\mu U\sin\theta/a$. Adding up the stream-wise components over the sphere:

$$F = \underbrace{2\pi\mu a U}_{\text{pressure}} + \underbrace{4\pi\mu a U}_{\text{shear}} = 6\pi\mu a U. \tag{2.2}$$
Check
Dimensions alone give $F \propto \mu a U$; the factor $6\pi$ needs the solution. SymPy confirms $E^4\psi = 0$, the no-slip conditions, the pressure, the shear and the two parts of (2.2) (Checks tab).

A falling drop reaches its terminal speed when the drag balances its weight less its buoyancy: $6\pi\mu a v = \tfrac43\pi a^3(\rho_w - \rho_a)g$, so

$$v_S(a) = \frac{2(\rho_w - \rho_a)g}{9\mu}\,a^2 = k a^2. \tag{2.3}$$

Stokes' law undermines itself

We dropped the air's inertia. Its size relative to the viscous force is the Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re} = 2a\rho_a v/\mu$ (by custom with the diameter). Put Stokes' own speed into it: $\mathrm{Re} = 4\rho_a(\rho_w - \rho_a)g\,a^3/(9\mu^2)$, growing as $a^3$.

In our cloud
Stokes' speed has Re = 1 at a = 41.0 µm. Our collectors start at 40 µm. At 100 µm Stokes gives 1.23 m/s and Re = 15: far outside the law's own assumption.

A drag balance for every size

For a rigid sphere the drag can depend only on $a$, $v$, $\rho_a$ and $\mu$: with the force that is five quantities in three units, so by Buckingham's theorem two dimensionless groups, the Reynolds number and the drag coefficient $C_D = F/(\tfrac12\rho_a v^2\pi a^2)$. The drag law is a curve $C_D(\mathrm{Re})$. The balance of weight and drag then reads

$$\tfrac43\pi a^3(\rho_w - \rho_a)g = \tfrac12\rho_a v^2\pi a^2\,C_D(\mathrm{Re}). \tag{2.4}$$

The unknown $v$ sits on both sides, once inside Re. Multiply by $\mathrm{Re}^2$ and it cancels: the Davies number

$$N_{Da} \equiv C_D\,\mathrm{Re}^2 = \frac{4\rho_a(\rho_w - \rho_a)g}{3\mu^2}\,d^3 \tag{2.5}$$

contains only the drop and the air. So the recipe is: compute $N_{Da}$, find the Re that belongs to it, and $v = \mu\,\mathrm{Re}/(\rho_a d)$. With Stokes' drag, $C_D = 24/\mathrm{Re}$, it gives $N_{Da} = 24\,\mathrm{Re}$ and returns (2.3) exactly.

The curve $\mathrm{Re}(N_{Da})$ for $0.01 \lesssim \mathrm{Re} \lesssim 300$ cannot be derived in closed form. These notes take it from Beard (1976), who fitted a polynomial in $\ln N_{Da}$ to the drag of rigid spheres: for Re up to 20, the numerical solutions of the full Navier–Stokes equations by LeClair, Hamielec and Pruppacher (1970), which those authors found in close agreement with measured drag on spheres and on water drops; above that, wind-tunnel measurements on water drops. Below a diameter of 19 µm Beard uses Stokes' law with a small correction for the slip of air molecules ($+1.2$ % on the speed of an 8 µm droplet); above 1.07 mm a separate fit lets the drop flatten. The curve is a fit and must not be extrapolated outside its range.

Check against measurement
Against Gunn and Kinzer's 34 measured speeds (in their 50 % humid air), the drag balance is within 0.49 % above 1.2 mm (where Beard fitted it to these very data), and 0.2 to 1.9 % below the data between 0.3 and 1.0 mm, always on the same side; the largest gap from 0.3 mm up is 2.07 % in dry air (the old study quoted 2.1 %). At 0.1 and 0.2 mm the gaps are −7.5 and −3.5 %, where the sources disagree among themselves about the small drops.
In our cloud
The drag balance gives 0.177 m/s at 40 µm, 0.348 m/s at 60 µm and 0.728 m/s at 100 µm. Stokes is too fast by 11 %, 27 % and 69 %. Near 100 µm the drag grows as $v^{1.29}$: 29 % of the way from the honey world ($F \propto v$) to the water world ($F \propto v^2$).

What the better speed changes

For one collector the change is modest. In both settings of chapter 1 the time is $t = \int w(R)\,dR/[v(R) - v(r)]$ with the same positive weight $w$ for both speed laws, so the ratio of the two times is a weighted mean of the ratio of the relative speeds: it must lie between that ratio's smallest and largest value over the radii crossed.

In our cloud
One collector, 40 → 100 µm: 9.96 min with Stokes, 13.02 min with the drag balance, a factor 1.31 (bounds 1.12 and 1.70). Closed box: the times to put half and nine tenths of the water into the collectors grow by factors 1.14 and 1.20.

For the whole cloud the change is much larger, and that was a surprise. In chapter 5 the full collection equation, with every drop allowed to meet every other, takes 4.51 min to put 90 % of the water into drops above 40 µm with Stokes speeds and 7.96 min with the drag balance: a factor 1.76, outside the single-collector bounds. The reason: in a population, collectors also meet each other and droplets meet droplets, and those rates depend on the difference of fall speeds between drops of similar size. Stokes' $a^2$ law exaggerates that difference far more than it exaggerates the speed of one collector.
