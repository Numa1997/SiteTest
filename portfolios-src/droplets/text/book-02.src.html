2 How fast a drop falls

Every rate in these notes is a difference of fall speeds, so the fall speed has to be right. Chapter 1 used Stokes' law. Here we derive it, see where it fails, and replace it by a drag balance that holds at every size in the study.

Draw a teaspoon slowly through honey. The honey closes in behind it at once, and doubling the speed doubles the push you feel. Swish the same spoon through water: the water piles up in front and swirls away behind, and doubling the speed roughly quadruples the push. In honey the fluid's viscosity sets the push; in water the momentum of the fluid thrown aside sets it. A cloud droplet lives in the honey world. A growing collector leaves it.

Stokes' drag, derived

Hold a sphere of radius $a$ at rest and let air stream past it at speed $U$. We want the force the air exerts on it. The derivation has seven steps, and each one does a single thing.

Step 1: the equations. Air in slow motion obeys two laws. Its volume is kept: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0$. And each parcel is pushed by pressure differences and by the rubbing of its neighbours: $\rho_a(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf u$, with $\mu$ the viscosity of air. The left side is the parcel's inertia. Around a cloud droplet it is tiny compared with the rubbing (we check how tiny in the next section), so we drop it. What is left is called creeping flow:

$$\nabla p = \mu\nabla^2\mathbf u,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0.$$

Step 2: one function instead of two. The flow looks the same from every side of the stream's axis, so it has two velocity components: $u_r$ away from the sphere's centre, and $u_\theta$ round it, where $r$ is the distance from the centre and $\theta$ the angle from the downstream direction. Keeping the volume ties the two together, and one function $\psi(r, \theta)$, the stream function, gives both:

$$u_r = \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta},\qquad u_\theta = -\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}.$$

Any $\psi$ put into these keeps the volume automatically. It also has a picture: the air flows along the lines of constant $\psi$, and $2\pi\psi$ is the volume of air passing each second through the circle on which $\psi$ has that value. Far from the sphere the stream is uniform, $u_r = U\cos\theta$ and $u_\theta = -U\sin\theta$, and the function that gives exactly these is $\psi = \tfrac12Ur^2\sin^2\theta$ (differentiate it to check).

Step 3 ★: remove the pressure. The curl of a gradient is zero, so taking the curl of the momentum equation removes $p$ and leaves an equation for $\psi$ alone:

$$\mathcal E^2\big(\mathcal E^2\psi\big) = 0,\qquad \mathcal E^2 = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2} + \frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Big(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\Big).$$

A reader who skips the curl loses nothing: what matters is that there is one equation for one function.

Step 4: guess the shape, solve for the rest. Far away $\psi$ depends on the angle as $\sin^2\theta$, and the condition on the sphere, no motion, is the same at every angle. So try the same angular shape everywhere, $\psi = \sin^2\theta\,f(r)$. A trial is justified by its success: this one will satisfy the equation and every boundary condition, and creeping flow past a body has only one solution that does. The angular part of $\mathcal E^2$ acts on $\sin^2\theta$ as $\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta(2\cos\theta) = -2\sin^2\theta$, so

$$\mathcal E^2\psi = \sin^2\theta\,\Big(f'' - \frac{2f}{r^2}\Big),$$

and applying the operator twice turns the equation into an ordinary one, $\big(\tfrac{d^2}{dr^2} - \tfrac{2}{r^2}\big)^2 f = 0$. Its solutions are powers. Put $f = r^n$: one application gives $[n(n - 1) - 2]\,r^{n-2}$, a second gives $[n(n - 1) - 2]\,[(n - 2)(n - 3) - 2]\,r^{n-4}$. The first bracket vanishes for $n = 2$ and $n = -1$, the second for $n = 4$ and $n = 1$. So

$$f(r) = Ar^4 + Br^2 + Cr + \frac{D}{r}.$$

Step 5: the four constants from the two boundaries. Far away $\psi$ must become $\tfrac12Ur^2\sin^2\theta$: so $A = 0$ and $B = U/2$. On the surface the air must not move: $u_r = 0$ needs $f(a) = 0$, and $u_\theta = 0$ needs $f'(a) = 0$:

$$\tfrac12Ua^2 + Ca + \frac{D}{a} = 0,\qquad Ua + C - \frac{D}{a^2} = 0.$$

Divide the first by $a$ and add it to the second: $\tfrac32Ua + 2C = 0$, so $C = -\tfrac34Ua$, and then $D = \tfrac14Ua^3$. The flow is found:

$$\psi = \tfrac12 U\sin^2\theta\Big(r^2 - \tfrac32 a r + \frac{a^3}{2r}\Big),\qquad u_r = U\cos\theta\Big(1 - \frac{3a}{2r} + \frac{a^3}{2r^3}\Big),\quad u_\theta = -U\sin\theta\Big(1 - \frac{3a}{4r} - \frac{a^3}{4r^3}\Big). \tag{2.1}$$

Look at how slowly the sphere's influence dies away, as $a/r$: ten radii from the centre, on the axis, the air is still 15 % slower than far away. Chapter 3 will need this long reach.

Step 6: the two stresses on the surface. The air acts on the sphere in two ways, by pressing on it and by rubbing along it. The rubbing is the viscosity times the rate at which the air's sideways speed grows as one steps off the surface, $\tau_{r\theta} = \mu\,\partial u_\theta/\partial r$ at $r = a$ (the other terms of the shear stress vanish there, because the air on the surface is at rest). From (2.1), $\partial u_\theta/\partial r = -U\sin\theta\,(3a/4r^2 + 3a^3/4r^4)$, which at $r = a$ is $-\tfrac32U\sin\theta/a$. For the pressure, take the divergence of the momentum equation: $\nabla^2p = \mu\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u) = 0$. The solution with the angular shape $\cos\theta$ that vanishes far away is $\cos\theta/r^2$, and putting it into the radial momentum equation fixes its size. Together:

$$p - p_\infty = -\frac32\,\frac{\mu Ua\cos\theta}{r^2},\qquad \tau_{r\theta}\Big|_{r=a} = -\frac32\,\frac{\mu U\sin\theta}{a}.$$

The pressure is higher on the upstream face ($\cos\theta < 0$) and lower on the downstream face. The viscous push along the normal, $2\mu\,\partial u_r/\partial r$, is zero on the surface.

Step 7: add up the force along the stream. A ring of the surface between $\theta$ and $\theta + d\theta$ has area $2\pi a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta$. The pressure pushes along the inward normal, whose stream-wise part is $-\cos\theta$; the shear pulls along the surface, whose stream-wise part is $-\sin\theta$. So

$$F_{\rm pressure} = \int_0^\pi \tfrac32\frac{\mu U}{a}\cos^2\theta\;2\pi a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta = 3\pi\mu aU\cdot\tfrac23,\qquad F_{\rm shear} = \int_0^\pi \tfrac32\frac{\mu U}{a}\sin^2\theta\;2\pi a^2\sin\theta\,d\theta = 3\pi\mu aU\cdot\tfrac43,$$

using $\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac23$ and $\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac43$. One third of the drag is pressure on the front, two thirds is the air rubbing along the sides:

$$F = \underbrace{2\pi\mu a U}_{\text{pressure}} + \underbrace{4\pi\mu a U}_{\text{shear}} = 6\pi\mu a U. \tag{2.2}$$
Check
Dimensions alone give $F \propto \mu a U$; the factor $6\pi$ needs the solution. SymPy confirms $\mathcal E^4\psi = 0$, the no-slip conditions, the pressure, the shear and the two parts of (2.2) (Checks tab).

A falling drop reaches its terminal speed when the drag balances its weight less its buoyancy: $6\pi\mu a v = \tfrac43\pi a^3(\rho_w - \rho_a)g$, so

$$v_S(a) = \frac{2(\rho_w - \rho_a)g}{9\mu}\,a^2 = k a^2. \tag{2.3}$$

Stokes' law undermines itself

We dropped the air's inertia. Its size relative to the viscous force is the Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re} = 2a\rho_a v/\mu$ (by custom with the diameter). Put Stokes' own speed into it: $\mathrm{Re} = 4\rho_a(\rho_w - \rho_a)g\,a^3/(9\mu^2)$, growing as $a^3$.

In our cloud
Stokes' speed has Re = 1 at a = 41.0 µm. Our collectors start at 40 µm. At 100 µm Stokes gives 1.23 m/s and Re = 15: far outside the law's own assumption.

A drag balance for every size

For a rigid sphere the drag can depend only on $a$, $v$, $\rho_a$ and $\mu$: with the force that is five quantities in three units, so by Buckingham's theorem two dimensionless groups, the Reynolds number and the drag coefficient $C_D = F/(\tfrac12\rho_a v^2\pi a^2)$. The drag law is a curve $C_D(\mathrm{Re})$. The balance of weight and drag then reads

$$\tfrac43\pi a^3(\rho_w - \rho_a)g = \tfrac12\rho_a v^2\pi a^2\,C_D(\mathrm{Re}). \tag{2.4}$$

The unknown $v$ sits on both sides, once inside Re. Multiply by $\mathrm{Re}^2$ and it cancels: the Davies number

$$N_{Da} \equiv C_D\,\mathrm{Re}^2 = \frac{4\rho_a(\rho_w - \rho_a)g}{3\mu^2}\,d^3 \tag{2.5}$$

contains only the drop and the air. So the recipe is: compute $N_{Da}$, find the Re that belongs to it, and $v = \mu\,\mathrm{Re}/(\rho_a d)$. With Stokes' drag, $C_D = 24/\mathrm{Re}$, it gives $N_{Da} = 24\,\mathrm{Re}$ and returns (2.3) exactly.

The curve $\mathrm{Re}(N_{Da})$ for $0.01 \lesssim \mathrm{Re} \lesssim 300$ cannot be derived in closed form. These notes take it from Beard (1976), who fitted a polynomial in $\ln N_{Da}$ to the drag of rigid spheres: for Re up to 20, the numerical solutions of the full Navier–Stokes equations by LeClair, Hamielec and Pruppacher (1970), which those authors found in close agreement with measured drag on spheres and on water drops; above that, the wind-tunnel measurements of water drops by Beard and Pruppacher (1969). Below a diameter of 19 µm Beard uses Stokes' law with a small correction for the slip of air molecules ($+1.2$ % on the speed of an 8 µm droplet); above 1.07 mm a separate fit lets the drop flatten. The curve is a fit and must not be extrapolated outside its range.

Method · the fall speed of a drop, as the model computes it
  1. Take the diameter $d = 2a$ and the slip factor $C_{sc} = 1 + 2.51\,\lambda/d$, with $\lambda$ the mean free path of the air's molecules (appendix A).
  2. If $d$ is below 19 µm: $v = C_{sc}\,k a^2$, Stokes' law (2.3) with slip. Done.
  3. Otherwise compute the Davies number $N_{Da}$ from (2.5) and its logarithm $X = \ln N_{Da}$.
  4. Evaluate Beard's polynomial $Y = \sum_{n=0}^{6} b_nX^n$ (coefficients in appendix A). The Reynolds number is $\mathrm{Re} = C_{sc}\,e^{Y}$.
  5. The speed is $v = \mu\,\mathrm{Re}/(\rho_a d)$.
Above $d$ = 1.07 mm a third fit replaces steps 3 and 4. No equation is solved by iteration: the Davies number removed the unknown speed from one side of (2.4).
Example 2.1 · the fall speed of one drop, step by step
The drop has a radius of 40 µm.
  1. $d$ = 80 µm. The mean free path at 10 °C and 850 hPa is $\lambda = 7.56\times10^{-8}$ m, so $C_{sc}$ = 1.0024.
  2. 80 µm is above 19 µm, so the drag curve is used.
  3. $N_{Da} = 4\rho_a(\rho_w - \rho_a)g\,d^3/(3\mu^2)$ = 22.30, and $X = \ln 22.30$ = 3.105.
  4. The polynomial gives $Y$ = −0.1808, so $\mathrm{Re} = 1.0024\times e^{-0.1808}$ = 0.837.
  5. $v = \mu\,\mathrm{Re}/(\rho_a d)$ = 0.1771 m/s.
Stokes' law would say $\mathrm{Re} = N_{Da}/24$ = 0.929 and $v$ = 0.1967 m/s: 11 % too fast. The same five steps for a 100 µm drop give $N_{Da}$ = 348.5, $\mathrm{Re}$ = 8.59 and $v$ = 0.7277 m/s, where Stokes' $N_{Da}/24$ would be 14.5. An 8 µm droplet stops at step 2: Stokes' $7.87\times10^{-3}$ m/s times its slip factor 1.0119 is $7.963\times10^{-3}$ m/s, 8 mm each second.
Check against measurement
Against Gunn and Kinzer's 34 measured speeds (in their 50 % humid air), the drag balance is within 0.49 % above 1.2 mm (where Beard fitted it to these very data), and 0.2 to 1.9 % below the data between 0.3 and 1.0 mm, always on the same side; the largest gap from 0.3 mm up is 2.07 % in dry air (the old study quoted 2.1 %). At 0.1 and 0.2 mm the gaps are −7.5 and −3.5 %.
In our cloud
The drag balance gives 0.177 m/s at 40 µm, 0.348 m/s at 60 µm and 0.728 m/s at 100 µm. Stokes is too fast by 11 %, 27 % and 69 %. Near 100 µm the drag grows as $v^{1.29}$: 29 % of the way from the honey world ($F \propto v$) to the water world ($F \propto v^2$).

What the better speed changes

For one collector the change is modest. In both settings of chapter 1 the time is $t = \int w(R)\,dR/[v(R) - v(r)]$ with the same positive weight $w$ for both speed laws, so the ratio of the two times is a weighted mean of the ratio of the relative speeds: it must lie between that ratio's smallest and largest value over the radii crossed.

In our cloud
One collector, 40 → 100 µm: 9.96 min with Stokes, 13.02 min with the drag balance, a factor 1.31 (bounds 1.12 and 1.70). Closed box: the times to put half and nine tenths of the water into the collectors grow by factors 1.14 and 1.20.

For the whole cloud the change is much larger, and that was a surprise. In chapter 5 the full collection equation, with every drop allowed to meet every other, takes 4.51 min to put 90 % of the water into drops above 40 µm with Stokes speeds and 7.96 min with the drag balance: a factor 1.76, outside the single-collector bounds. The reason: in a population, collectors also meet each other and droplets meet droplets, and those rates depend on the difference of fall speeds between drops of similar size. Stokes' $a^2$ law exaggerates that difference far more than it exaggerates the speed of one collector.
