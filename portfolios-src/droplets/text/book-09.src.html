9 What the model says, and what it does not

The three questions of chapter 0, and the two of Part IV, answered for the thesis cloud in a closed box at 10 °C and 850 hPa:

  1. Most of the water sits in drops above 40 µm after 5.8 min (half) and 13.5 min (nine tenths), with $t_{90}$ anywhere from 10.5 to 13.5 min depending on the collision efficiency (11.4 min with Hall's table). The kernel decides the speed, and within it the collision efficiency first; the bookkeeping changes $t_{90}$ by 2.3 to 5.1 min.
  2. The first drizzle drop (100 µm) appears in a cubic metre after about a minute, in a litre after about three, and in 10 cm³ after about ten, in every exact run through mergers of collectors with each other. That is about ten times earlier than the time at which half the water is drizzle. Small volumes grow more slowly than the mean field.
  3. Ice: collisions of small ice spheres make 50 to 1600 times less new ice than the old study's rule assumed; shattering of freezing drops could make a few particles per cm³ per minute, or none, because the measured yields scatter that much.
  4. Energy and entropy (chapters 10 and 11): every merger lowers the surface free energy and raises the entropy by about $10^{10}\,k_B$, so the direction is fixed; the free energy drives water by itself only through the vapour, a true gradient descent in which every drop above the mean radius grows and every drop below it shrinks, so that droplets eat droplets, and which takes hours to days; which drops collide, and when, is set by gravity, and the cloud's collectors eat droplets where an energy-only lottery would merge droplets with droplets.
  5. Place (chapter 12): in a cloud with a shape, a mean motion and a stochastic element, water that starts in the updraft core is three times as likely to reach the ground as water that starts near the edge, and at fixed size a longer outline means less rain; the size of the largest eddies, poorly known, moves the rain by a factor of four.
What these notes leave out
  • No condensation: a rising cloud condenses 0.06 to 0.25 g/m³ of new water per minute at 0.5 to 2 m/s updraft (example 9.1), two to seven times what collection moves in the first minute. The closed box isolates collection; it is not a forecast.
  • In chapters 1 to 9 no fall out of the box, no updraft, no cloud depth: $F_{40}$ is a growth measure, not rain at the ground. Chapter 12 adds all three in a simple form, still without condensation.
  • The collision efficiency of nearly equal drops, which makes the first drizzle, is the least known ingredient; turbulence would raise it.
  • The initial spectrum's far tail decides the first drizzle in large volumes; we cut it at three standard deviations and say so.
Example 9.1 · how much water rising air condenses, step by step
Saturated air at 10 °C and 850 hPa rises. As it rises it expands and cools, cold air holds less vapour, and the excess condenses.
  1. The vapour the air can hold. At 10 °C the saturation vapour pressure is $e_s$ = 1227.2 Pa (Bolton's formula, the one in the growth constant $G$). As a mass per kilogram of air, $q_s = \varepsilon e_s/(p - e_s)$ = 9.111 g/kg, with $\varepsilon = R_d/R_v$ = 0.622.
  2. Energy of a rising parcel. A kilogram of air that rises by $dz$ does work against gravity, $g\,dz$, takes it from its heat, $c_p\,dT$, and gets back the latent heat $L$ of whatever condenses, $-L\,dq_s$. With no heat from outside the three add to zero: $c_p\,dT + g\,dz + L\,dq_s = 0$.
  3. How fast it cools. Dry air would cool at $g/c_p$ = 9.76 K per km ($c_p$ = 1005 J/(kg K)). Saturated air cools more slowly, because the condensing water warms it: differentiating $q_s$ along the way (Clausius–Clapeyron) and putting it into step 2 gives the moist lapse rate, $\Gamma_m = g\,\big(1 + Lq_s/(R_dT)\big)/\big(c_p + \varepsilon L^2q_s/(R_dT^2)\big)$ = 4.98 K per km.
  4. How much condenses. Step 2 solved for $dq_s/dz$: $(c_p\Gamma_m - g)/L$ = −1.939 g/kg per km. Times the air's density, 1.0458 kg/m³: 2.028 g of liquid water appear in each m³ of air for every km of rise.
  5. Per minute. At an updraft of 0.5 m/s the air rises 30 m in a minute: 0.061 g/m³. At 2 m/s, 0.243 g/m³.
  6. Against collection. In the first minute of chapter 5's run, collection moves 0.036 g/m³ of water into drops of 20 µm and more. Condensation in moderate updrafts adds two to seven times that much new water every minute, and spreads it over all drops.
