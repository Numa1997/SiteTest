9 What the model says, and what it does not

The three questions of chapter 0, answered for the thesis cloud in a closed box at 10 °C and 850 hPa:

  1. Most of the water sits in drops above 40 µm after 5.8 min (half) and 13.5 min (nine tenths), with an uncertainty from the collision efficiency of about two minutes (11.4 min with Hall's table). The kernel decides the speed, and within it the collision efficiency first; the bookkeeping changes $t_{90}$ by 2.5 to 5.2 min.
  2. The first drizzle drop (100 µm) appears in a cubic metre after about a minute, in a litre after about three, and in 10 cm³ after about ten, always through mergers of collectors with each other. That is about ten times earlier than the time at which half the water is drizzle. Small volumes grow more slowly than the mean field.
  3. Ice: collisions of small ice spheres make almost no new ice; shattering of freezing drops could make a few particles per cm³ per minute, but its yield for this geometry is unmeasured.
What these notes leave out
  • No condensation: a rising cloud condenses 0.06 to 0.25 g/m³ of new water per minute at 0.5 to 2 m/s updraft, as much as collection moves at the start. The closed box isolates collection; it is not a forecast.
  • No fall out of the box, no updraft, no cloud depth: $F_{40}$ is a growth measure, not rain at the ground.
  • The collision efficiency of nearly equal drops, which makes the first drizzle, is the least known ingredient; turbulence would raise it.
  • The initial spectrum's far tail decides the first drizzle in large volumes; we cut it at three standard deviations and say so.

Each of these is a door to a further study, not a flaw hidden in this one.
