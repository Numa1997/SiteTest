3 Which droplets touch

Chapter 1 let every droplet in the swept tube stick. It does not.

Drive through heavy rain and every drop ahead hits the windscreen. Drive through fine mist and much of it never touches the glass: the air pushed aside by the car carries it round. A falling drop pushes the air below it aside in the same way, and only droplets heavy enough to keep going while the air turns reach its surface.

The droplet's equation of motion

Sit on the collector: the air far away rises past it at $U = v(R)$, and near it the air has some velocity field $\mathbf u(\mathbf x)$. The small droplet, the partner, feels the drag on its velocity relative to the local air, and its own weight:

$$\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt} = \mathbf v,\qquad \tau\,\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = \mathbf u(\mathbf x) - \mathbf v - \tau g^*\mathbf e_z,\qquad g^* = g\Big(1 - \frac{\rho_a}{\rho_w}\Big). \tag{3.1}$$

$\tau$ is the partner's response time: in Stokes drag $\tau = 2\rho_w r^2/(9\mu)$, and we use $\tau = v(r)/g^*$ so that the partner falls at the speed of chapter 2 when the air is still. Measure lengths in $R$, speeds in $U$ and time in $R/U$ (the time the air takes to pass the collector). Then

$$\frac{d\mathbf y}{ds} = \mathbf w,\qquad \mathrm{St}\,\frac{d\mathbf w}{ds} = \mathbf a(\mathbf y) - \mathbf w - S\,\mathbf e_z,\qquad \mathrm{St} = \frac{\tau U}{R},\quad S = \frac{v(r)}{U}. \tag{3.2}$$

One number decides most of the story. The Stokes number St is the partner's stopping distance $\tau U$ in collector radii: how far it would coast if the air stopped turning it. A partner with St ≪ 1 follows the air round; one with St ≫ 1 goes straight and hits. $S$ is its own settling speed in units of $U$. The partner touches when its centre comes within $1 + p$ of the collector's, $p = r/R$.

An 8 µm droplet meeting a 40 µm collector: St = 3.60, S = 0.045.

Two model flows

The air around a sphere is known exactly only in two limits. Creeping flow (Re → 0) is (2.1): the air sticks to the surface and its disturbance reaches far, decaying as $1/r$. Potential flow (Re → ∞, no boundary layer) is a uniform stream plus a dipole, $\psi = \tfrac12 U\sin^2\theta\,(r^2 - a^3/r)$: the air slips along the surface and its disturbance decays as $1/r^3$, so droplets are turned only close by and more of them hit. Our collectors have Re between 0.8 and 9, in between. Langmuir (1948) joined the two efficiencies with an empirical weight, $E = (E_S + E_P\,\mathrm{Re}/60)/(1 + \mathrm{Re}/60)$; we use it and call it what it is, an interpolation.

The critical Stokes number

Take a point partner without weight ($p = 0$, $S = 0$) straight below the collector, on the axis. Let $\eta$ be its distance from the surface in collector radii. In potential flow the air on the axis approaches the surface at $1 - (1 + \eta)^{-3} \approx 3\eta$, so near the surface (3.2) becomes

$$\mathrm{St}\,\eta'' + \eta' + 3\eta = 0,\qquad \eta \propto e^{\kappa s},\quad \kappa = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 12\,\mathrm{St}}}{2\,\mathrm{St}}. \tag{3.3}$$

A damped oscillator. If $12\,\mathrm{St} < 1$ it is overdamped and creeps toward the surface for ever; if $12\,\mathrm{St} > 1$ it overshoots zero, which means the partner reaches the surface. So in potential flow

$$\boxed{\mathrm{St}_c = \tfrac{1}{12}.} \tag{3.4}$$

In creeping flow the air's approach vanishes as $\tfrac32\eta^2$, the linear argument fails, and the threshold must be computed along the whole axis. Our solver gives $\mathrm{St}_c$ = 1.21194, the value Michael and Norey (1970) give "correct to five decimal places" (Langmuir 1948 had 1.214). Below the critical number a point droplet never touches; a real droplet can still graze the collector with its own radius.

From the grazing path to the efficiency

Start partners far below at a sideways offset $b$ and integrate (3.2). Those starting inside some $b_c$ hit; those outside miss. Partners far upstream are spread evenly and move together, so the hit rate is $\pi b_c^2$ times their flux, and

$$E = \frac{b_c^2}{(1 + p)^2}. \tag{3.5}$$

The real capture area over the geometric one. (In creeping flow the disturbance reaches so far that we start at 200 radii and count the partners crossing the start disc with the stream function; doubling the distance changes E(40, 8) by less than $10^{-5}$.)

In our cloud
E(40 µm, 8 µm) = 0.344. Hall's table gives 0.50 and Langmuir's 1948 table 0.394 (for his air at 785 hPa and 2 °C, where our solver gives 0.359). The old study's stored table, made by a program that is lost, had 0.343.

Our efficiencies run 0.1 to 0.2 below Hall's for collectors of 40 to 100 µm, and near zero for collectors below about 20 µm, where Hall's table uses calculations that let the two drops' flows interact (Davis 1972, Jonas 1972). Ours lets only the collector move the air (the superposition method), which is good when the partner is much smaller than the collector and poor when they are nearly equal. Above $p = 0.6$ it fails, and we hold E at its value at $p = 0.6$. Chapters 5 and 6 show what these choices change.
