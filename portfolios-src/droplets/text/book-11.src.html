11 A cloud with a shape

Watch smoke leave a chimney on a windy day. The whole plume leans with the wind, which is the part you could predict. Its edge frays into puffs that no one could predict one by one but that behave alike on average: they spread the smoke, and they mix it with the clear air around it. A drop in a cloud moves the same way: it falls, it is carried by the cloud's large motion, and it is jostled by eddies too small to follow.

Until now the cloud was a box in which every drop could meet every other, and no drop had a place. A real cloud has a shape and an edge, and air outside the edge is dry. This chapter gives the drops positions, lets them move, and asks how much it matters where a drop sits.

11.1 The velocity of a drop: a mean part and a stochastic element

A drop moves with the air plus its own fall. Split the air's velocity into a mean part $\mathbf U(\mathbf x)$, the steady motion of the cloud as a whole, and the eddies smaller than the scale we resolve, $\mathbf u'$:

$$\frac{d\mathbf X}{dt} = \mathbf U(\mathbf X) + \mathbf u'(t) - v_T(r)\,\mathbf e_z. \tag{11.1}$$

The first and last terms are deterministic: $v_T$ is the fall speed of chapter 2, and for $\mathbf U$ we take one overturning cell that fills the cloud's extent, rising at $W_0$ = 1 m/s in the middle and sinking at the sides. With the cell's width $L_c$, height $H_c$ and lower left corner $(x_1, z_1)$, its stream function $\psi = (W_0L_c/2\pi)\,\sin(2\pi(x - x_1)/L_c)\,\sin(\pi(z - z_1)/H_c)$ gives $U_x = \partial\psi/\partial z$, $U_z = -\partial\psi/\partial x$, which has no divergence: air is neither made nor lost.

The middle term is the stochastic element. We cannot follow each eddy, but we know two things about them from Kolmogorov's theory of turbulence: how strong they are and how long they remember. Eddies smaller than our cell size $h$ have a velocity spread per component $\sigma^2 = (\varepsilon h)^{2/3}$, and a drop carried by them forgets its velocity after the Lagrangian time $T_L = 2\sigma^2/(C_0\varepsilon)$, with $C_0 \approx 6$ the measured Lagrangian constant. The simplest process with exactly that spread and that memory is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. Over a step $\Delta t$ each component is updated exactly by

$$u'(t + \Delta t) = u'(t)\,e^{-\Delta t/T_L} + \sigma\sqrt{1 - e^{-2\Delta t/T_L}}\;\xi + \tfrac12\,\frac{\partial\sigma^2}{\partial x_i}\Big(1 + \frac{u'^2}{\sigma^2}\Big)\Delta t, \tag{11.2}$$

with $\xi$ a fresh standard normal number. The last term matters only where $\varepsilon$ varies in space: without it, drops would pile up where the eddies are weak, like people drifting out of a crowded room into a quiet one. Thomson (1987) showed that this drift is what keeps evenly spread air evenly spread.

In our cloud
With $h$ = 50 m and $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$ m²/s³: $\sigma$ = 0.79 m/s, $T_L$ = 21 s, so the stochastic element spreads a puff of drops like a diffusion with coefficient $\sigma^2T_L$ = 13 m²/s, about 126 m in ten minutes. At $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$ the numbers are 0.37 m/s and 45 s, at $4\times10^{-2}$ they are 1.26 m/s and 13 s.

One more piece of physics enters at the edge. Inside the shape the air is saturated and drops neither grow nor shrink by vapour, as in the box. Outside it the air holds 80 % of the vapour it could, and a drop evaporates as

$$\frac{dr^2}{dt} = 2GS,\qquad S = -0.2, \tag{11.3}$$

where $G = 9.73\times10^{-11}$ m²/s collects heat conduction and vapour diffusion (Rogers and Yau 1989). A 10 µm droplet that leaves the cloud is gone in 2.6 s; a 100 µm drizzle drop lasts 257 s, less than the 412 s it needs to fall the 300 m from the cloud base to the ground. Only drizzle well above 100 µm reaches the ground.

11.2 Super-droplets

A slice of cloud 2 km wide and 1.5 km high holds about $10^{16}$ drops in its cloudy part; we cannot follow each one. Shima et al. (2009) let one computational super-droplet stand for $\xi$ real drops of the same size at the same place. The slice is cut into cells 50 m on a side (and 50 m deep); in each cell the super-droplets are shuffled into random pairs, and a pair of multiplicities $\xi_j \ge \xi_k$ merges with probability

$$p = \xi_j\,K(r_j, r_k)\,\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta V}\,\frac{n(n - 1)/2}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}, \tag{11.4}$$

where $n$ is the number of super-droplets in the cell of volume $\Delta V$ and the last factor makes up for testing only $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ of the $n(n-1)/2$ possible pairs. In a merger $\xi_k$ drops of the second kind each swallow one of the first, so the counts stay whole and the water is kept exactly. The kernel $K$ is that of chapters 1 to 4: the stochastic element moves drops between cells, but the speed at which two drops in a cell approach each other is still set by gravity.

Check: the box, and evenly spread air
Switch the motion off and put all super-droplets in one cell: the method must reproduce the collection equation. With 10 240 super-droplets the water reaches half and nine tenths above 40 µm at 5.807 ± 0.046 and 13.657 ± 0.080 min (4 runs), against 5.873 and 13.716 min from the collection equation with the same modes (cut at three standard deviations, 16 bins per doubling). With 32 super-droplets per cell the box is about 10 % slow, with 128 within 2 %; the runs below use 128. Second check: 7200 air parcels spread evenly, moved for 30 min by the cell and the stochastic element with $\varepsilon$ falling from $4\times10^{-2}$ in the core to $10^{-3}$ outside, stay even: the scatter of their counts per region is 0.79 times the scatter of pure chance.

11.3 What the shape does

We ran four shapes of similar size, a cumulus with a flat base and a cauliflower top, a disc, a box and a tall thin tower, each for 45 min and three random histories, and for the cumulus we switched pieces of the physics off one at a time.

The standard error of the rained share over the three runs is at most 0.4 percentage points. "Edge per area" is the length of the shape's outline per unit of its area in the slice.

The table reads as a competition between two exits. Water leaves either as rain through the ground or as vapour through the edge. The stochastic element is what carries drops across the edge: switching it off raises the rain from 32.6 % to 46.1 % without changing when the first drizzle lands. The more edge a cloud has for its size, the more water it loses that way: the tower, with 6.06 km of outline per km², rains 22.3 %, the cumulus with 3.90 rains 32.6 %, and the disc, with about the cumulus's outline per area but three quarters of its size, 29.5 %. The overturning cell helps the rain: in still air the drops stay in place while the eddies fray the edge, and only 23.4 % rains out. The cell carries drops up the middle, where they grow, and lets the large ones fall out through the base, where its updraft fades to zero.

11.4 Where a drop sits

In our cloud
Of 11 508 passages of 100 µm in the three reference runs, 90.2 % happened in the updraft core, which is 61.0 % of the cloud's area. The first super-droplet of drizzle appeared after about 2 min in every run.

Drizzle is born in the core, where the cell recirculates drops and the edge is far away; a droplet near the edge is more likely to be carried out by an eddy and evaporate than to be eaten. So the same droplet has a different fate depending on where it sits, and that is the precise sense of the phrase: the kernel is the same everywhere in this cloud, but the place decides how long a drop stays among partners.

What this chapter does not do
  • No condensation. The shape is a fixed saturated region with nothing to resupply it, so the shares above are of a fixed start, not the precipitation efficiency of a growing cloud.
  • Super-droplets lump drops. Each stands for about $4\times10^{11}$ droplets or $4\times10^{9}$ collectors, so "the first super-droplet of 100 µm" is a group of drops, not the luckiest single drop of chapter 6.
  • Two dimensions and one cell. The mean motion is a single idealised cell. It can be any field given on the grid; a field measured from pictures of real clouds, for example by stereo photography of cloud motion, would replace it, and that is the next study, not done here.
  • Constants. $\sigma^2 = (\varepsilon h)^{2/3}$ and $C_0 = 6$ are standard choices, each uncertain by tens of percent; the stochastic element moves drops between cells and does not change how fast two drops in one cell approach each other (chapter 7's question).
