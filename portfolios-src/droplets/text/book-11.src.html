11 Who eats whom

Picture a lottery that draws two names from a hat and merges those two drops. Every draw lowers the free energy, so a rule that only asks "does the free energy fall?" accepts every draw. What the cloud becomes then depends entirely on how the hat was filled: whether every pair had one ticket, or whether some pairs had thousands.

Chapter 10 showed that the free energy fixes where a closed cloud is heading but cannot choose which drops meet. This chapter makes that precise with three merging rules on the same landscape, then keeps the books of who actually eats whom in the cloud, and finally asks whether the cloud's size distribution is the one of greatest entropy.

11.1 Three rules on one landscape

The standard way to let energy decide in a simulation is the Metropolis Monte Carlo: propose a change and accept it with probability

$$P_{\rm accept} = \min\!\big(1,\;e^{-\Delta F/k_BT}\big). \tag{11.1}$$

For every merger $\Delta F$ is below $-10^{9}\,k_BT$ (problem 6 works out two 10 µm droplets: $-9.8\times10^{9}\,k_BT$), so every proposed merger is accepted. The rule therefore does nothing but carry out its proposals. If each pair of drops is proposed equally often, an uninformed lottery with one ticket per pair, the result is coalescence with a kernel that is the same for every pair, a constant kernel, with time counted in mergers. Two cautions. First, the uniform proposal is an extra assumption, not something energy supplies. Second, no split is ever proposed, so this is not Metropolis sampling of an equilibrium state; it is a one-way chain whose whole character comes from its proposals. Weight the tickets by the collision rate of chapters 1 to 4 instead, and the same acceptance rule reproduces the real cloud exactly: energy accepts whatever gravity proposes.

A second way to let energy decide is steepest descent: always take the merger that lowers the free energy most. The drop, $\sigma\cdot4\pi\,[r_1^2 + r_2^2 - (r_1^3 + r_2^3)^{2/3}]$, grows with both radii, so steepest descent always merges the two largest drops; the product is then the largest, and it eats every other drop in order of decreasing size.

Method · the three rules, as the model runs them

All three start from the same kind of volume: 100 cm³ holding 30 100 drops drawn from the two modes of the thesis cloud, cut at three standard deviations. After every merger the surface destroyed, $4\pi(r_1^2 + r_2^2 - r_{\rm new}^2)$, is subtracted from the running total, and the total is written down each time the number of drops passes one of 41 marks.

  1. The collision kernel. The exact simulation of chapter 6, unchanged: pairs are proposed and accepted in proportion to $K$.
  2. Every pair alike. The same simulation with the kernel replaced by one number, the same for every pair. Then every pair is equally likely to be the next, and every proposal is accepted, as (11.1) demands. The value of the number changes only the clock, never the order of the mergers.
  3. Largest pair first. Sort the drops by size. Merge the two largest. The product is now the largest; merge it with the next in the list, and so on to the end of the list. Nothing is random after the drops are drawn.

Rules 2 and 3 have no clock, and that is not an oversight. A free-energy difference is measured in joules, a rate in events per second, and nothing in the energy turns one into the other. Something must supply a mobility. For the vapour it was the diffusion of water through air, $M_i$ in (10.7); for collisions it is the kernel, in m³/s, and the kernel comes from mechanics. That is why figure 11.1 can only count mergers along its horizontal axis.

The uninformed lottery spends almost all its mergers joining droplets to droplets, because droplets are 300 times more numerous than collectors; it makes a 100 µm drop only when nearly every drop has merged. Steepest descent makes one after five mergers, by joining the largest collectors, and then lets one drop eat the whole volume. The cloud does neither: its collectors eat droplets. All three end in one drop of 0.30 mm holding the water of the 100 cm³. The landscape fixes that end; the path, and with it the clock, belongs to the kernel, to geometry, fall speeds and collision efficiencies.

11.2 The ledger of the cloud

To follow the competition we sort the drops into three populations: droplets ($r$ < 20 µm, the gap between the two starting modes), collectors (20 to 100 µm) and drizzle ($r$ ≥ 100 µm). Inside the collection equation's step we keep a ledger of every merger: which population ate which, and how much water was eaten. We also tag the water that started in the droplet mode, and the tag rides with the water through every merger. The ledger only watches: the run with it reproduces chapter 5 bit for bit.

Read the two columns against each other: rare as events, large in water. Collectors eating collectors is one merger in three hundred, yet by $t_{90}$ it has moved 16 % of all the water, because each such meal is a whole collector. These are the mergers that start the first drizzle in the exact runs of chapter 6; the bulk of drizzle's water is droplets, eaten one at a time. The droplets almost never eat each other: two droplets fall at nearly the same speed and their collision efficiency is tiny. So the population holding 93 % of the surface free energy, the one an energy rule would merge first, is the one gravity merges last. Compare the vapour's ledger of section 10.4, where the free energy does drive the water: there droplets eating droplets is the main channel, and the collectors gain little.

11.3 The distribution of greatest entropy

There is a second, different entropy: the entropy of our knowledge of the size distribution (Jaynes 1957). If all we know is the number of drops $N$ and their water $M$, the least biased distribution is the one that maximises $H = -\int n\ln n\,dm$ at fixed $N$ and $M$. The maximum is found in three steps.

Step 1: vary the distribution. Change $n(m)$ by a small amount $\delta n(m)$. The three quantities change by

$$\delta H = -\int(\ln n + 1)\,\delta n\,dm,\qquad \delta N = \int\delta n\,dm,\qquad \delta M = \int m\,\delta n\,dm.$$

Step 2: the condition for a maximum. At the maximum $H$ must not change for any $\delta n$ that leaves $N$ and $M$ alone. Lagrange's way of saying that is to ask $H - \alpha N - \beta M$ to be stationary for every $\delta n$ whatever, with two numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ to be found later:

$$\int\big(-\ln n - 1 - \alpha - \beta m\big)\,\delta n\,dm = 0\ \text{ for every }\delta n\quad\Rightarrow\quad n(m) = e^{-1-\alpha}\,e^{-\beta m} \equiv C\,e^{-\beta m}.$$

Step 3: the two numbers from the two things we know. $N = \int_0^\infty Ce^{-\beta m}\,dm = C/\beta$ and $M = \int_0^\infty m\,Ce^{-\beta m}\,dm = C/\beta^2$. Dividing, $\beta = N/M$, and then $C = N^2/M$:

$$n(m) = \frac{N^2}{M}\,e^{-Nm/M}. \tag{11.2}$$
In our cloud
At the start $N = 3.010\times10^{8}$ drops per m³ hold $M = 1.116\times10^{-3}$ kg/m³. The mean mass $M/N = 3.709\times10^{-12}$ kg is that of a 9.60 µm sphere, and (11.2) lets the number of drops fall by a factor $e$ for each further $3.709\times10^{-12}$ kg of mass. It has one hump; the cloud has two.

For the constant kernel of section 11.1 this same exponential solves the collection equation (5.1) exactly (problem 7). The number of drops then follows from the moment identity (5.2) with $\phi = 1$: the bracket is $-1$ and $K$ comes out of the integral, so $dN/dt = -\tfrac12KN^2$. Divide by $-N^2$: $d(1/N)/dt = K/2$, a straight line in time,

$$N(t) = \frac{N_0}{1 + KN_0t/2}, \tag{11.3}$$

and in the collection equation every start with finite water approaches it, in the sense that the distribution of $m/\bar m$ tends to the exponential's shape as $t \to \infty$ (Menon and Pego 2004, Theorem 5.1, for this kernel). So for the uninformed lottery the shape of greatest entropy and the long-time shape coincide. The exponential there comes from the kinetics of the constant kernel; neither energy nor entropy enters its dynamics. The real kernel is not constant, and the test is to compare.

In our cloud
With every pair alike, starting from the thesis cloud, the spectrum error from (11.2) falls to 0.17 when one drop in a hundred is left and to 0.018 at one in a thousand. With the collision kernel it is 1.32 at 6 min and 1.92 at $t_{90}$, and the gamma does no better (1.38 and 1.90). The collector hump eating the droplet hump is two populations, and no distribution built from the number and the water alone can draw two humps.

The choice of variable matters too. Maximising entropy with a prior uniform in radius instead of mass gives $n(m)\propto m^{-2/3}e^{-\beta m}$, another shape from the same principle. Maximum entropy, used this way, is a hypothesis about shape, and the kernel decides whether it holds.

Everything so far happened in a box with no place in it: every drop could meet every other. The next chapter gives the cloud a shape, a wind and a stochastic element, and asks what changes where a drop sits.
