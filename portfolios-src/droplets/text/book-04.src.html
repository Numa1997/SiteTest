4 Touching is not merging

Watch rain on a puddle. Most drops vanish into it at once; some bounce, or skate across on a film of air before they sink. Two drops in a cloud face two hazards. Before they touch, the thin film of air between them must drain away, or they bounce. After they touch, a hard hit can stretch the merged drop so far that it tears apart again. The law below describes only the second hazard.

Which energy can do the tearing? Two drops of masses $m_1$, $m_2$ with velocities $\mathbf v_1$, $\mathbf v_2$ have kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2^2$. Write each velocity as the velocity $\mathbf V$ of the common centre of mass plus a part of the relative velocity $\Delta\mathbf v = \mathbf v_1 - \mathbf v_2$: $\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf V + \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\Delta\mathbf v$, $\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf V - \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\Delta\mathbf v$. Square and add; the cross terms cancel, and

$$\tfrac12 m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2^2 = \tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)V^2 + \tfrac12\mu_{\rm red}\,\Delta v^2,\qquad \mu_{\rm red} = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1 + m_2}. \tag{4.1}$$

The centre of mass keeps moving whatever happens (momentum is conserved), so only the second part, the collision kinetic energy $\mathrm{CKE} = \tfrac12\mu_{\rm red}\Delta v^2$, can deform the drops. Compare it with the surface energy of the merged drop of diameter $d_c = (d_1^3 + d_2^3)^{1/3}$; their ratio is a Weber number,

$$\mathrm{We} = \frac{\mathrm{CKE}}{\pi\sigma d_c^2},\qquad E_{\rm coal} = e^{-1.15\,\mathrm{We}}. \tag{4.2}$$

The exponential law is the thesis's, and the form Straub et al. (2010) fitted to simulations of raindrop pairs; it is a fit, not a consequence of energy conservation, and it was made for drops of 0.35 mm and more.

In our cloud
For an 8 µm droplet hitting a 40 µm collector the collision energy is $3.0\times10^{-14}$ J and We = $2.0\times10^{-5}$: the surface is 50 000 times stiffer than the hit, and E_coal = 0.99998. For two collectors of 60 and 50 µm, We = $2.9\times10^{-4}$. Only for drizzle and rain does the law bite: a 500 µm drop meeting a 250 µm drop has We = 0.52 and E_coal = 0.55.

So for the growth that decides observable 1, coalescence is a stated check: setting E_coal = 1 changes $t_{90}$ by 0.25 %. It matters in two other places. For millimetre drops, where measured laws differ among themselves (Low and List 1982 give 0.34 where (4.2) gives 0.55 for 500/250 µm). And for the thesis kernel, which used Stokes speeds in (4.2): at 500/250 µm Stokes' relative speed is 23 m/s instead of 2.1 m/s, We = 61, and the merger is switched off. That is why the thesis kernel's large drops pile up below a millimetre.

Check against measurements
The law (4.2) gives 1 to within $4\times10^{-4}$ for every pair that decides observable 1, but it knows nothing of the air film. What experiments say: for cloud drops of 55–105 µm radius colliding with each other (size ratio 0.5–1), the coalescence efficiency is "certainly greater than 91 % and likely greater than 95 %" (Beard et al. 2002), so collector–collector mergers do merge. For small droplets hitting drizzle-sized collectors, bouncing has been seen: Beard and Ochs (1984) find that, averaged over a collector's growth from 50 to 500 µm by catching 30 µm droplets, only half of the collisions lead to a merger. So taking $E_{\rm coal} \approx 1$ is supported for the pairs that make the first drizzle, and is an assumption the law cannot test for drizzle drops sweeping up cloud droplets.
