4 Touching is not merging

Watch rain on a puddle. Most drops vanish into it at once; some bounce, or skate across on a film of air before they sink. Two drops in a cloud face the same choice: a gentle touch lets surface tension pull them into one sphere, a hard hit can tear them apart again.

Which energy can do the tearing? Two drops of masses $m_1$, $m_2$ with velocities $\mathbf v_1$, $\mathbf v_2$ have kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2^2$. Write each velocity as the velocity $\mathbf V$ of the common centre of mass plus a part of the relative velocity $\Delta\mathbf v = \mathbf v_1 - \mathbf v_2$: $\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf V + \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\Delta\mathbf v$, $\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf V - \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\Delta\mathbf v$. Square and add; the cross terms cancel, and

$$\tfrac12 m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2v_2^2 = \tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)V^2 + \tfrac12\mu_{\rm red}\,\Delta v^2,\qquad \mu_{\rm red} = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1 + m_2}. \tag{4.1}$$

The centre of mass keeps moving whatever happens (momentum is conserved), so only the second part, the collision kinetic energy $\mathrm{CKE} = \tfrac12\mu_{\rm red}\Delta v^2$, can deform the drops. Compare it with the surface energy of the merged drop of diameter $d_c = (d_1^3 + d_2^3)^{1/3}$; their ratio is a Weber number,

$$\mathrm{We} = \frac{\mathrm{CKE}}{\pi\sigma d_c^2},\qquad E_{\rm coal} = e^{-1.15\,\mathrm{We}}. \tag{4.2}$$

The exponential law is the thesis's, and the form Straub et al. (2010) fitted to simulations of raindrop pairs; it is a fit, not a consequence of energy conservation, and it was made for drops of 0.35 mm and more.

In our cloud
For an 8 µm droplet hitting a 40 µm collector the collision energy is $3.0\times10^{-14}$ J and We = $2.0\times10^{-5}$: the surface is 50 000 times stiffer than the hit, and E_coal = 0.99998. For two collectors of 60 and 50 µm, We = $2.9\times10^{-4}$. Only for drizzle and rain does the law bite: a 500 µm drop meeting a 250 µm drop has We = 0.52 and E_coal = 0.55.

So for the growth that decides observable 1, coalescence is a stated check: setting E_coal = 1 changes $t_{90}$ by 0.26 %. It matters in two other places. For millimetre drops, where measured laws differ among themselves (Low and List 1982 give 0.34 where (4.2) gives 0.55 for 500/250 µm). And for the thesis kernel, which used Stokes speeds in (4.2): at 500/250 µm Stokes' relative speed is 23 m/s instead of 2.1 m/s, We = 61, and the merger is switched off. That is why the thesis kernel's large drops pile up below a millimetre.

Check
No measurement of coalescence for cloud droplets this small was found in the sources read; every energy law gives 1 to within $4\times10^{-4}$ for these pairs, but none of them describes a bouncing air film. The notes say so rather than pretend a measurement.
