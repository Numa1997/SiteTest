Part IVEnergy, chance and place

10 Energy, entropy and who eats whom

Watch a window in the rain. Drops sit on the glass; two that touch merge at once into one and never split again. If the glass could choose, it would hold one big drop, because one drop has less surface than many. Yet nothing happens until drops touch. A drop that grows heavy slides down and eats every drop in its track. The order of the meals is set by gravity and by what lies in the path, not by the energy. A cloud is the same window without the glass.

Parts I to III never mentioned energy or entropy. They followed the drops' motion and counted their meetings. This chapter asks what the two great principles of physics say about the same cloud: that energy runs downhill, and that entropy grows. Both turn out to hold exactly. Both also turn out to say less than one might hope, and the gap between what they say and what the cloud does is the most instructive part of the study.

10.1 Every merger lowers the energy

Water's surface costs energy: making a square metre of it takes $\sigma$ = 0.07422 J at 10 °C, the surface tension. A drop of mass $m$ has radius $r = (3m/4\pi\rho_w)^{1/3}$, so its surface energy is a power of its mass,

$$\sigma\,4\pi r^2 = \sigma\,(36\pi)^{1/3}\rho_w^{-2/3}\,m^{2/3},\qquad E_s = \sigma\,(36\pi)^{1/3}\rho_w^{-2/3}\sum_{\text{drops}} m_i^{2/3}. \tag{10.1}$$

$E_s$ is the cloud's surface energy per m³ of air. Put $\phi(m) = m^{2/3}$ into the moment identity (5.2): each merger replaces $m^{2/3} + m'^{2/3}$ by $(m + m')^{2/3}$. Write $x = m/(m + m')$ for the share of the product's water brought by the first partner. Then

$$(m + m')^{2/3} - m^{2/3} - m'^{2/3} = (m + m')^{2/3}\,\big[1 - x^{2/3} - (1 - x)^{2/3}\big]. \tag{10.2}$$

For $0 < x < 1$ we have $x^{2/3} > x$ (a number below one grows when raised to a power below one), so $x^{2/3} + (1-x)^{2/3} > x + (1 - x) = 1$ and the bracket is negative. Every merger lowers the surface energy, whatever the two sizes. Inserted into (5.2),

$$\boxed{\;\frac{dE_s}{dt} = \frac{\sigma(36\pi)^{1/3}}{2\rho_w^{2/3}}\iint K\,n(m)\,n(m')\,\big[(m+m')^{2/3} - m^{2/3} - m'^{2/3}\big]\,dm\,dm' \;\le\; 0.\;} \tag{10.3}$$

A function that can only fall along every solution is called a Lyapunov function. $E_s$ is one, for the collection equation and also along every single history of the exact Monte Carlo of chapter 6, because the statement holds merger by merger, not just on average.

Check: the limits of one merger
The bracket of (10.2) is deepest at $x = \tfrac12$: two equal drops lose $1 - 2^{-1/3}$ = 20.6 % of their joint surface. A large drop of radius $R$ eating a small one of radius $r$ destroys the share $1 - \tfrac23(r/R) + \tfrac19(r/R)^4 - \dots$ of the small one's surface: almost all of it. So the surface, and with it the energy, sits mostly in the smallest drops, and it is destroyed most efficiently by feeding small drops to large ones.
In our cloud
At the start the cloud holds $E_s$ = 0.0214 J per m³ of air, 92.5 % of it in the droplets. A 40 µm collector eating an 8 µm droplet releases $5.17\times10^{-11}$ J, 1700 times the kinetic energy of their collision ($3.04\times10^{-14}$ J). In the collection equation of chapter 5 the surface energy fell at every one of the 3600 one-second steps of the hour; the two-bin scheme, by Jensen's inequality for the concave $m^{2/3}$, can only remove a little more surface than the exact products, and over the hour it removed 0.1 % more.

10.2 Where the energy goes, and the entropy it makes

The released energy first shakes the merged drop, which wobbles like a struck jelly; water's viscosity stills the wobble in a quarter of a millisecond at 40 µm (Lamb's damping time $r^2/5\nu_w$). The drop warms by $4.6\times10^{-5}$ K and passes the heat to the air. Heat $\Delta E$ delivered at temperature $T$ is entropy,

$$\Delta S = \frac{\Delta E_s}{T}, \tag{10.4}$$

$1.32\times10^{10}\,k_B$ for that one capture ($k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant). To split again, the drop would have to gather that entropy back out of the air. Statistical mechanics gives such a fluctuation the weight $e^{-\Delta S/k_B} = e^{-1.32\times10^{10}}$: it never happens. This is the exact sense in which the second law governs the cloud. It fixes the direction: fewer, larger drops, never the reverse.

In our cloud
Over the hour merging releases 0.0212 J per m³, nearly all the surface energy the cloud started with, and produces $7.48\times10^{-5}$ J/K of entropy per m³, $1.80\times10^{10}\,k_B$ per merger on average. At its largest the release is 1.49 % of the power gravity's drag dissipates. Gravity turns as much energy into heat as all the drops' surfaces hold every 28 s.

So the energy that merging releases is real, but small. The direction it sets is absolute. Its size is irrelevant to the cloud's energy budget, which is ruled by gravity, turbulence and, in a real cloud, the latent heat of condensation that these notes leave out (chapter 9).

10.3 A cloud that only minimises energy

Suppose energy decided everything. The standard way to let energy decide is the Metropolis Monte Carlo: propose a change, here merging a pair chosen at random, and accept it with probability

$$P_{\rm accept} = \min\!\big(1,\;e^{-\Delta E/k_BT}\big). \tag{10.5}$$

For every merger $\Delta E < 0$ by $10^{9}$ to $10^{12}\,k_BT$ (section 10.1), so every proposal is accepted, and every split would be refused. The rule then reduces to: pick two drops at random and merge them. That is coalescence with a kernel that is the same for every pair, a constant kernel, with time counted in mergers, since the Metropolis rule has no clock. Think of a lottery that draws two names from a hat and merges those two drops. The cloud's own lottery is weighted: the kernel of chapters 1 to 4 draws big, fast drops far more often than small ones, and two drops of the same size almost never.

A second way to let energy decide is steepest descent: always take the merger that releases the most energy. The energy released, $\sigma\cdot4\pi\,[r_1^2 + r_2^2 - (r_1^3 + r_2^3)^{2/3}]$, grows with both radii, so steepest descent always merges the two largest drops; the product is then the largest, and it eats every other drop in order of decreasing size.

All three end in the same place, one drop of 0.30 mm holding all the water of the 100 cm³. Energy fixes that end; it cannot choose among paths, because every path is downhill. The choice, and with it the clock, belongs to the kernel: to geometry, fall speeds and collision efficiencies.

10.4 Who eats whom

To follow the competition we sort the drops into three populations: droplets ($r$ < 20 µm, the gap between the two starting modes), collectors (20 to 100 µm) and drizzle ($r$ ≥ 100 µm). Inside the collection equation's step we keep a ledger of every merger: which population ate which, how much water was eaten, how much surface energy was released. We also tag the water that started in the droplet mode and let the tag ride with the water through every merger, so that at any time we know what each population is made of. The ledger only watches: the run with it reproduces chapter 5 bit for bit.

The ledger turns chapter 6's lesson into numbers. Collector–collector mergers are rare, a third of a percent of all mergers, yet they are how the first drizzle drops start in the exact runs; the bulk of drizzle's water is droplets, eaten one at a time. And the droplets almost never eat each other: two droplets fall at nearly the same speed and their efficiency is tiny, so the population that holds 92.5 % of the surface energy, the population energy would merge first, is the one gravity merges last.

10.5 Does the cloud maximise entropy?

There is a second, different entropy: the entropy of our knowledge of the size distribution (Jaynes 1957). If all we know is the number of drops $N$ and their water $M$, the least biased distribution is the one that maximises $H = -\int n\ln n\,dm$ at fixed $N$ and $M$. Setting the variation of $H - \alpha N - \beta M$ to zero gives $-\ln n - 1 - \alpha - \beta m = 0$, an exponential:

$$n(m) = \frac{N^2}{M}\,e^{-Nm/M}. \tag{10.6}$$

Is the cloud's distribution this one? For the constant kernel of section 10.3 the answer is yes, and exactly. Substituting (10.6) into (5.1) with $K$ constant shows that it is a solution when the number falls as

$$N(t) = \frac{N_0}{1 + KN_0t/2}, \tag{10.7}$$

and the exponential is the shape every start with finite water approaches (a theorem of Menon and Pego, 2004). So the two ideas, the Monte Carlo that minimises energy and the distribution that maximises entropy, are one model: the cloud with its kernel replaced by a constant. The real kernel is not constant, and the test is to compare.

In our cloud
With every pair alike, starting from the thesis cloud, the spectrum error from (10.6) falls to 0.170 when one drop in a hundred is left and to 0.018 at one in a thousand. With the kernel of chapters 1 to 4 it is 1.32 at 6 min and 1.92 at $t_{90}$; the gamma does no better (1.38 and 1.90). The collector hump eating the droplet hump is two populations, and no distribution built from the number and the water alone can draw two humps.

The choice of variable matters too. Maximising entropy with a prior uniform in radius instead of mass gives $n(m)\propto m^{-2/3}e^{-\beta m}$, another shape from the same principle. What singles out mass for the constant kernel is the kinetics. Maximum entropy, used this way, is a hypothesis about shape, and the kernel decides whether it holds.

10.6 What energy and entropy decide

The balance sheet
  • Exact: the surface energy (10.3) only falls, and each merger makes about $10^{10}\,k_B$ of entropy, so merging is irreversible. The end state of a closed cloud is one drop.
  • Exact, and small: the energy released is at most 1.5 % of what gravity's drag dissipates; it drives nothing.
  • Not decided by energy: the path and the clock. Three rules on one landscape give three different diets; the cloud's is fixed by the kernel.
  • Maximum entropy: the right shape for a cloud in which every pair is alike, and far from the shape of a cloud ruled by gravity.

Everything so far happened in a box with no place in it: every drop could meet every other. The next chapter gives the cloud a shape, a wind, and a stochastic element, and asks how much it matters where a drop sits.
