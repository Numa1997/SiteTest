Part IVEnergy, chance and place

10 Energy and entropy of merging

Watch a window in the rain. Drops sit on the glass; two that touch merge at once into one and never split again. If the glass could choose, it would hold one big drop, because one drop has less surface than many. Yet nothing happens until drops touch. A drop that grows heavy slides down and eats every drop in its track. The order of the meals is set by gravity and by what lies in the path, not by the energy. A cloud is the same window without the glass.

Parts I to III followed the drops' motion and counted their meetings. They never asked what the two great principles of physics say about the same cloud: that free energy runs downhill, and that entropy grows. This chapter asks. Both principles hold exactly. The interesting part is how much each one decides, and how fast it can act on its own.

10.1 Every merger lowers the surface free energy

Water's surface costs work to make: stretching it by a square metre at fixed temperature takes $\sigma$ = 0.07422 J at 10 °C. So $\sigma$ is the surface's free energy per square metre (the part of its energy available as work). A drop of mass $m$ has radius $r = (3m/4\pi\rho_w)^{1/3}$, and its surface free energy is a power of its mass,

$$\sigma\,4\pi r^2 = \sigma\,(36\pi)^{1/3}\rho_w^{-2/3}\,m^{2/3},\qquad F_s = \sigma\,(36\pi)^{1/3}\rho_w^{-2/3}\sum_{\text{drops}} m_i^{2/3}. \tag{10.1}$$

$F_s$ is the surface free energy of the drops in one m³ of air. A merger replaces $m^{2/3} + m'^{2/3}$ by $(m + m')^{2/3}$. Write $x = m/(m + m')$ for the share of the product's water brought by the first partner:

$$(m + m')^{2/3} - m^{2/3} - m'^{2/3} = (m + m')^{2/3}\,\big[1 - x^{2/3} - (1 - x)^{2/3}\big]. \tag{10.2}$$

For $0 < x < 1$ we have $x^{2/3} > x$ (a number below one grows when raised to a power below one), so $x^{2/3} + (1-x)^{2/3} > 1$ and the bracket is negative. Every merger lowers $F_s$, whatever the two sizes. Putting (10.2) into the moment identity (5.2) gives

$$\boxed{\;\frac{dF_s}{dt} = \frac{\sigma(36\pi)^{1/3}}{2\rho_w^{2/3}}\iint K\,n(m)\,n(m')\,\big[(m+m')^{2/3} - m^{2/3} - m'^{2/3}\big]\,dm\,dm' \;\le\; 0.\;} \tag{10.3}$$

A quantity that can only fall is called a Lyapunov function. $F_s$ is one, for the collection equation and also along every single history of the exact Monte Carlo of chapter 6, because the statement holds merger by merger.

Check: the limits of one merger
The bracket of (10.2) is deepest at $x = \tfrac12$: two equal drops lose $1 - 2^{-1/3}$ = 20.6 % of their joint surface. A large drop of radius $R$ eating a small one of radius $r$ destroys the share $1 - \tfrac23(r/R) + \tfrac19(r/R)^4 - \dots$ of the small one's surface, almost all of it. So the free energy sits mostly in the smallest drops, and it is destroyed most efficiently by feeding small drops to large ones.
In our cloud
At the start the drops in a cubic metre hold $F_s$ = 0.021 J, 93 % of it in the droplets. A 40 µm collector eating an 8 µm droplet lowers $F_s$ by $5.2\times10^{-11}$ J, 1700 times the kinetic energy of their collision. In the collection equation of chapter 5, $F_s$ fell at every one of the 3600 one-second steps of the hour.

10.2 Heat, entropy and the direction of time

The free energy released first shakes the merged drop, which wobbles like a struck jelly; water's viscosity stills the wobble in a quarter of a millisecond at 40 µm (Lamb's damping time $r^2/5\nu_w$). The heat that appears is more than the free energy lost, because a surface also carries entropy: a square metre of surface has entropy $-d\sigma/dT$, and destroying it releases that entropy too. Each square metre of surface destroyed gives the heat $u_s = \sigma - T\,d\sigma/dT$ = 0.115 J, 1.56 times $\sigma$. For the capture above that is $8.0\times10^{-11}$ J, which warms the merged drop by $7.1\times10^{-5}$ K before the air takes it away. The entropy of the world (drop, surface and air) rises by

$$\Delta S = -\frac{\Delta F_s}{T} = \frac{\sigma\,|\Delta A|}{T}, \tag{10.4}$$

$1.32\times10^{10}\,k_B$ for that one capture ($k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $|\Delta A|$ the surface destroyed). For the merged drop to split again by a thermal fluctuation, that entropy would have to come back out of the air, and statistical mechanics gives such a fluctuation the weight $e^{-\Delta S/k_B} = e^{-1.32\times10^{10}}$. No thermal fluctuation ever splits a cloud drop. Drops do break, but only when mechanical work does it: the collisions of millimetre raindrops, or a drop of several millimetres torn by the air it falls through. Those drops lie outside this closed box of small drops, so within it the second law fixes the direction: fewer, larger drops.

In our cloud
Over the hour merging releases 0.033 J of heat per m³ and raises the entropy by $7.5\times10^{-5}$ J/K per m³, about $1.8\times10^{10}\,k_B$ per merger. At its largest the heat of merging is 2.3 % of the heat gravity's drag makes. Gravity turns as much energy into heat as all the drops' surfaces hold every 28 s.

10.3 The free energy of the whole cloud

Free energy is $F = E - TS$, and the entropy $S$ includes the drops' own: many drops scattered through the air can be arranged in more ways than a few. How much is that worth? A drop of 8 µm is a particle of mass $2.1\times10^{-12}$ kg, and the entropy of $N$ such particles in a volume $V$ is the ideal-gas value, about $k_B[\ln(V/N\lambda^3) + 5/2]$ per particle, with $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$ the thermal wavelength. For our droplets $\lambda = 2.9\times10^{-18}$ m and this is 104 $k_B$ per drop. A merger destroys one drop's worth of it, 104 $k_B$, while releasing $10^{10}\,k_B$ of surface entropy. Minimising $F$ therefore means merging, by a margin of a hundred million to one: the free energy of the whole cloud is lowest when all its water sits in as few drops as possible.

So energy and entropy, written together as the free energy, say exactly where a closed cloud of small drops is heading. They do not yet say how it gets there, or how fast. Free energy can drive motion only if something lets water move down its gradient. For collisions, nothing does: the drops meet when gravity brings them together, whatever the free energy says. But there is a second channel, through the vapour, where the free energy itself drives the water. That channel is the honest version of "a gradient descent that minimises energy".

10.4 The gradient descent that nature runs: ripening through the vapour

Leave a glass of beer to stand. The foam coarsens: small bubbles vanish and big ones grow, without any bubble touching another, because gas passes through the liquid from the small bubbles, where the curved skin squeezes it harder, to the large ones. Ice cream left in a freezer grows coarse crystals the same way.

A small drop holds its water less tightly than a large one. Its curved surface pushes on the water inside, so the water's tendency to escape, its chemical potential, is raised by the curvature (Kelvin's effect). Dissolved salt lowers it (Raoult's effect). Per kilogram of water, relative to flat pure water,

$$\mu = R_vT\Big(\frac{a}{r} - \frac{b}{r^3}\Big),\qquad a = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho_wR_vT} = 1.14\ \text{nm}, \tag{10.5}$$

with $R_v$ the gas constant of water vapour and $b$ proportional to the salt in the drop. Water diffuses through the vapour from drops of high $\mu$ to drops of low $\mu$. With the growth constant $G$ and ventilation factor $f_v$ of chapter 12, (12.4), each drop's mass changes as

$$\frac{dm_i}{dt} = -M_i\,(\mu_i - \bar\mu),\qquad M_i = \frac{4\pi\rho_wr_iGf_v}{R_vT}, \tag{10.6}$$

where $\bar\mu$ is set by the vapour so that the total water stays fixed, and $M_i$ is a mobility. Since $\mu_i = \partial F/\partial m_i$, this is precisely a gradient descent of the free energy at fixed water, and it lowers $F$ at the rate

$$\frac{dF}{dt} = \sum_i \mu_i\,\frac{dm_i}{dt} = -\sum_i M_i\,(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2 \;\le\; 0. \tag{10.7}$$

We ran it on 10 cm³ of the thesis cloud (3010 drops, three random draws), sealed and saturated, once with pure water and once with every drop holding the salt of a 0.05 µm grain of ammonium sulfate.

In our cloud
By the time collection has put nine tenths of the water above 40 µm (13.5 min), it has destroyed 82 % of the surface free energy; ripening has destroyed 2.5 %. Ripening needs an hour to remove 13 % and a day to remove 61 %. In pure water a third of the drops vanish within the hour; with salt none do: each shrinks only to a haze particle of 0.27 µm, where the salt holds it, and the water moved is the same.

So the competition one might picture, large drops starving small ones by energy alone, is real. Large drops do grow at the expense of small ones, driven by nothing but the free energy, exactly as a gradient descent. It is simply slow: it works on a time of hours to days, while gravity's collisions finish the same job in minutes. In a real cloud ripening is buried under the much larger vapour changes that rising and sinking air bring, which is why the collection equation, which leaves it out, is the right first model of rain.

10.5 What energy and entropy decide

The balance sheet
  • Exact: the surface free energy (10.3) only falls, and each merger raises the entropy by about $10^{10}\,k_B$, so no thermal fluctuation undoes it. The free energy of the whole cloud is lowest with all the water in a few drops.
  • Exact, and small: the heat of merging is at most 2.3 % of the heat gravity's drag makes; it drives nothing.
  • A real gradient descent: ripening through the vapour (10.6) lowers the free energy by itself, but over hours to days.
  • Not decided by free energy: which drops collide, and when. That is the subject of the next chapter.
