Part IVEnergy, chance and place

10 Energy and entropy of merging

Watch a window in the rain. Drops sit on the glass; two that touch merge at once into one and never split again. If the glass could choose, it would hold one big drop, because one drop has less surface than many. Yet nothing happens until drops touch. A drop that grows heavy slides down and eats every drop in its track. The order of the meals is set by gravity and by what lies in the path, not by the energy. A cloud is the same window without the glass.

Parts I to III followed the drops' motion and counted their meetings. They never asked what the two great principles of physics say about the same cloud: that free energy runs downhill, and that entropy grows. This chapter asks. Both principles hold exactly. The interesting part is how much each one decides, and how fast it can act on its own.

10.1 Every merger lowers the surface free energy

Water's surface costs work to make: stretching it by a square metre at fixed temperature takes $\sigma$ = 0.07422 J at 10 °C. So $\sigma$ is the surface's free energy per square metre (the part of its energy available as work). The argument that every merger lowers it has three steps.

Step 1: the surface of a drop as a function of its water. A drop of mass $m$ has radius $r = (3m/4\pi\rho_w)^{1/3}$, so its surface $4\pi r^2 = 4\pi\,(3m/4\pi\rho_w)^{2/3}$. Collect the numbers, $4\pi\,(3/4\pi)^{2/3} = (36\pi)^{1/3}$, and the surface free energy is a power of the mass,

$$\sigma\,4\pi r^2 = \sigma\,(36\pi)^{1/3}\rho_w^{-2/3}\,m^{2/3},\qquad F_s = \sigma\,(36\pi)^{1/3}\rho_w^{-2/3}\sum_{\text{drops}} m_i^{2/3}. \tag{10.1}$$

$F_s$ is the surface free energy of the drops in one m³ of air.

Step 2: one merger. A merger replaces $m^{2/3} + m'^{2/3}$ by $(m + m')^{2/3}$. Write $x = m/(m + m')$ for the share of the product's water brought by the first partner, and take $(m + m')^{2/3}$ out as a common factor:

$$(m + m')^{2/3} - m^{2/3} - m'^{2/3} = (m + m')^{2/3}\,\big[1 - x^{2/3} - (1 - x)^{2/3}\big]. \tag{10.2}$$

For $0 < x < 1$ we have $x^{2/3} > x$ (a number below one grows when raised to a power below one), so $x^{2/3} + (1-x)^{2/3} > 1$ and the bracket is negative. Every merger lowers $F_s$, whatever the two sizes.

Step 3: the whole cloud. The moment identity (5.2) gives the rate of change of $\int\phi\,n\,dm$ for any $\phi$. Take $\phi(m) = \sigma(36\pi)^{1/3}\rho_w^{-2/3}m^{2/3}$, the surface free energy of one drop: then $\int\phi\,n\,dm = F_s$, and the bracket of (5.2), $\phi(m + m') - \phi(m) - \phi(m')$, is the change in one merger, step 2. So

$$\boxed{\;\frac{dF_s}{dt} = \frac{\sigma(36\pi)^{1/3}}{2\rho_w^{2/3}}\iint K\,n(m)\,n(m')\,\big[(m+m')^{2/3} - m^{2/3} - m'^{2/3}\big]\,dm\,dm' \;\le\; 0.\;} \tag{10.3}$$

A quantity that can only fall is called a Lyapunov function. $F_s$ is one, for the collection equation and also along every single history of the exact Monte Carlo of chapter 6, because the statement holds merger by merger.

Check: the limits of one merger
The bracket of (10.2) is deepest at $x = \tfrac12$: two equal drops lose $1 - 2^{-1/3}$ = 20.6 % of their joint surface. A large drop of radius $R$ eating a small one of radius $r$, with $p = r/R$: the product has radius $R(1 + p^3)^{1/3}$, and its surface exceeds the large drop's by

$$4\pi R^2\big[(1 + p^3)^{2/3} - 1\big] = 4\pi R^2\big[\tfrac23p^3 - \tfrac19p^6 + \dots\big].$$

Subtract that from the small drop's surface $4\pi R^2p^2$ and divide by it: the merger destroys the share $1 - \tfrac23p + \tfrac19p^4 - \dots$ of the small one's surface, almost all of it. So the free energy sits mostly in the smallest drops, and it is destroyed most efficiently by feeding small drops to large ones.

10.2 Heat, entropy and the direction of time

The free energy released first shakes the merged drop, which wobbles like a struck jelly; water's viscosity stills the wobble in a quarter of a millisecond at 40 µm (Lamb's damping time $r^2/5\nu_w$, with $\nu_w = 1.307\times10^{-6}$ m²/s the kinematic viscosity of water at 10 °C). The heat that appears is more than the free energy lost, because a surface also carries entropy: a square metre of surface has entropy $-d\sigma/dT$, and destroying it releases that entropy too. Each square metre of surface destroyed gives the heat $u_s = \sigma - T\,d\sigma/dT$ = 0.115 J, 1.56 times $\sigma$. For the capture above that is $8.0\times10^{-11}$ J, which warms the merged drop by $7.1\times10^{-5}$ K before the air takes it away. Now add up the entropy of the world. The air receives the heat $u_s|\Delta A|$ at temperature $T$, so its entropy rises by $u_s|\Delta A|/T$. The surface that disappeared takes its own entropy, $-(d\sigma/dT)\,|\Delta A|$, with it. The water of the drops is unchanged. The sum is $\big[u_s + T\,d\sigma/dT\big]|\Delta A|/T$, and the bracket is $\sigma$:

$$\Delta S = -\frac{\Delta F_s}{T} = \frac{\sigma\,|\Delta A|}{T}, \tag{10.4}$$

$1.32\times10^{10}\,k_B$ for that one capture ($k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $|\Delta A|$ the surface destroyed). For the merged drop to split again by a thermal fluctuation, that entropy would have to come back out of the air, and statistical mechanics gives such a fluctuation the weight $e^{-\Delta S/k_B} = e^{-1.32\times10^{10}}$. No thermal fluctuation ever splits a cloud drop. Drops do break, but only when mechanical work does it: the collisions of millimetre raindrops, or a drop of several millimetres torn by the air it falls through. Those drops lie outside this closed box of small drops, so within it the second law fixes the direction: fewer, larger drops.

10.3 The free energy of the whole cloud

Free energy is $F = E - TS$, and the entropy $S$ includes the drops' own: many drops scattered through the air can be arranged in more ways than a few. How much is that worth? A drop of 8 µm is a particle of mass $2.1\times10^{-12}$ kg, and the entropy of $N$ such particles in a volume $V$ is the ideal-gas value, about $k_B[\ln(V/N\lambda^3) + 5/2]$ per particle, with $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$ the thermal wavelength. For our droplets $\lambda = 2.9\times10^{-18}$ m and this is 104 $k_B$ per drop.

Where the formula comes from, in three steps. Count places: one particle in a volume $v$ can sit in about $v/\lambda^3$ distinguishable places, because quantum mechanics blurs a particle with the momenta of temperature $T$ over a length $\lambda$. Share the volume: $N$ identical particles each range over all of $V$, which gives $(V/\lambda^3)^N$ arrangements; but swapping two identical particles gives nothing new, so divide by the $N!$ swaps. With Stirling's formula $N! \approx (N/e)^N$ the count is about $(eV/N\lambda^3)^N$: the $e$ is what is left of the $1/N!$. Take the logarithm: this count is the partition function $Z$ of the drops' motion, and the entropy is $S = k_B\ln Z + E/T$. The first term gives $k_B[\ln(V/N\lambda^3) + 1]$ per particle; the second, with the kinetic energy $E = \tfrac32Nk_BT$, adds $\tfrac32k_B$, which makes the $\tfrac52$ (Sackur and Tetrode).

  5. The entropy. $\ln(1.381\times10^{44})$ = 101.64, plus 5/2: 104.14 $k_B$ per drop.
  6. Against the surface. The capture of example 10.3 released $1.324\times10^{10}\,k_B$, $1.27\times10^{8}$ times more than the one drop's worth of placement entropy it destroyed.

A merger destroys one drop's worth of it, 104 $k_B$, while releasing $10^{10}\,k_B$ of surface entropy. Minimising $F$ therefore means merging, by a margin of a hundred million to one: the free energy of the whole cloud is lowest when all its water sits in as few drops as possible.

So energy and entropy, written together as the free energy, say exactly where a closed cloud of small drops is heading. They do not yet say how it gets there, or how fast. Free energy can drive motion only if something lets water move down its gradient. For collisions, nothing does: the drops meet when gravity brings them together, whatever the free energy says. But there is a second channel, through the vapour, where the free energy itself drives the water. That channel is the honest version of "a gradient descent that minimises energy".

10.4 The gradient descent that nature runs: ripening through the vapour

Leave a glass of beer to stand. The foam coarsens: small bubbles vanish and big ones grow, without any bubble touching another, because gas passes through the liquid from the small bubbles, where the curved skin squeezes it harder, to the large ones. Ice cream left in a freezer grows coarse crystals the same way.

This section does what the chapter's title promises and the sections above could not: it takes the free energy, turns it into a force on the water of every drop, lets the water move down that force, and keeps the books of who gains and who loses at each step. Five steps lead there: the force, the path the water can take, the rule that keeps the water fixed, the proof that the free energy falls, and the reading of one step.

Step 1: the force. How tightly a drop holds its water. Move a little water $dm$ into a drop of radius $r$. The drop swells: its mass is $m = \tfrac43\pi\rho_wr^3$, so $dm = 4\pi\rho_wr^2\,dr$, and its surface $A = 4\pi r^2$ grows by $dA = 8\pi r\,dr$. Dividing, $dA/dm = 2/(\rho_wr)$: a kilogram of water costs a small drop more new surface than a large one. The surface free energy of that drop changes by $\sigma\,dA$, and the price per kilogram is the drop's chemical potential,

$$\mu_i \equiv \frac{\partial F_s}{\partial m_i} = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho_wr_i} = R_vT\,\frac{a}{r_i},\qquad a = \frac{2\sigma}{\rho_wR_vT} = 1.14\ \text{nm}. \tag{10.5}$$

$R_v$ = 461.5 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ is the gas constant of water vapour; it enters only to give the length $a$, which is the natural unit of the problem. There is a mechanical way to see (10.5). The curved skin squeezes the water inside by the pressure $2\sigma/r$ (Laplace), and pushing a kilogram of water, a volume $1/\rho_w$, in against that pressure takes the work $2\sigma/(\rho_wr)$. Flat water, $r \to \infty$, has $\mu = 0$: it is the zero of the scale. (In this section $\mu$ always carries a drop's index or a bar and is a chemical potential; the air's viscosity, which had the same letter in chapters 2 and 3, does not appear here.)

In our cloud
An 8 µm droplet holds its water at $\mu$ = 18.6 J/kg, a 40 µm collector at 3.7 J/kg. In everyday terms: the water in 8 µm droplets sits as high above a lake, in energy, as if it had been lifted 1.89 m, and the water in 40 µm drops as if lifted 0.38 m. Water runs downhill, from the small drop to the large one, if a path lets it.

Dissolved salt changes the price. A drop that holds salt keeps its water more tightly (Raoult's effect), and (10.5) gains a second term, $\mu = R_vT\,(a/r - b/r^3)$, with $b$ proportional to the mass of salt in the drop. We come back to it at the end of the section.

Step 2: the path. Water passes through the vapour. Drops do not touch here, so the only path between them is the air: water evaporates from one drop, crosses as vapour and condenses on another. The vapour has a chemical potential too. If the air holds a share $s$ more vapour than air in balance with flat water ($s$ is the supersaturation; $s$ = 0.01 means 1 % more), then the vapour's potential on the same scale is $R_vT\ln(1 + s)$, and for the $s$ of order $10^{-4}$ met here that is $\bar\mu = R_vT\,s$ to better than a part in ten thousand. A drop is in balance with the air when the two potentials are equal, $s = a/r$: the smaller the drop, the more vapour the air must hold to keep it alive. That is Kelvin's law.

When they are not equal, vapour diffuses toward the drop or away from it. Around a sphere a steady diffusion has the vapour density approaching its far value as $1/\text{distance}$, like the potential of a charge, and the total flow through any shell round the drop is $4\pi rD_v$ times the density difference between the far air and the drop's surface ($D_v$ is the diffusivity of vapour in air): the flow grows with the radius, not with the surface. Condensing vapour also releases latent heat, which warms the drop and raises its own vapour pressure until the heat has leaked into the air; that slows the flow. Rogers and Yau (1989, chapter 7) put both resistances, diffusion and heat conduction, into one growth constant $G$, and a falling drop gains a little from the fresh air it meets, the ventilation factor $f_v$ (it is 1.00 for droplets and 1.07 at 40 µm):

$$\frac{dm_i}{dt} = 4\pi\rho_w\,r_i\,G\,f_v\Big(s - \frac{a}{r_i}\Big). \tag{10.6}$$
In our cloud
$G = 9.73\times10^{-11}$ m²/s. Heat conduction is 62 % of the resistance and diffusion 38 %: with diffusion alone $G$ would be $2.53\times10^{-10}$ m²/s, 2.6 times larger. (Chapter 12 uses the same $G$ for drops that leave the cloud, (12.4), where $s = -0.2$.)

Write (10.6) with the potentials of step 1 and it takes the form that every law of slow, damped transport has, a flow proportional to a difference of potential:

$$\frac{dm_i}{dt} = -M_i\,(\mu_i - \bar\mu),\qquad M_i = \frac{4\pi\rho_w\,r_i\,G\,f_v}{R_vT}. \tag{10.7}$$

$M_i$ is the drop's mobility: how many kilograms per second flow for each J/kg of difference. Compare Ohm's law, current equal to conductance times voltage difference, or heat flowing between two bodies in proportion to their temperature difference.

Step 3: the rule. The water is fixed, and that sets the vapour. The box is sealed, and the vapour cannot act as a store: the air holds 9.4 g/m³ of vapour, but $s$ moves only in its fourth decimal place, so the vapour's content changes by about a thousandth of a g/m³ while the drops hold 1.1 g/m³. So what one drop loses another must gain: $\sum_i dm_i/dt = 0$. Put (10.7) in and solve for the one unknown, $\bar\mu$:

$$\bar\mu = \frac{\sum_i M_i\,\mu_i}{\sum_i M_i},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad r_c \equiv \frac{a}{s} = \frac{\sum_i r_i f_{v,i}}{\sum_i f_{v,i}}. \tag{10.8}$$

The vapour settles at the mobility-weighted mean of the drops' potentials. Nobody sets $s$ from outside; the drops set it themselves. The second form says the same thing as a radius. $r_c$ is the critical radius, the size of a drop that is exactly in balance with the air, and with $f_v \approx 1$ it is simply the mean radius of all the drops. This is the whole rule of who eats whom through the vapour:

Key result · who eats whom through the vapour
At every instant, every drop larger than the mean radius $r_c$ grows and every drop smaller than it shrinks. The line between eaters and eaten is not fixed: as small drops vanish the mean radius rises, and a drop that was growing yesterday finds itself below the line today and starts to give its water back.

Step 4: the proof. The free energy falls, as a sum of squares. The free energy changes because the masses change:

$$\frac{dF_s}{dt} = \sum_i \frac{\partial F_s}{\partial m_i}\,\frac{dm_i}{dt} = \sum_i \mu_i\,\frac{dm_i}{dt}.$$

Since the gains and losses add to zero, we may subtract $\bar\mu\sum_i dm_i/dt = 0$ without changing anything, and then (10.7) gives

$$\boxed{\;\frac{dF_s}{dt} = \sum_i (\mu_i - \bar\mu)\,\frac{dm_i}{dt} = -\sum_i M_i\,(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2 \;\le\; 0.\;} \tag{10.9}$$

Every drop contributes a square, so every term of the sum is a loss. Measure the potentials from the vapour's level $\bar\mu$: a drop that shrinks gives up water it held above that level, a drop that grows takes water in below it, and both lower the total. The descent stops only when all $\mu_i$ are equal, which cannot happen while pure-water drops of two different sizes are left: it ends with one size and, given time, one drop. And because the box is at one temperature, (10.4) turns this into the entropy made each second, $dS/dt = -\dot F_s/T = \sum_i M_i(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2/T$: a flow times the force that drives it, summed over the drops. This is the second law doing work: it does not only forbid the way back, it gives the rate of the way forward.

★ Why this deserves the name gradient descent. A ball in honey does not accelerate down a slope; it moves at the speed at which the friction balances the slope. Rayleigh wrote that balance as a minimum principle, and Onsager showed it holds for slow transport in general: among all the flows $\dot m_i$ that keep the water fixed, nature takes the ones that minimise

$$\mathcal R = \underbrace{\sum_i \frac{\dot m_i^{\,2}}{2M_i}}_{\text{half the dissipation}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_i \mu_i\,\dot m_i}_{dF_s/dt} \;-\; \bar\mu\sum_i \dot m_i. \tag{10.10}$$

The last term is the constraint, with $\bar\mu$ as its Lagrange multiplier. Setting $\partial\mathcal R/\partial\dot m_i = 0$ gives $\dot m_i/M_i + \mu_i - \bar\mu = 0$, which is (10.7): the vapour's potential is the multiplier that keeps the water fixed. So ripening is the steepest descent of $F_s$ at fixed water, with steepness measured by the cost $\dot m^2/2M$ of pushing water through the vapour. This is the overdamped cousin of a Lagrangian: no inertia, only a force and a friction. Collisions have no such function. Their rate $K$ contains fall speeds and collision efficiencies and does not ask how much free energy a merger would release; that is the exact sense in which they are not a gradient descent.

Step 5: one step, read off. Divide (10.6) by $4\pi\rho_wr^2$ to get the rate of the radius, and use $s = a/r_c$:

$$\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{G f_v\,a}{r}\Big(\frac{1}{r_c} - \frac{1}{r}\Big). \tag{10.11}$$

Three things can be read from (10.11) without a computer. Below $r_c$ the rate is negative and grows without limit as $r \to 0$: a shrinking drop shrinks faster and faster, and vanishes in a finite time. Above $r_c$ the rate is positive, has its largest value $Ga/(4r_c^2)$ at $r = 2r_c$ (differentiate and set to zero, with $f_v = 1$), and falls off as $1/r$ beyond. And the clock: a drop whose radius differs from $r_c$ by about $r_c$ moves at about $Ga/r_c^2$, so it needs about

$$\tau \approx \frac{r_c^3}{G\,a} \tag{10.12}$$

to change its radius by $r_c$. The cube is the important part: each doubling of the mean radius takes eight times longer than the last, so ripening always slows down.

Check: the descent against its own formula
The run stores the surface free energy itself, and separately adds up the right side of (10.9), $\sum_i M_i(\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2$, over its time steps. From 1 minute to 1 day the stored fall of $F_s$ is 0.3 to 1.1 % larger than the summed formula; the gap is the time step (the sum takes each step's rate at its beginning) and the last surface of drops that vanish within a step. The water is kept to $5\times10^{-12}$ of itself. The two ways of putting the cloud into the computer agree: after one hour the classes have 86.9 % of the free energy left, the three draws of 3010 drops 86.8 %. The theory of this process (Lifshitz and Slyozov 1961, Wagner 1961) predicts that after a long time $r_c^3$ grows steadily at the rate $\tfrac49Ga$; between 12 and 24 hours ours grows 1.8 times faster, so with its two humps this cloud has not yet reached that final state.
In our cloud
By the time collection has put nine tenths of the water above 40 µm (13.5 min), it has destroyed 82 % of the surface free energy; ripening has destroyed 2.5 %. Ripening needs an hour to remove 13 % and a day to remove 61 %. In pure water a third of the drops vanish within the hour; with salt none do: each shrinks only to a haze particle of 0.28 µm, where the salt holds it, and the water moved is the same. At the start ripening lowers the free energy at $4.6\times10^{-7}$ W per m³ of cloud; collection, averaged up to 13.5 min, at $2.2\times10^{-5}$ W per m³, 47 times faster.

So the competition one might picture, large drops starving small ones by energy alone, is real, and it is a gradient descent in the exact sense of (10.10). Its books can be kept step by step, and they say that the mean radius is the dividing line. It is simply slow: its clock is $r_c^3/(Ga)$, an hour and a half at the start and eight times longer with each doubling of the drops, while gravity's collisions finish the same job in minutes. In a real cloud ripening is buried under the much larger vapour changes that rising and sinking air bring (in rising cloud air $s$ is typically a few tenths of a percent, Rogers and Yau 1989, some twenty times the 0.014 % the drops set here), which is why the collection equation, which leaves it out, is the right first model of rain.

10.5 What energy and entropy decide

The balance sheet
  • Exact: the surface free energy (10.3) only falls, and each merger raises the entropy by about $10^{10}\,k_B$, so no thermal fluctuation undoes it. The free energy of the whole cloud is lowest with all the water in a few drops.
  • Exact, and small: the heat of merging is at most 2.3 % of the heat gravity's drag makes; it drives nothing.
  • A real gradient descent: ripening through the vapour (10.7) lowers the free energy by itself, as a sum of squares (10.9). Every drop above the mean radius grows and every drop below it shrinks, so through the vapour droplets eat droplets; the clock is $r_c^3/(Ga)$, hours to days.
  • Not decided by free energy: which drops collide, and when. That is the subject of the next chapter.
