Worked problems

Problems

Each problem uses the model's own numbers (10 °C, 850 hPa unless stated). Try it, then open the solution.

1The runaway drop. With Stokes' law and E = 1, when does a 40 µm collector in 0.643 g/m³ of small droplets reach infinite radius, and why does no real drop do that?
At 21.1 minutes, and only on paper

From (1.3), $t^* = 1/(cR_0)$ with $c = kW/(4\rho_w)$. With $k = 1.2296\times10^8$ m⁻¹ s⁻¹, $W = 6.43\times10^{-4}$ kg/m³ and $R_0 = 40$ µm, $c = 19.8$ m⁻¹ s⁻¹ and $t^*$ = 21.1 min.

The blow-up comes from $dR/dt \propto R^2$, and that $R^2$ comes from Stokes' speed. Above about 40 µm the drag balance replaces it (chapter 2) and the speed grows more slowly than $R^2$, as $R^{1.33}$ at 100 µm and roughly $R^1$ near 0.3 mm, so the growth slows to about exponential; the droplets also run out, and millimetre drops break up. The lesson is not the infinity but its cause: growth that feeds on its own size.

2The smallest droplet that can hit. A 40 µm collector falls at 0.177 m/s. Ignoring the droplet's own size and weight, what is the smallest droplet radius that can reach its surface in potential flow, and in creeping flow?
1.2 µm in potential flow, 4.7 µm in creeping flow

A point droplet hits when $\mathrm{St} = \tau U/R$ exceeds the critical value: 1/12 in potential flow (3.4), 1.21194 in creeping flow. So $\tau \ge \mathrm{St}_c R/U$; in potential flow $\tau \ge 1.88\times10^{-5}$ s. With Stokes' $\tau = 2\rho_w r^2/(9\mu)$, $r = \sqrt{9\mu\tau/(2\rho_w)}$ = 1.22 µm. In creeping flow the same steps give 4.67 µm.

The real air around a 40 µm collector (Re = 0.84) is much closer to creeping flow, which is why 8 µm droplets, only twice the creeping-flow limit, hit with an efficiency of just 0.344. Their own radius helps them: they touch when their centre is 8 µm from the surface.

3Why the thesis kernel stalls. A 500 µm drop meets a 250 µm drop. Compute the Weber number (4.2) with Stokes speeds and with drag-balance speeds.
We = 61 against 0.52: Stokes switches the merger off

The relative speed is 23.1 m/s with Stokes' law and 2.12 m/s with the drag balance. The collision energy grows as the square of the relative speed, so We grows by $(23.1/2.12)^2 \approx 120$: from 0.52 to 61. Then $E_{\rm coal} = e^{-1.15\,\mathrm{We}}$ is 0.55 against practically zero.

A wrong fall speed enters twice: once in the rate at which drops meet, once squared in whether they merge. With Stokes speeds the large drops of the thesis kernel stop merging with each other below a millimetre.

4Golovin's clock. For the sum kernel $K = b(m + m')$ the number of drops obeys $dN/dt = -bLN$. With $b$ = 1.5 m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹ and $L$ = 1 g/m³, what fraction of the drops is left after one hour?
0.45 %: the test spectrum is pushed far

From (5.2) with $\phi = 1$, the bracket is $-1$ and the double integral of $b(m + m')n\,n'$ is $2bLN$, so $dN/dt = -bLN$ and $N = N_0 e^{-bLt}$. Here $bLt$ = 5.40 and $N/N_0$ = 0.00452. The test is hard on the scheme: the mass peak moves from about 10 µm to about half a millimetre, and the scheme follows it with a spectrum error of 0.051 at 8 bins per doubling.

5A litre of cloud. Using (6.1), what concentration of drops above 100 µm must the mean field reach for half of all 1000 cm³ volumes to hold one?
693 per m³, reached after about three minutes

The median is where $V n_{100} = \ln 2$, so $n_{100}$ = ln 2/(10⁻³ m³) = 693 per m³. The collection equation reaches it after 175 s; the 30 exact runs in 1000 cm³ gave a median of 175 s. In a whole cubic metre only 0.69 per m³ is needed, which the mean field reaches after about a minute: the first drizzle in a large volume comes from one-in-a-million luck.

6A temperature for splitting. Two droplets of 10 µm merge. How much surface energy is released, and at what temperature would the Metropolis rule (10.5) accept the reverse split with probability $1/e$?
$3.85\times10^{-11}$ J, and a temperature of $2.79\times10^{12}$ K

The product has radius $10\cdot2^{1/3}$ = 12.60 µm. The surface lost is $2\cdot4\pi(10\ \mu\mathrm m)^2 - 4\pi(12.60\ \mu\mathrm m)^2$, and with $\sigma$ = 0.07422 N/m the energy released is $\Delta E = 3.85\times10^{-11}$ J, which is $9.84\times10^{9}\,k_BT$ at 283 K. The split is accepted with probability $e^{-\Delta E/k_BT_{\rm eff}}$, which is $1/e$ when $k_BT_{\rm eff} = \Delta E$, at $T_{\rm eff} = 2.79\times10^{12}$ K.

No air is that hot. Drops do break up in real clouds, but large ones, when a collision's kinetic energy beats the surface tension (Weber number near 1, chapter 4); that is mechanics, not heat. A Metropolis rule with an invented temperature high enough to split drops would be a free parameter with nothing physical behind it, which is why chapter 10 uses the rule only at the true temperature.

7The cloud that maximises entropy. Show that $n = (N^2/M)\,e^{-Nm/M}$ with $N = N_0/(1 + KN_0t/2)$ solves the collection equation (5.1) when the kernel $K$ is the same for every pair, and that $M$ stays fixed.
The gain is $\tfrac12K(N^4/M^2)\,m\,e^{-Nm/M}$; the rest is bookkeeping

Gain: the convolution $\int_0^m n(m')\,n(m - m')\,dm' = (N^2/M)^2\,e^{-Nm/M}\int_0^m dm' = (N^4/M^2)\,m\,e^{-Nm/M}$, because the two exponents add to $-Nm/M$ for every $m'$. Loss: $K\,n(m)\int_0^\infty n\,dm' = K N\,(N^2/M)\,e^{-Nm/M}$. Left side: $\partial n/\partial t = \big[2N\dot N/M - (N^2/M)(\dot N m/M)\big]e^{-Nm/M}$, and $\dot N = -KN^2/2$ from integrating (5.1) over $m$. Substituting, the left side is $\big[-KN^3/M + \tfrac12K(N^4/M^2)\,m\big]e^{-Nm/M}$, which is gain minus loss. The water $\int m\,n\,dm = (N^2/M)(M/N)^2 = M$ for every $t$.

The exponential is also what maximum entropy gives from $N$ and $M$ alone (10.6). So the cloud in which every pair is equally likely, which is the Metropolis cloud of section 10.3, keeps the maximum-entropy shape once it has it, and approaches it from any start (figure 10.6). Gravity's kernel prefers pairs with different fall speeds, and that preference is what keeps the real cloud far from this shape.

8A puff and a drizzle drop. In a quiet cloud, ε = 10⁻³ m²/s³, with eddies up to 100 m, how far does the stochastic element spread a puff of droplets in ten minutes? And does a drizzle drop of 150 µm that leaves the cloud base reach the ground 300 m below, through air at 80 % humidity?
About 140 m; and no, it evaporates on the way

From (12.3), $\sigma^2 = (\varepsilon L_e)^{2/3} = (0.1\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2})^{2/3}$, so $\sigma$ = 0.46 m/s, and the memory is $T_L = 2\sigma^2/(C_0\varepsilon)$ = 72 s. A velocity that keeps its value for about $T_L$ and then forgets it makes a random walk with diffusion coefficient $K = \sigma^2T_L$ = 15.5 m²/s, and after a time $t \gg T_L$ the spread is $\sqrt{2Kt}$ = 136 m.

The drop: (12.4) with $S = -0.2$, $G = 9.73\times10^{-11}$ m²/s and the ventilation factor, which is 2.00 at 150 µm because the drop falls at 1.21 m/s and refreshes the air around it. Integrating $dr^2/dt = 2GSf_v(r)$ while the drop falls at its own speed, the drop is gone after 385 s, about 44 m above the ground. A 200 µm drop arrives, with 41 % of its water. Rain needs drops well above the size where drizzle begins.
