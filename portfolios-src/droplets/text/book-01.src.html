Part IOne drop

1 One drop sweeping a cloud

We start with the simplest picture that still contains the mechanism: one collector falling through a uniform cloud of identical small droplets, every droplet in its path sticking to it, and the fall speed given by Stokes' law (derived in chapter 2).

Run through drizzle holding a sheet of cardboard in front of you. Each second the sheet sweeps a volume of air equal to its area times your speed, and catches the drops in that volume. A falling collector is such a sheet, with one difference: every droplet it catches makes it wider and heavier, so it falls faster and sweeps more.

The growth law

Two spheres touch when their centres come within $R + r$. So, seen from the collector, every droplet whose centre enters a tube of radius $R + r$ is hit. The droplets fall too, at $v(r)$, so the tube advances through them at the difference of the two speeds. With $W$ the mass of droplet water per m³ of air (the liquid water content), the collector's mass $M$ grows as

$$\frac{dM}{dt} = E\,\pi (R + r)^2\,\big[v(R) - v(r)\big]\,W,\qquad M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_w R^3. \tag{1.1}$$

Here $E$ is the fraction of droplets in the tube that really touch and merge; in this chapter $E = 1$. This is the continuous collection model of Langmuir (1948). With Stokes' fall speed $v(a) = k a^2$, $k = 2(\rho_w - \rho_a)g/(9\mu)$, and $dM/dt = 4\pi\rho_w R^2\,dR/dt$:

$$\frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{E k W}{4\rho_w}\,\frac{(R + r)^3 (R - r)}{R^2}. \tag{1.2}$$

When the droplets are much smaller than the collector, $r \ll R$, this is $dR/dt = cR^2$ with $c = EkW/(4\rho_w)$. Separate the variables, $dR/R^2 = c\,dt$, and integrate from $R_0$:

$$\boxed{R(t) = \frac{R_0}{1 - cR_0 t}}\qquad\text{which is infinite at}\qquad t^* = \frac{1}{cR_0}. \tag{1.3}$$
Check
Units: $k$ is m⁻¹ s⁻¹, $W/\rho_w$ is a pure number, so $c$ is m⁻¹ s⁻¹ and $cR_0t$ is a pure number. Limit $W \to 0$: $R = R_0$ for all time, as it should be.

The radius runs away to infinity in a finite time, and counting powers of $R$ shows why: the swept area grows as $R^2$, the Stokes speed as $R^2$, and the mass to be added for each micrometre of radius as $R^2$. So $dR/dt \propto R^2 R^2/R^2 = R^2$: the growth feeds on itself faster than linearly, and $\int dR/R^2$ converges, which is the condition for reaching infinity in finite time. In a real cloud three things stop it: above about 40 µm the drag is no longer Stokes' (chapter 2), the droplets run out, and millimetre drops break up.

With droplets of finite size the integral of (1.2) can still be done (partial fractions):

$$t(R) = \frac{4\rho_w}{EkW}\big[F(R) - F(R_0)\big],\qquad F(R) = \frac{1}{8r}\ln\frac{R - r}{R + r} - \frac{3R + 2r}{4(R + r)^2}. \tag{1.4}$$

As $r \to 0$, $F(R) \to -1/R$ and (1.4) becomes (1.3).

In our cloud
With $W = 0.643$ g/m³ (300 droplets of exactly 8 µm per cm³; chapter 0's 0.805 g/m³ is the same number spread over a range of sizes, whose mean cube of the radius is 1.25 times $(8\ \mu\mathrm m)^3$), $k = 1.2296\times10^{8}$ m⁻¹ s⁻¹ and $E = 1$, a 40 µm collector grows to 100 µm in 9.96 min by (1.4); with $r \ll R$, (1.3) gives 12.64 min: the droplets' own size shortens the time by a fifth. By (1.3) the radius would blow up at 21.1 min; with the droplets' own size, (1.2), already at 17.8 min.

Where the time goes

Write (1.3) as $t = t^*(1 - R_0/R)$. Growing from 40 to 60 µm takes one third of the time to infinity, and from 40 to 100 µm, 60 % of it. The time is spent while the collector is small, slow and, as chapter 3 will show, clumsy at hitting droplets. So the speed of the whole process is decided at the start, not by the runaway at the end.

A closed box: the droplets run out

In the thesis cloud there is one collector for every 300 droplets, and a 40 µm collector already holds the water of $(40/8)^3 = 125$ droplets. If all collectors were identical and shared the droplets equally, each would end at the radius $R_m$ that holds its own water and its 300 droplets: $R_m^3 = R_0^3 + 300\,r^3$, so $R_m = 60.15$ µm. Mass conservation turns the droplet water into a function of $R$, $W(R) = N_c\,\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_w(R_m^3 - R^3)$, and (1.2) remains a single equation for $R(t)$.

In our cloud
Identical collectors, finite droplets, $E = 1$, Stokes speeds: half of all the water is inside the collectors after 2.93 min and nine tenths after 9.19 min.

Do not read $R_m$ as a ceiling. It is the share of an average collector. A collector that starts larger sweeps more, so it grows more: for $r \ll R$ all collectors see the same droplet water, so $1/R_0 - 1/R$ is the same for all of them, and a collector that starts at 44 µm ends near 70 µm while one that starts at 40 µm ends at 60 µm. Luck in the order of captures does the same (chapter 6). Chapter 5 follows the whole population.
