8 Ice: can collisions make new ice?

Shake ice cubes in a glass and small chips break off: the number of pieces rises, the mass of ice does not. Now picture a large supercooled drop, colder than 0 °C but still liquid, that touches a speck of ice. It freezes from the outside in; the shell traps the liquid core, the core expands as it freezes, and the shell may crack and spit splinters. Here the ice mass does grow (the drop's water becomes ice), and so may the number of pieces.

8.1 Counting new ice

The thesis asked whether collisions multiply ice. Its cold case is the same cloud at −10 °C and 600 hPa, with the droplets split by the thesis's fit for the share that stays liquid when supercooled, 44.55 % at −10 °C: 134.1 liquid droplets and 166.9 ice spheres (density 900 kg/m³) per cm³. Whatever the mechanism, new ice is counted the same way: how often a kind of event happens, times how many fragments each event makes,

$$\frac{dN_{\rm new}}{dt} = \sum_{\text{pairs}} \underbrace{K\,n_1 n_2}_{\text{events per m}^3\text{ and s}}\times\underbrace{F}_{\text{fragments per event}}. \tag{8.1}$$

The event rates come from the kernel of chapters 1–4, with each phase's density, liquid freezing on contact with ice and ice sticking to ice. The fragments per event cannot be derived here: they come only from experiments. So the question becomes which experiments apply to these particles.

8.2 Three candidates

Rime splintering (Hallett and Mossop 1974): an ice particle collecting droplets throws off splinters while the rime freezes. They measured from −2 to −16 °C and found production peaking near −5 °C and stopping below about −8 °C (Korolev and Leisner 2020, Table 2). At −10 °C it is zero.

Shattering of freezing drops. In our cloud almost every such event (2.799 of 2.803 per cm³ and minute with drops of 25 µm radius or more) is a large supercooled drop sweeping up a small ice sphere and freezing, the picture above. That is the geometry of the laboratory experiments, which nucleated their drops with tiny ice crystals. The only free-fall study with drops of our size that reports both how often drops shatter and how many pieces they make, Brownscombe and Thorndike (1968, drops of 50–90 µm diameter), saw at most 14 % of drops shatter, with at most 12 fragments, both maxima over −5 to −15 °C. So a freezing drop makes between 0 and 0.14 × 12 = 1.7 fragments; both numbers are upper-leaning.

Breakup in ice–ice collisions. Phillips, Yano and Khain (2017) fitted a law to collisions of ice particles from 0.5 mm to 5 mm (coefficients as printed by Sotiropoulou et al. 2021). With $K_0 = \tfrac12\mu_{\rm red}\Delta v^2$ the collision kinetic energy of chapter 4 and $\alpha = \pi D^2$ the surface of the smaller particle,

$$F = \alpha A\left[1 - \exp\!\Big(-\Big(\frac{C K_0}{\alpha A}\Big)^{\gamma}\Big)\right],\qquad \gamma = 0.3, \tag{8.2}$$

where $A$ is the number of breakable bumps per m² of ice surface and $C$ (J⁻¹) how fragile they are. Read it as a saturation law: a hard hit breaks every bump, $F \to \alpha A$; a soft hit breaks a fraction. For soft hits, $C K_0 \ll \alpha A$, the bracket is about $(CK_0/\alpha A)^{0.3}$, and the fragments grow only as the 0.3 power of the energy. That weak power is the danger: it keeps producing fragments however small the energy, so the answer depends on how far one trusts it below the experiments.

In our cloud: one collision worked through
A 40 µm ice sphere falls at 0.1726 m/s, an 8 µm one at 0.0076 m/s. Their collision energy is $K_0 = 2.6\times10^{-14}$ J. With $D$ held at the law's lower limit of 0.5 mm inside $A$, as its users recommend ($\alpha$ stays the true surface of the 8 µm sphere), $\alpha A = 9.2\times10^{-3}$ and $C K_0 = 1.6\times10^{-7}$ (at $C = 6.3\times10^6$ J⁻¹), so $F = 3.4\times10^{-4}$ fragments: one new splinter per three thousand such collisions. The old study's rule gave this collision 0.51 fragments.

Over all pairs, the ice–ice collisions in our cloud carry $1.3\times10^{-14}$ to $2.3\times10^{-13}$ J (5 % and 95 % of events), eight to twelve orders of magnitude below the collisions (8.2) was fitted to. Every number from (8.2) here is an extrapolation of that size.

What a student should take from the table is not one number but its shape. The measured channel, freezing drops shattering, can give a few new particles per cm³ and minute, or none: the measurements scatter that much. Ice–ice breakup, the mechanism the thesis was about, gives between 1600 times (the law used as recommended) and 52 times (the law trusted all the way down) less new ice than the old study's rule, which had no measurement behind it. One more caution: 167 ice spheres per cm³ is the thesis's starting state and far more ice than real clouds at −10 °C hold (of order one per litre). Ice–ice collisions scale as the square of the ice number, shattering only linearly, so in a real cloud breakup matters even less than the table shows.
