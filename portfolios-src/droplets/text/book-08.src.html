8 Ice: can collisions make new ice?

Shake ice cubes in a glass and small chips break off; drip water onto a freezing window and some drops crack and spit splinters. Each chip is new ice made from old ice: the number of pieces rises, the mass of ice does not.

The thesis asked whether collisions multiply ice. Its cold case is the same cloud at −10 °C and 600 hPa, with the droplets split by the thesis's fit for the share that stays liquid when supercooled, 44.55 % at −10 °C: 134.1 liquid droplets and 166.9 ice spheres per cm³. New particles per volume and time are (fragments per event) × (events per volume and time). The event rates follow from the kernel of chapters 1–4, with each phase's density, liquid freezing on contact with ice and ice sticking to ice. The fragments per event can only come from measurements, and there are three candidates.

Two conclusions follow, and both are honest only with their limits. First, collisions between the thesis's small ice spheres carry 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻¹⁰ of the energy of the laboratory collisions that break ice, and the only law fitted to those collisions, pushed down that far, gives at least seven thousand times less new ice than the old study's rule assumed. The old rule's tenfold multiplication of ice had no measurement behind it. Second, the one channel that could matter at −10 °C is the shattering of drops that freeze, and its yield was measured for drops freezing alone, not for drops freezing on contact with ice. So these notes bound the answer to the thesis's question, from almost nothing to a few new particles per cm³ and minute, but cannot pin it down.
