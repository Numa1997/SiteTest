12 A cloud with a shape

Watch smoke leave a chimney on a windy day. The whole plume leans with the wind, which is the part you could predict. Its edge frays into puffs that no one could predict one by one but that behave alike on average: they spread the smoke and mix it with the clear air around it. A drop in a cloud moves the same way: it falls, it is carried by the cloud's large motion, and it is jostled by eddies too small to follow one by one.

Until now the cloud was a box in which every drop could meet every other, and no drop had a place. A real cloud has a shape and an edge, and the air outside the edge is dry. This chapter gives the drops positions and lets them move, adding one effect at a time, and then asks what changes where a drop sits.

12.1 The velocity of a drop: a mean part and a stochastic element

A drop moves with the air plus its own fall. Split the air's velocity into a mean part $\mathbf U(\mathbf x)$, the steady motion of the cloud as a whole, and a stochastic element $\mathbf u'$, the eddies:

$$\frac{d\mathbf X}{dt} = \mathbf U(\mathbf X) + \mathbf u'(t) - v_T(r)\,\mathbf e_z. \tag{12.1}$$

The first and last terms are deterministic: $v_T$ is the fall speed of chapter 2, and $\mathbf U$ is an overturning motion held inside the cloud. We write it with a stream function $\psi$, $U_x = \partial\psi/\partial z$, $U_z = -\partial\psi/\partial x$, which makes the flow divergence-free (air is neither made nor lost), and we ask $\psi$ to vanish on the cloud's outline, so that the mean wind runs along the outline and never through it:

$$\nabla^2\psi = -\omega \ \text{inside the cloud},\qquad \psi = 0 \ \text{on and outside the outline}. \tag{12.2}$$

The vorticity $\omega$ is that of two counter-rotating cells, rising in the middle and sinking at the sides; we solve (12.2) on a 25 m grid and scale $\psi$ so the fastest updraft is 1 m/s. The middle term of (12.1) is the stochastic element. We cannot follow each eddy, but Kolmogorov's theory gives two things about eddies up to a size $L_e$: how strong they are and how long they remember. Each component of $\mathbf u'$ has variance $\sigma^2 = (\varepsilon L_e)^{2/3}$, and a drop carried by them forgets its velocity after the Lagrangian time $T_L = 2\sigma^2/(C_0\varepsilon)$, with $C_0 \approx 6$ the measured Lagrangian constant (Sawford 1991). The simplest process with exactly that spread and that memory is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, which we advance over a step $\Delta t$ as

$$u_i'(t + \Delta t) = u_i'\,e^{-\Delta t/T_L} + \sigma\sqrt{1 - e^{-2\Delta t/T_L}}\;\zeta_i + a_i\,\Delta t,\qquad a_i = \tfrac12\,\partial_i\sigma^2 + u_i'\,\frac{(\mathbf u' + \mathbf U)\cdot\nabla\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}, \tag{12.3}$$

with $\zeta_i$ fresh standard normal numbers. The first two terms are exact for any step. The drift $a_i$, advanced with the simple Euler rule, matters only where $\varepsilon$ varies in space: without it drops would drift out of strong eddies and pile up in weak ones, like people drifting out of a crowded room into a quiet one. Thomson (1987) asked what drift keeps evenly spread air evenly spread; for this two-dimensional case with a mean flow the answer is $a_i$ above, and the Checks tab derives it.

In our cloud
With ε = 10⁻² m²/s³ and eddies up to $L_e$ = 100 m: σ = 1.00 m/s and $T_L$ = 33 s, so the eddies spread a puff of drops like a diffusion with coefficient $\sigma^2T_L$ = 33 m²/s, about 200 m in ten minutes. With $L_e$ = 50 m the coefficient is 13 m²/s, with 200 m it is 84 m²/s: it grows as $L_e^{4/3}$. The largest eddy is the least certain number in this chapter, and we show below how much it matters.

Each drop carries its own $\mathbf u'$, unrelated to its neighbours'. That is the standard model of dispersion, and it spreads drops correctly on average, but it cannot fold an edge into separate puffs, because neighbouring drops do not move together. A model that did would have to resolve the eddies as a field.

12.2 Leaving the cloud

Inside the outline the air is saturated and drops neither grow nor shrink by vapour, as in the box. Outside it the air holds 80 % of the vapour it could, and a drop evaporates as

$$\frac{dr^2}{dt} = 2GSf_v(r),\qquad S = -0.2, \tag{12.4}$$

where $G = 9.73\times10^{-11}$ m²/s collects heat conduction and vapour diffusion (Rogers and Yau 1989, chapter 7) and $f_v$ is the ventilation factor: a falling drop keeps meeting fresh dry air, which speeds its evaporation (Pruppacher and Klett 1997, 13-60 and 13-61). $f_v$ is 1.00 at 10 µm, 1.55 at 100 µm and 2.44 at 200 µm. A 10 µm droplet that leaves the cloud is gone in 2.6 s. A 100 µm drizzle drop lasts 205 s, much less than the 412 s it would need to fall the 300 m from the base to the ground; a 150 µm drop also vanishes on the way, and a 200 µm drop arrives with 41 % of its water. Drops leave the slice only through the ground, which counts them as rain; the air reflects at every wall.

12.3 Super-droplets

The cumulus slice holds about $10^{16}$ drops; we cannot follow each one. Shima et al. (2009) let one computational super-droplet stand for $\xi$ real drops of the same size at the same place. The slice is cut into cells 50 m on a side (and 50 m deep); in each cell the super-droplets are shuffled into random pairs, and a pair of multiplicities $\xi_j \ge \xi_k$ merges $\gamma$ times, where $\gamma$ is drawn so that its mean is

$$p = \xi_j\,K(r_j, r_k)\,\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta V}\,\frac{n(n - 1)/2}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}. \tag{12.5}$$

Here $n$ is the number of super-droplets in the cell of volume $\Delta V$, and the last factor makes up for testing only $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ of the $n(n - 1)/2$ possible pairs. In a merger each of the $\xi_k$ drops of the second kind swallows $\gamma$ of the first, so the water is kept exactly. The kernel $K$ is that of chapters 1 to 4: the stochastic element moves drops between cells, but how fast two drops in one cell approach each other is still set by gravity.

Check: super-droplets against the collection equation
Switch the motion off and put all super-droplets in one cell: the method must reproduce the collection equation (5.873 and 13.716 min to half and nine tenths of the water above 40 µm, with the same modes cut at three standard deviations). With 32 super-droplets per cell the box is $12 \pm 4$ % slow in reaching nine tenths (32 runs). With 128 it is $0.1 \pm 2.1$ % slow in our 32 runs and $6.0 \pm 1.6$ % in 64 runs by an independent reviewer: together about 4 %. With 512 it is $1.1 \pm 0.9$ %, with 2048 $0.8 \pm 0.7$ %. The cloud runs use 128. In 44 to 46 % of the box's mergers $\gamma$ exceeds 1, more than Shima et al. intended; a ten times shorter step leaves the times unchanged, so we keep $\Delta t$ = 1 s and say so.
Check: evenly spread air stays evenly spread
Spread 7200 air parcels evenly over the slice and move them with the cell and the stochastic element, with ε falling from $4\times10^{-2}$ m²/s³ in the core to $10^{-3}$ outside. Count them in 150 regions; for pure chance the variance of the counts over its expected value is 1 ± 0.12. With the drift of (12.3) it is 0.85 to 1.19 after 30 min and 0.83 to 1.16 after 2 hours (four runs). Without the drift it is 5.4 to 5.8 and then 15.3 to 15.7: the parcels pile up in the quiet air.

Read it from the top. Gravity alone would bring three quarters of the water to the ground within 45 min. Dry air below the base takes most of that back: drizzle drops fall out of the cloud too small to survive the 300 m of dry air, and three times as much water evaporates under the cloud as reaches the ground. The overturning motion helps the rain, from 19.6 % to 27.7 %, because it carries drops up the middle and round again, so that they leave the base larger. The size of the largest eddy decides the rain more than anything else here: from 50 to 200 m the rain falls from 20.5 % to 4.7 %, because the eddies' spreading grows as $L_e^{4/3}$. Ventilation costs 7.7 points. Turbulence's effect on the collisions themselves (chapter 7) changes nothing measurable, 11.2 % against 11.4 %. These are the honest error bars of a model of this kind: the shares are sure to the point within the model, and uncertain by a factor of a few in the world, because the eddy size and the missing condensation are not known well enough.

12.5 What the shape does

A fair test of shape must change only the shape. So we drew outlines by chance: a flat base at 300 m and a radius that wanders with the angle, $R(\theta) = R_0\exp\big(\sum_{k=2}^{12} c_k\cos(k\theta + \phi_k)\big)$, with random $c_k$ of size proportional to a roughness divided by $k$, and $R_0$ chosen so that every outline encloses the same 0.64 km². Roughness 0 gives a disc cut flat at its base; roughness 0.6 gives a ragged tower of bumps. Each outline gets its own mean motion from (12.2) and the same eddies.

In our cloud
Across the seven different outlines drawn by chance, the rain falls as the outline lengthens: the correlation is −0.91, and each extra kilometre of outline per km² of cloud costs about 1.9 points of rain (from 9.0 % at 4.95 per km to 3.6 % at 7.00 per km). Outlines with similar length per area can still differ by a point or more, because their height and width differ too.

So at fixed size the edge matters, and the mechanism is the one the ladder showed: the eddies carry drops across the outline into dry air, and a longer outline offers more places to cross. The named shapes on the same figure follow the trend loosely but cannot test it, because their areas and heights differ as well.

12.6 Where a drop sits

Now we can run the ledger of chapter 11 inside the shaped cloud, region by region: who eats whom, where the free energy is released, how far each region's sizes are from the distribution of greatest entropy, and what becomes of the water that started in each place.

Over the 45 min, 30 % of the surface free energy was released in the core, 51 % in the flanks and 18 % in the edge band. Of the water that started in the core, 20.5 % reached the ground; from the flanks 16.9 %; from the edge band 7.1 % (mean of 4 runs). Weighted by water, 35 % of the passages of 100 µm happened in the core, which is 17.6 % of the cloud's area.

This is the precise sense of "depending on where we sit in the field". The kernel is the same everywhere in this cloud, but the place decides the diet and the fate. In the core, where the updraft recirculates drops, drizzle already holds 40 % of the water at 10 min and eats a quarter of the mergers; at the edge drizzle holds half as much and collectors still eat nearly every droplet themselves. Water that starts in the core is three times as likely to reach the ground as water that starts in the edge band, which mostly evaporates. Nowhere is the size distribution close to the one of greatest entropy; the only region that comes near the gamma is the dry air below the base, where only the largest drops survive and the distribution has a single hump.

What this chapter does not do
  • No condensation. The outline is a fixed saturated region with nothing to resupply it: a real cumulus is fed by air rising through its base and condensing, and its outline moves. So the shares here are of a fixed start, not a real cloud's efficiency.
  • No vapour budget. Evaporated water vanishes; the dry air never moistens and never cools, which would slow the losses near the edge.
  • The eddies. Each drop has its own eddy velocity (no puffs). Fast-falling drizzle crosses eddies faster than air does, which shortens its memory (the crossing-trajectories effect); that is left out. The largest eddy $L_e$ is a choice, and it moves the rain by a factor of four.
  • Super-droplets lump drops. Each stands for about $4\times10^{11}$ droplets or $4\times10^{9}$ collectors, so a "super-droplet of 100 µm" is a group of drops, not the luckiest single drop of chapter 6; at 128 per cell the timing is about 4 % slow.
  • Two dimensions, and a mean motion of one idealised kind. The motion can be any field given on the grid; a field measured from pictures of real clouds would replace it, and that is the next study, not done here.
