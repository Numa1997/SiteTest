6 Luck: the first drizzle

The collection equation follows the average cloud. Rain begins with the luckiest drops.

Two identical collectors start side by side. One happens to meet another collector early: at a stroke it has twice the mass, falls faster and sweeps more; the other waits. Like savings started a few days apart, the early lead never closes. Among a million collectors, a few are lucky in this way, early and more than once, and they are the first drizzle.

6.1 The process behind the equation

In a well-mixed volume $V$, each pair of drops $(i, j)$ merges in the next $dt$ with probability $K(r_i, r_j)\,dt/V$, whatever happened before. The cloud is a random process that jumps from one list of drop sizes to the next. The probability of every possible list obeys a master equation; its average obeys (5.1) only if the counts in different size classes are uncorrelated, which becomes exact as $V \to \infty$ (Gillespie 1972). In a small volume it is not: when one drop gets lucky, it has also used up droplets that its neighbours can no longer have.

6.2 Sampling the process exactly

Gillespie (1975) showed how to draw the history of such a process without any time step. If the total rate of all pairs is $a_0$, the waiting time to the next merger is exponential, $t_{\rm w} = -\ln u_1/a_0$ with $u_1$ uniform on (0, 1], and the pair is chosen in proportion to its rate. Computing all $N^2/2$ pair rates after every merger is too slow for a million drops, so we use his second device, thinning: propose pairs at a larger rate that is easy to compute, and accept each with the probability true rate over proposed rate. We sort drops into size classes and give each pair of classes an upper bound $K^*$ on the kernel. Proposals then arrive at the rate $\sum K^* n_an_b/V$; a proposed pair is accepted with probability $K/K^*$. The accepted events are exactly the coalescence process, because a Poisson process thinned with probability $K/K^*$ is a Poisson process of rate $K/V$ for each pair.

Method · one event of the exact simulation, as the model takes it

Before the run: every drop is an entry in a list, with its radius. The drops are sorted into size classes, four per doubling of the radius. For every pair of classes $(a, b)$ a bound is computed once: $K^*_{ab} = \pi(r_a^{\max} + r_b^{\max})^2$ times the largest difference of fall speeds between the two classes, times the largest collision efficiency the table holds for them. No pair of drops from those classes can have a larger kernel. Then the model repeats six steps.

  1. The proposal rate. With $n_a$ the number of drops now in class $a$, $$a^* = \sum_{a
  2. Wait. Draw a uniform random number $u_1$; the time to the next proposal is $-\ln u_1/a^*$. Advance the clock.
  3. Choose a pair. Pick the pair of classes with probability proportional to its term in $a^*$, then one drop from each class with equal chances. (If the same drop is picked twice, nothing happens.)
  4. Accept or reject. Compute the true kernel $K$ of these two drops and draw a second number $u_2$. The merger happens if $u_2 < K/K^*_{ab}$.
  5. Merge. The two drops become one of radius $(r_1^3 + r_2^3)^{1/3}$. Move it to its class and update the terms of $a^*$ that contain the changed classes.
  6. Go back to step 1, until the clock reaches the end of the run.

No time step appears anywhere, so there is no time-step error: the only approximations are the kernel itself and the rounding of the computer's numbers. The random numbers come from a seeded generator, so every run can be repeated exactly.

Example 6.1 · the clock of 10 cm³ at the start
One drawn volume of 10 cm³ (seed 1) holds 3010 drops, 10 of them collectors, and so 4 528 545 pairs. Adding the true rates $K/V$ of all pairs gives 1.223 per second: a merger about every 0.82 s. Nearly all of these are a collector catching a droplet. The 45 pairs of two collectors carry 0.22 % of the total rate, one such merger every 373 s on average; the 4.5 million pairs of two droplets carry 0.036 %. Keep the 373 s in mind for section 6.3: the first drizzle drop in such a volume appears after about ten minutes, and it is made by exactly these rare mergers.
Check
The bound is checked at every proposal (zero violations in 430 runs); 50 to 59 % of proposals are accepted, depending on the volume. Each run conserves water exactly. Section 6.4 compares the averages with the collection equation.

The drops are drawn from the two lognormal modes, but cut at three standard deviations ($\sigma_g^3$): no collector starts above 69 µm. Without the cut, the lognormal tail itself would put 0.25 drops of 100 µm in every m³ at the start, and in a large volume the first drizzle would be decided by the initial spectrum, which comes from aerosol and condensation physics outside these notes.

6.3 The first 100 µm drop

Run the process many times in volumes of 1, 10, 100 and 1000 cm³ (200, 100, 100 and 30 runs) and note when the first drop reaches 100 µm.

Method · the time of the first drizzle drop, as the runs measure it
  1. One volume. Draw its drops from the two modes, cut at three standard deviations. The number of each kind is fixed, not drawn: $N V$ rounded, so 1 cm³ holds exactly 300 droplets and 1 collector, 10 cm³ exactly 3000 and 10. Each run has its own seed, $1000\,V + k$ for run $k$ in a volume of $V$ cm³, so every run can be repeated.
  2. Run it. Carry out the exact simulation of section 6.2 for one hour.
  3. Watch every merger. After each merger look at the new drop. The first time any drop reaches 100 µm, write down the clock. A drop never shrinks, so that moment is the first passage and cannot be undone.
  4. Many volumes. Repeat for all runs of that size. The median: sort the times of the runs that made a drop and take the middle one (of an even number, the later of the two middle ones); the curves of figure 6.1 show the share of runs that have had one, against time.
Example 6.2 · why capture alone is too slow
Could the first drizzle come from one collector that is simply big to begin with, sweeping droplets? Take the most favourable case the cloud allows.
  1. The largest start. The collector mode is cut at three standard deviations: $40\ \mu\text m\times1.2^3$ = 69.12 µm.
  2. The most food. Give it all the droplet water of the cloud, $W = 8.050\times10^{-4}$ kg/m³, as 8 µm droplets, and let none be used up.
  3. The growth rate. The radius grows by (1.1) divided by $4\pi\rho_wR^2$, with the drag-balance speeds and the efficiency of chapter 3. At 69 µm, where $E$ = 0.477 and the speeds differ by 0.4231 m/s, that is 3.035 µm per minute; at 100 µm it is 5.556 µm per minute.
  4. Integrate. Stepping the radius forward in steps of 0.05 s from 69 to 100 µm takes 448 s.
  5. Count the meals. Each capture adds $(8\ \mu\text m)^3$ of volume, so the collector needs $(100^3 - 69^3)/8^3$ = 1312 captures.
  6. The luck in capture. Each capture is a random event, and the wait for the next one is exponentially distributed, with a mean that shortens as the collector grows. The total time is the sum of 1312 independent waits, so its variance is the sum of their variances: a relative spread of 2.97 %. Even a collector lucky by three times that spread arrives only about 9 % sooner.
So capture alone, at its very best, needs 7.5 minutes. The exact runs in 1000 cm³ make the first drizzle in three. The extra speed must come from somewhere else, and the runs say where.
In our cloud
In 1 cm³ (one collector and 300 droplets) no drop ever reaches 100 µm: there is not enough water (a 100 µm drop holds the water of 3.75 cm³ of this cloud). The median time to the first 100 µm drop is 605 s in 10 cm³, 286 s in 100 cm³ and 175 s in 1000 cm³. In every one of the 230 runs that made one, the drop got there by merging with another drop of 30 µm or more: collectors meeting collectors, not droplet capture alone, make the first drizzle here. Capture alone would be far too slow: the largest collector the cloud starts with (69 µm), sweeping 8 µm droplets with all of the cloud's droplet water and none used up, would need 1312 captures and 448 s to reach 100 µm, and the number of captures in a given time varies by only a few percent, so no amount of capture luck brings that down to three minutes.

The larger the volume, the sooner its luckiest drop arrives. To reach a cubic metre, which we cannot simulate drop by drop, use the collection equation's tail. A drop above 100 µm stays above 100 µm, so the first one has arrived by time $t$ if the volume holds at least one. If such drops are rare and independent, their number in $V$ is Poisson-distributed with mean $V n_{100}(t)$, where $n_{100}$ is the concentration of drops above 100 µm in the mean field. The formula follows in three steps.

Step 1: cut the volume into small pieces. Divide $V$ into $J$ equal pieces, so small that none holds more than one large drop. A piece holds one with the probability $n_{100}V/J$, and if large drops are independent of each other, the pieces are independent too. (That every piece holds at most one needs the drops to be rare: a few in the whole volume, not a crowd.)

Step 2: no piece holds one. The probability that all $J$ pieces are empty is the product, $(1 - n_{100}V/J)^J$.

Step 3: let the pieces shrink. As $J \to \infty$ this becomes $e^{-n_{100}V}$, the limit that defines the exponential. The median is the moment when this probability has fallen to one half:

$$P(\text{no 100 µm drop in } V \text{ by } t) = e^{-V n_{100}(t)},\qquad \text{median: } V n_{100} = \ln 2. \tag{6.1}$$

Two assumptions went in, and both will be tested: the large drops must be rare (step 1), and their presence in one place must not depend on their presence in another (step 2).

Method · the prediction (6.1), as the model evaluates it
  1. Solve the collection equation for the same cut modes, at 16 bins per doubling with 1 s steps, and write down every 5 s the number of drops per m³ in all bins at or above 100 µm, plus those in the overflow store.
  2. Keep the largest value reached so far. (Later, large drops merge with each other and their number falls; a volume that once held one has still had its first. This is a repair, not part of the derivation: once large drops merge with each other they are no longer independent, and (6.1) no longer holds as it stands.)
  3. The predicted median for a volume $V$ is the first record at which $V$ times that largest value reaches $\ln 2$ = 0.693.
Example 6.3 · the prediction for a litre
  1. $V$ = 1000 cm³ = $10^{-3}$ m³, so the mean field must reach $n_{100} = 0.693/10^{-3}$ = 693 drops per m³ above 100 µm.
  2. The record at 175 s is the first above that: 744.5 per m³.
  3. So the predicted median is 175 s. For 100 cm³ the threshold is 6931 per m³, reached at 280 s; for a whole cubic metre only 0.693 per m³, reached at 60 s at this grid and record spacing.
In our cloud
(6.1) predicts medians of 280 s in 100 cm³ and 175 s in 1000 cm³; the exact runs gave 286 and 175 s. Trusting it for a cubic metre: the first 100 µm drop in 1 m³ appears after about a minute (55, 57 and 61 s with 8, 16 and 32 bins per doubling: (6.1) leans on the far tail of the collection equation, which converges slowly), the first 1 mm drop after about 18 min (untested by exact runs: a 1 mm drop holds the water of 3.75 litres of cloud). Half of the water is in drops above 100 µm only after 585 s.

In 10 cm³ the argument fails, and the way it fails is instructive. The mean field says that at most 32 % of such volumes ever hold a 100 µm drop; every one of the 100 exact runs made one, after a median of 605 s. Ten cm³ hold about ten collectors and the water for one drop of 139 µm, no more. Once two collectors merge, the merged drop is the largest in the volume by far and sweeps up the rest: every box ends with one drop of 100 to 140 µm. The collection equation describes a cloud so large that its large drops keep growing on water from far away: at 605 s it already has 54 % of the water above 100 µm, but packed into drops of 155 µm (mass-weighted mean radius), only 0.39 of them per 10 cm³. Counting those few large drops, (6.1) misses the many boxes whose single drop is just over 100 µm. (6.1) needs large drops that are rare and independent of the volume's own water, which in a volume this small they are not.

So in a cubic metre the first drizzle drop appears about ten times earlier than the time at which half the water has become drizzle. That rain starts with a few lucky drops is Telford's (1955) point, and Kostinski and Shaw (2005) estimated a factor of about ten for the luckiest one in a million collectors when the luck lies in droplet captures. Here the factor is similar but the luck is different: it lies in meeting another collector early. Note what carried the luck: the collection equation's tail already contains it, because (5.1) moves drops one merger at a time. What does not contain it is the smooth-growth bookkeeping of the thesis (section 5.2), in which identical collectors grow identically.

6.4 Small volumes

The collection equation multiplies concentrations: the rate of collector–collector mergers per m³ is $\tfrac12Kc^2$, with $c$ the collectors per m³. Count instead the pairs that really exist, in four steps.

Step 1: the equation's count. In a volume $V$ that holds $n = cV$ collectors, the equation's rate becomes $\tfrac12Kc^2V = (K/V)\cdot\tfrac12n^2$: as if there were $\tfrac12n^2$ pairs, each merging at the rate $K/V$ of section 6.1.

Step 2: the true count. The first collector of a pair can be any of the $n$, the second any of the other $n - 1$, and each pair has been counted twice: $\tfrac12n(n - 1)$ pairs.

Step 3: the ratio. The true rate is the fraction $(n - 1)/n$ of the equation's. A collector cannot meet itself; the equation, written for a cloud too large to count, does not know that.

Step 4: what a random count would change. Had the number of collectors in a volume been drawn at random (Poisson, mean $\bar n$), the average of $n(n - 1)$ would have been $\bar n^2$ exactly, and the deficit would vanish on average. Our volumes hold exactly one collector per cm³ (section 6.3), so the deficit is there in every run. Either way, every merger then uses up partners in its own volume, which a mean field spread over an infinite cloud never notices.

Method · the average growth of small volumes
  1. In every run of section 6.3 the share of the volume's water in drops of 40 µm or more, $F_{40}$, is written down once a minute.
  2. For each volume size the shares of all its runs are averaged, minute by minute: 200 runs of 1 cm³, 100 of 10 and of 100 cm³, 30 of 1000 cm³.
  3. The collection equation for the same cut modes gives the mean-field curve.
The average is taken over the runs' shares. A volume whose single collector is small and one whose collector is large count equally, which is why the 1 cm³ curve starts below the others (figure 6.2).
