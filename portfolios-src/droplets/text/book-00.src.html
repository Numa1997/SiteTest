0 The question

Look at a summer cumulus. It is made of water, but nothing falls out of it: its droplets are about ten micrometres across, and they sink through the air at about a centimetre per second, slower than the air rises in the cloud. Twenty minutes later the same cloud may rain. Raindrops are a hundred times wider than cloud droplets and hold a million times more water each. Something has to gather a million droplets into one drop, and it has to do it in minutes.

Gravity does it. A drop that is a little larger than its neighbours falls a little faster, overtakes the droplets below it and swallows some of them; it grows, falls faster still and swallows more. These notes build that process from first principles, one physical effect at a time, and ask three questions of it:

  1. How long does it take until most of the water sits in large drops? (Observable 1.)
  2. How long until the very first drizzle drop appears in a given volume of cloud, and how much does that time vary from one volume to the next? (Observable 2.)
  3. If the cloud is cold and holds ice, do collisions make new ice particles? (Observable 3, the question of the thesis these notes grew from: N. Koudsie, Collisional Ice Multiplication Processes in Clouds, Bachelor thesis, Leipzig 2022.)

The cloud we study

The test case is the thesis's warm cloud at 10 °C and 850 hPa: 300 droplets per cm³ with radii spread around 8 µm, and one larger drop per cm³ around 40 µm, which we call a collector. Collector is a role, not a kind of particle: in any encounter the larger drop is the collector. Both groups are given as lognormal size distributions (section 5.1). The droplets hold 0.805 g of water per m³ of air, the collectors 0.311 g/m³. The cloud is a closed, well-mixed box: no air rises, nothing condenses, nothing falls out. That isolates collection, at the price of everything else a real cloud does (chapter 9 says what).

A word on size names, all radii: cloud droplets are below 20 µm; drizzle drops have r ≥ 100 µm and fall about 0.7 m/s; raindrops have r ≥ 250 µm. Observable 1 counts the water in drops of radius r ≥ 40 µm, the fraction $F_{40}(t)$, and the times $t_{50}$ and $t_{90}$ at which it reaches one half and nine tenths. It is a measure of growth, not a rain gauge: the box has no ground.

An estimate before any model

A collector of radius $R$ falling at speed $v$ sweeps a cylinder of air of volume $\pi R^2 v$ per second. With the simplest fall-speed law (Stokes, chapter 2) a 40 µm drop falls at 0.197 m/s, so it sweeps $9.9\times10^{-10}$ m³ each second. One million collectors per m³ sweep $9.9\times10^{-4}$ of the air per second: if every droplet they met stuck, the droplet water would be cleared on a time scale of 17 min. Growth speeds this up, a collision efficiency below one slows it down: expect a few minutes to ten minutes for $t_{50}$. Every later result is checked against this estimate.

Conventions
  • SI units; radii in µm in the text. $r$ is a radius, $d = 2r$ a diameter, $R$ the larger radius in a pair.
  • Air at 10 °C and 850 hPa unless stated: density $\rho_a = 1.0458$ kg/m³ (ideal gas), viscosity $\mu = 1.771\times10^{-5}$ Pa s (Sutherland's law with the thesis's constants), water density $\rho_w = 1000$ kg/m³, $g = 9.81$ m/s².
  • "In our cloud" boxes give numbers computed by the model on this page; the Checks tab says how each is tested.
  • The notes climb a ladder: L0 (chapter 1) is the simplest model, and each later chapter adds one effect, with a number that shows it matters.
