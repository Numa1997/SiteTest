7 Turbulence, checked by a number

Stir a cup of tea with tea leaves in it. The leaves ride the swirls, but the heavier ones lag behind the flow and bump into the lighter ones. A cloud's turbulence does the same to drops: its shear moves neighbours past each other, and drops of different inertia answer its accelerations differently.

How strongly a drop feels the smallest eddies is measured by its response time against the eddies' turnover time, the Kolmogorov time $\tau_K = \sqrt{\nu/\varepsilon}$, with $\varepsilon$ the rate at which the turbulence dissipates energy per kg of air. Clouds have $\varepsilon$ from about $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$ m²/s³.

In our cloud
At $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$ m²/s³, $\tau_K$ = 41 ms. An 8 µm droplet (response time 0.8 ms) follows the eddies almost perfectly; a 40 µm collector (20 ms) has a Stokes number of about 0.5.

Ayala, Rosa and Wang (2008) worked out the mean relative speed of two sedimenting drops in turbulence for weak inertia. For the pair that governs observable 1, 40 µm on 8 µm, their formula raises the kernel by 0.4 %, 5.6 % and 28 % at $\varepsilon$ = 10, 100 and 400 cm²/s³. Used inside its range (size ratio below 0.6, radii up to 60 µm, Stokes number below 1) in the collection equation, it shortens $t_{90}$ by 1.7 % at 100 cm²/s³: far less than the 16 % spread between efficiency tables. So turbulence stays a check for observable 1.

For observable 2 it is different. The first drizzle grows through mergers of nearly equal collectors (chapter 6), whose gravitational relative speed is small, and that is exactly where turbulence helps most: simulations find the collision rate of 18 and 20 µm drops doubled at 100 cm²/s³ and raised sevenfold at 400 (Wang et al. 2008). No calculation was found for drops of 50 to 60 µm, so these notes cannot give a number. Turbulence would make the first drizzle earlier than chapter 6 says; chapter 6's times are an upper bound for a turbulent cloud.
