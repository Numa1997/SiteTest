7 Turbulence, checked by a number

Stir a cup of tea with tea leaves in it. The leaves ride the swirls, but the heavier ones lag behind the flow and bump into the lighter ones. A cloud's turbulence does the same to drops: its shear moves neighbours past each other, and drops of different inertia answer its accelerations differently.

How strongly a drop feels the smallest eddies is measured by its response time against the eddies' turnover time, the Kolmogorov time $\tau_K = \sqrt{\nu/\varepsilon}$, with $\varepsilon$ the rate at which the turbulence dissipates energy per kg of air. Clouds have $\varepsilon$ from about $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-1}$ m²/s³.

In our cloud
At $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$ m²/s³, $\tau_K$ = 41 ms. An 8 µm droplet (response time 0.8 ms) follows the eddies almost perfectly; a 40 µm collector (18 ms) has a Stokes number of about 0.4.

Ayala, Rosa and Wang (2008) worked out the mean relative speed of two sedimenting drops in turbulence for weak inertia. The kernel is the sphere of radius $R + r$ round the collector times the mean speed $\langle|w_r|\rangle$ at which partners cross it along the line of centres, $K = 2\pi(R + r)^2\langle|w_r|\rangle$. In still air a partner approaching at the angle $\theta$ from the vertical crosses at $|\Delta v\cos\theta|$, whose mean over the sphere is $\tfrac12|\Delta v|$, which returns $\pi(R + r)^2|\Delta v|$. Turbulence adds a random part to that speed, with variance $\sigma_w^2$. With $\tau_1$, $\tau_2$ the two response times ($\tau = v/g$) and $v_1$, $v_2$ the fall speeds,

$$\langle|w_r|\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\sqrt{\sigma_w^2 + \frac{\pi}{8}(\tau_1 - \tau_2)^2g^2},\qquad \sigma_w^2 = \underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon\,(R + r)^2}{15\,\nu}}_{\text{shear}} + \underbrace{a_0\,(\tau_1 - \tau_2)^2\,\frac{\varepsilon^{3/2}}{\nu^{1/2}}}_{\text{accelerations}} + \underbrace{\frac{2\,\varepsilon}{15\,\nu}\,(v_1\tau_1 - v_2\tau_2)^2}_{\text{settling through shear}}. \tag{7.1}$$

The factor $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ is the mean size of a Gaussian number of unit spread, and the $\pi/8$ is chosen so that with $\sigma_w = 0$ the still-air value $\tfrac12|\Delta v|$ comes back. Each term has a picture. Shear: two points a distance $R + r$ apart in a small eddy move at different speeds, by about the separation over the eddy's turnover time. Accelerations: the air is thrown about with accelerations of size $\varepsilon^{3/4}\nu^{-1/4}$, and two drops of different response time answer them differently ($a_0$ = 6.9 at the Reynolds number of a cloud). Settling through shear: a drop falling at $v$ through sheared air carries a velocity mismatch of about $v\tau$ times the shear. For the pair that governs observable 1, 40 µm on 8 µm, their formula raises the kernel by 0.4 %, 5.6 % and 28 % at $\varepsilon$ = 10, 100 and 400 cm²/s³. The gain comes from the last two terms of (7.1), of similar size: the fluid's accelerations, which the light droplet follows and the heavy collector does not (the tea leaves again), and the coupling of the drops' settling to the shear of the eddies. The second is the larger at 10 and 100 cm²/s³, the first at 400. Used inside its range (size ratio below 0.6, radii up to 60 µm, Stokes number below 1) in the collection equation, it shortens $t_{90}$ by 1.7 % at 100 cm²/s³: far less than the 3.0 min by which the collision efficiency moves $t_{90}$ (10.5 to 13.5 min, chapter 5).

Example 7.1 · formula (7.1) evaluated, step by step
A 40 µm collector and an 8 µm droplet, in turbulence of $\varepsilon$ = 100 cm²/s³ = $10^{-2}$ m²/s³.
  1. The times. $\tau_1 = v_1/g$ = 18.05 ms for the collector and $\tau_2$ = 0.812 ms for the droplet; the smallest eddies turn over in $\tau_K$ = 41.2 ms. The air's kinematic viscosity is $\nu = 1.694\times10^{-5}$ m²/s.
  2. Shear. $\varepsilon(R + r)^2/(15\nu) = 9.07\times10^{-8}$ m²/s². Negligible: 48 µm is nothing to an eddy.
  3. Accelerations. The air's accelerations have the size $\varepsilon^{3/4}\nu^{-1/4}$ = 0.49 m/s², a twentieth of gravity. The term is $a_0(\tau_1 - \tau_2)^2\,\varepsilon^{3/2}\nu^{-1/2} = 5.01\times10^{-4}$ m²/s².
  4. Settling through shear. $\tfrac{2\varepsilon}{15\nu}(v_1\tau_1 - v_2\tau_2)^2 = 8.01\times10^{-4}$ m²/s², the largest of the three.
  5. The random speed. The three add to $\sigma_w^2 = 1.30\times10^{-3}$ m²/s², so $\sigma_w$ = 3.61 cm/s.
  6. Gravity's part. $\tfrac{\pi}{8}(\tau_1 - \tau_2)^2g^2 = 1.12\times10^{-2}$ m²/s², nine times larger.
  7. The mean crossing speed. $\langle|w_r|\rangle = \sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sqrt{1.30\times10^{-3} + 1.12\times10^{-2}}$ m/s = 8.93 cm/s, against 8.46 cm/s in still air: a factor 1.056.
The lesson is in step 7. The turbulent speed is not small: 3.6 cm/s, a third of gravity's 10.6 cm/s (the square root of step 6). But it is random in direction, so the two add under the square root, as the two short sides of a right triangle make its long side, and a third becomes five percent.

That 1.7 % is a lower bound, for two reasons a student should see. The formula is switched off where it is not valid, for collectors above 60 µm, and at 400 cm²/s³ for every collector above 43 µm, whose Stokes number passes 1; that is why the stronger turbulence shortens $t_{90}$ by only 1.1 %, an artefact of the cut, not physics. And only the geometric part of the kernel is raised: simulations find that turbulence also raises the collision efficiency itself, by up to about a factor of 2 for size ratios like ours (Wang et al. 2008), and studies with the full turbulent kernel find a marked speed-up of warm rain (Grabowski and Wang 2013). So turbulence stays a stated check here, and $t_{90}$ in still air is an upper bound on the time in a turbulent cloud.

For observable 2 it is different. The first drizzle grows through mergers of nearly equal collectors (chapter 6), whose gravitational relative speed is small, and that is exactly where turbulence helps most: simulations find the collision rate of 18 and 20 µm drops doubled at 100 cm²/s³ and raised sevenfold at 400 (Wang et al. 2008). No calculation was found for drops of 50 to 60 µm, so these notes cannot give a number. Turbulence would make the first drizzle earlier than chapter 6 says; chapter 6's times are an upper bound for a turbulent cloud.
