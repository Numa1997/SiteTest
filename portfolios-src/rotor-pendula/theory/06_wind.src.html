Part IIThe real wind

6 The wind

Part I held the rotor at a steady speed in a steady wind. Real wind blows harder higher up and comes in gusts of every size, and the rotor answers by speeding up and slowing down. This chapter builds that wind, from the energy it carries to a turbulent field that can be drawn and animated, and asks what the rotor, and with it the second blade, makes of it. On the way it explains why the rotor turns at a tip-speed ratio of 7.55.

6.1 Moving air carries energy

Hold your flat hand out of a car window at motorway speed and the air shoves it back hard; at walking pace you feel nothing. Air has mass, about a kilogram per cubic metre, and mass in motion carries energy.

A cubic metre of air of density $\rho$ ({{n:rho}} kg/m³) moving at $v$ carries $\tfrac12\rho v^2$. Through a frame of area $A$ facing the wind passes, every second, the volume $Av$:

$$P_{\rm wind} = \tfrac12\rho v^2 \times A v = \tfrac12\,\rho\,A\,v^3. \tag{6.1}$$

The speed enters three times: twice in each kilogram's energy, once in how many kilograms arrive. Double the wind, eight times the power. An open doorway, 2 m by 1 m, at 10 m/s passes {{n:door_P}} W, an electric kettle. The rotor's disc, $\pi\times 63^2$ = {{n:disc_area}} m², about two football pitches, receives {{n:Pwind_6}}, {{n:Pwind_8}} and {{n:Pwind_10}} MW at 6, 8 and 10 m/s.

6.2 No turbine can take all of it

A turbine that stopped the air completely would take nothing: still air cannot get out of the way of the air behind it, which would flow around instead. A turbine that does not slow the air takes nothing either. The best slows it part of the way. Treat the rotor as a thin disc of area $A$ that slows the air through it to $v(1-a)$; far behind, the wake moves at $v_{\rm w}$. Mass: $\dot m = \rho Av(1-a)$ per second. Momentum: slowing it from $v$ to $v_{\rm w}$ takes the force $F = \dot m(v - v_{\rm w})$, the thrust (the push of the surrounding air on the widening stream's sides drops out; Bernoulli's law in front of and behind the disc gives the same thrust). Energy: the air's loss of kinetic energy all goes into the disc, where $F$ acts on air moving at $v(1-a)$:

$$\tfrac12\dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})(v + v_{\rm w}) = \dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})\,v(1-a) \quad\Rightarrow\quad v(1-a) = \frac{v + v_{\rm w}}{2}.$$

The speed at the disc is the average of the speeds far ahead and far behind, so $v_{\rm w} = v(1 - 2a)$, and the power is

$$P = \dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})\,v(1-a) = \tfrac12\rho A v^3\cdot 4a(1-a)^2, \qquad C_p = \frac{P}{P_{\rm wind}} = 4a(1-a)^2. \tag{6.2}$$

The power coefficient $C_p$ is zero at $a = 0$ and rises until $dC_p/da = 4(1-a)(1-3a) = 0$, at $a = \tfrac13$ (the model holds for $a < \tfrac12$, where the wake still moves forward):

$$C_{p,\max} = \frac{16}{27} = {{n:betz}}. \tag{6.3}$$

No turbine can take more than {{n:betz_pct}} % of the power of the wind that crosses its disc: the Betz limit (Betz 1920), Figure {{fig:betz}}. At the best setting the wind is slowed by a third at the disc, which chapter 7 uses. The 5 MW turbine reaches $C_p = 0.482$, {{n:cp_of_betz}} % of the limit: {{n:PT_6}}, {{n:PT_8}} and {{n:PT_10}} MW at its shaft at 6, 8 and 10 m/s. Above 11.4 m/s it turns its blades out of the wind to spill the excess; these notes stay below that.

6.3 Blades are wings

Lift and drag
feel
Tilt your flat hand out of the car window, front edge up, and it rises: it turns the oncoming air down, and the air pushes back.
physics
The force across the oncoming air is lift, the force along it drag. A wind gauge's cups are pushed by drag and never outrun the wind; a blade tip runs seven times faster than the wind, which only lift, acting across the air's motion, can do.
measure
In a wind tunnel: the two forces on a wing section, divided by the air's pressure $q = \tfrac12\rho W^2$ and the area $S$, are the coefficients $C_L$ and $C_D$.
maths
$L = qSC_L$, $D = qSC_D$; for a thin wing at small angle of attack $\alpha$, $C_L \approx 2\pi\alpha$.

Ride on a piece of blade at the distance $r$ from the hub. Two streams of air reach you: the wind from the front at $v(1-a)$, and, because you move sideways at $\Omega r$, a head wind, as a cyclist feels on a still day. Their sum, the apparent wind, comes in at the inflow angle $\gamma$ to your path:

$$\tan\gamma = \frac{v(1-a)}{\Omega r}. \tag{6.4}$$

The lift, at right angles to the apparent wind, leans forward by $\gamma$: its part $L\sin\gamma$ along the path turns the rotor, $L\cos\gamma$ pushes it downwind; the drag holds the piece back by $D\cos\gamma$ (Figure {{fig:speeds}}). Turn too slowly, and most of the wind passes the gaps unslowed and leaves swirling; too fast, and drag eats the gain. With lift a hundred times the drag, the best tip speed is several times the wind's: for the 5 MW turbine $\lambda^* = 7.55$ (Jonkman et al. 2009), Part I's ratio. At 8 m/s the tip moves at {{n:ut8}} m/s while the slowed wind reaches it at {{n:ax8}} m/s: the apparent wind is {{n:app8}} m/s, only {{n:inflow8}}° off the blade's path, and the tip helps turn the rotor only if its lift is more than {{n:ld_min}} times its drag. To the tip of a blade, and to the second blade hinged there, the wind is a gentle side-draught on a fast, nearly head-on stream of air.

6.4 A gusty wind over the rotor

Watch a field of tall wheat on a windy day. The gusts do not arrive everywhere at once: darker patches, where the wind presses the stalks harder, sweep across the field, grow, merge and fade. Large patches last longer than small ones. The rotor of a turbine, 126 m across, is wider than most of them, so at any moment some of its disc is in a gust and some in a lull.

No one can predict turbulent wind gust by gust; what can be predicted are its statistics. Turbines are designed against a standard set, IEC 61400-1, used here as NREL's program TurbSim implements it (Jonkman 2009), in four parts.

The mean. Near the ground the air is slowed by friction, so the wind blows faster higher up. The standard takes a power law in the height $z$,

$$U(z) = U_{\rm hub}\left(\frac{z}{90\ \mathrm m}\right)^{0.2}, \tag{6.5}$$

so a blade tip, rising from 27 m to 153 m every turn, meets {{n:shear_bot}} m/s at the bottom and {{n:shear_top}} m/s at the top when the hub sees 8 m/s.

The size of the gusts. At any point the wind wanders about its mean in three directions: along the shaft ($u$), across ($v$) and up ($w$). Their standard deviations are set by a turbulence intensity; in the standard's strongest class, A, $\sigma_u = 0.16\,(0.75\,U_{\rm hub} + 5.6\ \mathrm{m/s})$, {{n:wf_sig_u}} m/s at 8 m/s, with $\sigma_v = 0.8\sigma_u$ and $\sigma_w = 0.5\sigma_u$.

How the gusts spread over time.

A spectrum
feel
The sliders of a music equaliser: the same song is a little bass, a lot of middle, some treble. A gusty wind is a mixture of slow swells and quick flickers in the same way.
physics
The spectrum says how much of the variance, the mean square wobble, sits at each frequency.
measure
Record the wind at one point with an anemometer, take the Fourier transform of the record, square its size, and average over many records: the periodogram.
maths
$\sigma^2 = \int_0^\infty S(f)\,df$; the variance between $f_1$ and $f_2$ is $\int_{f_1}^{f_2}S\,df$.

The standard uses the Kaimal spectrum, fitted to measured winds,

$$S_K(f) = \frac{4\,\sigma_K^2\,L_K/U_{\rm hub}}{\left(1 + 6 f L_K/U_{\rm hub}\right)^{5/3}}, \qquad L_u = 340\ \mathrm m,\quad L_v = 113\ \mathrm m,\quad L_w = 28\ \mathrm m, \tag{6.6}$$

with $L_K$ the size of the eddies that carry most of the gusts. Below $U/(6L)$ it is flat; above, it falls as $f^{-5/3}$, Kolmogorov's law for the cascade in which large eddies break into smaller ones. Integrated, with $x = 6fL_K/U$,

$$\int_0^f S_K\,df' = \sigma_K^2\int_0^x\frac{\tfrac23\,dx'}{(1+x')^{5/3}} = \sigma_K^2\Big[1 - (1 + x)^{-2/3}\Big],$$

so half the variance lies below $(1+x)^{2/3} = 2$, $f_{1/2} = (2^{3/2} - 1)\,U/(6L_K)$: at 8 m/s, {{n:wf_fhalf}} Hz for $u$, swells longer than {{n:wf_thalf}} s. The gusts along the shaft mostly last minutes (Figure {{fig:spectrum}}).

{{table:fig_spectrum}}

How the gusts spread over space.

Coherence
feel
Two people standing a step apart feel the same slow swell of a gust, but not the same flicker.
physics
The coherence of two points is the share of their fluctuation at one frequency that they have in common: 1 moving together, 0 unrelated. Big, slow eddies cover both points; small, quick ones do not.
measure
Two anemometers: from the Fourier transforms $X$, $Y$ of their records, averaged over many records, $\mathrm{Re}\langle XY^*\rangle/\sqrt{\langle|X|^2\rangle\langle|Y|^2\rangle}$ in each frequency band.
maths
$\mathrm{Coh}(r,f)$ falls from 1 at $r = 0$; for eddies drifting at $U$, roughly $\exp(-a\,fr/U)$, distance in units of the eddy's length $U/f$.

The standard gives it for $u$,

$$\mathrm{Coh}_u(r, f) = \exp\left[-12\sqrt{\left(\frac{f r}{U_{\rm hub}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{0.12\,r}{340\ \mathrm m}\right)^2}\,\right]. \tag{6.7}$$

The first term under the root is the eddy-length argument; the second keeps the slowest gusts from being perfectly shared. The standard does not specify $v$ and $w$, and TurbSim's IEC model leaves them unrelated from point to point; these notes give them TurbSim's smooth-model default, $\exp(-0.75\,f r\,\mathrm{s/m})$. The three components are independent of each other.

Making a field with these statistics. Put 121 points on a square grid over the disc, 12.6 m apart. At one frequency $f_k$, the 121 records' parts at that frequency form a random vector of known covariance, $S(f_k)\,\Delta f$ times the coherence matrix $C_{pq} = \mathrm{Coh}(r_{pq}, f_k)$. Factor $C = HH^{\mathsf T}$ (Cholesky, $H$ lower triangular) and draw independent complex numbers $\xi_q$ of unit variance; then

$$\big\langle (H\xi)(H\xi)^\dagger\big\rangle = H\,\langle\xi\xi^\dagger\rangle\,H^{\mathsf T} = HH^{\mathsf T} = C,$$

exactly the covariance wanted. Summing over the frequencies gives the field (Veers 1988):

$$u_p(t) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{k} \sqrt{2\,S(f_k)\,\Delta f}\;\sum_{q\le p} H_{pq}(f_k)\,\xi_{qk}\,e^{2\pi i f_k t}. \tag{6.8}$$

The record is 256 s long, $f_k = k/(256\ \mathrm s)$ for $k = 1$ to 255, stored every 0.5 s, and repeats after 256 s. Its 255 frequencies carry {{n:wf_band_pct}} % of the variance of (6.6) for $u$ at 8 m/s, the rest lying in swells slower than the record; so each spectrum is scaled up over those frequencies until its variance is $\sigma_K^2$, and that is the $S$ of (6.8). (TurbSim reaches the same end by rescaling the series it has made.) Upstream, the field is drawn with Taylor's frozen turbulence (Taylor 1938): eddies drift past faster than they change, so $u(-x, z, t) = u(0, z, t + x/U(z))$.

6.5 What the rotor makes of it

A heavy flywheel turned by a hand that pushes unevenly turns almost evenly: the flywheel smooths the pushes. The turbine's rotor is such a flywheel. At 8 m/s it carries, as kinetic energy, about {{n:store_s}} s of its own output, so a gust lasting a second barely changes its speed; only changes that last tens of seconds do.

The rotor and main generator have $J_{\rm rot} = 4.38\times10^7$ kg m², and

$$J_{\rm rot}\dot\Omega = Q_{\rm aero} - Q_{\rm gen}(\Omega), \qquad Q_{\rm aero} = \frac{P}{\Omega} = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^3\,v^2\,\frac{C_p(\lambda)}{\lambda},$$

with $C_p(\lambda)$ from NREL's ROSCO table and $v$ the wind averaged over the disc, $v_{\rm eff}$. (That table peaks at $C_p$ = 0.466, at $\lambda$ = 7.64, a little below the 0.482 of section 6.2, which comes from Jonkman et al. 2009.) The control sets the main generator's torque from the rotor's speed alone, the torque law, mainly $Q_{\rm gen} = k\Omega^2$. At $\lambda^*$, $v = \Omega r_{\rm rot}/\lambda^*$, so $Q_{\rm aero} \propto \Omega^2$ too: one square law balances the wind at the best ratio in every wind, and the rotor settles there by itself. That is Part I's rule $\Omega = \lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$. Below 8.98 rpm the torque ramps down to zero at 6.9 rpm, so the turbine starts and stops gently.

Two corrections to Part I follow. At a steady 8 m/s the two torques balance at {{n:rpm_ctrl_8}} rpm, {{n:ctrl_vs_lam}} % below $\lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$, because NREL set the square law's constant to balance at $\lambda$ = {{n:lam_k2}} rather than at the peak of this table; that raises the second blade's power by about one per cent. At 6 m/s the rule of Part I would put the rotor at 6.87 rpm, below the ramp's start, where the main generator gives no torque at all. So the rotor runs faster, at {{n:rpm_real_6}} rpm ($\lambda$ = {{n:lam_real_6}}), and the second blade, held stiffer, gives {{n:Pst_real_6}} mW instead of the {{n:Pst_6}} mW of (3.5), a change of {{n:Preal_pct_6}} %.

The gusts reach the second blade in two ways. The Euler torque of (1.6), while the rotor changes speed, changes the power by about {{n:l2_euler_parts}} parts in 10 000: nothing. More important, the rotor's speed itself changes, over tens of seconds, while the second blade settles in about a second (section 4.1): at every moment it swings as in a steady wind at that moment's speed. With $c$ fixed, put $\kappa = \kappa^*\Omega_0/\Omega$ and $G = G_0\Omega_0^2/\Omega^2$ into (3.3), with $\kappa^* = R - 1$ at $\Omega_0$:

$$\bar P(\Omega) = I\Omega^3\,\frac{(R-1)\frac{\Omega_0}{\Omega}\,G_0^2\frac{\Omega_0^4}{\Omega^4}}{2(R-1)^2\big(1 + \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Omega^2}\big)} = \frac{I\,G_0^2\,\Omega_0^5}{2(R-1)\,(\Omega^2 + \Omega_0^2)},$$

and divided by its value at $\Omega_0$,

$$\frac{\bar P(\Omega)}{\bar P(\Omega_0)} = \frac{2}{1 + \Omega^2/\Omega_0^2}. \tag{6.9}$$

With $x = \Omega/\Omega_0$: $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{2}{1+x^2} = -1$ and $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\frac{2}{1+x^2} = +1$ at $x = 1$. So a shift of the mean speed by a fraction $\epsilon$ changes the power by $-\epsilon$, and a speed that wanders about a fixed mean with a relative spread $s$ raises it by $\tfrac12 s^2$ whichever way it wanders (the average of a curve that bends up lies above its value at the average).

On our blade
The full equation of the second blade was run through {{n:l2_records}} ten-minute records of the disc-averaged wind at a mean of 8 m/s, synthesised from the spectrum (6.6), scaled to its band as in section 6.4 and averaged over a continuous disc with the coherence (6.7), the rotor's speed taken from its own equation. The speed wanders with a standard deviation of {{n:sigOm}} % of its mean, and the wander alone adds {{n:jensen}} % by the curvature of (6.9). With a pure square law the speed wanders more, by {{n:sigOm_k2}} %, and the second blade's power changes by {{n:l2_k2_tot}} % against steady operation: the wander adds {{n:l2_k2_wnd}} %, exactly the curvature's $\tfrac12s^2$ = {{n:jensen_k2}} %, and the mean speed moves by {{n:l2_k2_dOm}} % and changes the power by {{n:l2_k2_msh}} %, as the slope of $-1$ predicts. With the turbine's real torque law the total turns to {{n:l2_nrel_tot}} %. In these runs, at 8 m/s, the rotor runs just above the kink; in a lull it drops onto the ramp, where the main generator brakes much less, so it slows less than it speeds up in a gust. Its mean speed rises by {{n:l2_nrel_dOm}} %, which changes the power by {{n:l2_nrel_msh}} %, while the wander still adds {{n:l2_nrel_wnd}} %. The field of section 6.4 tells the same story: in its first record at 8 m/s, class A, the rotor's speed wanders by {{n:wf_om_sd}} % and the second blade gives {{n:wf_P_none}} mW over 256 s, {{n:wf_P_none_pct}} % below the steady {{n:wf_P_calm}} mW. Gusts change the second blade's power by a few per cent, with a sign set by the main generator's torque law, not by the wind.
What to remember
  • The wind carries the power $\tfrac12\rho Av^3$, and no turbine can take more than 16/27 of it; the best slows the wind by a third at the rotor.
  • Blades are wings, and run fastest where they work best: at 7.55 times the wind's speed at the tip. A square torque law holds them there.
  • Turbulence is described by its statistics: a mean that grows with height, the Kaimal spectrum in time (flat, then $f^{-5/3}$), an exponential coherence in space. Veers' method turns them into a field, with a Cholesky factor per frequency.
  • The rotor is a flywheel: gusts make its speed wander slowly, by about a tenth. The second blade follows the rotor's speed from moment to moment, and its power changes by a few per cent.
Problems
  1. A small turbine on a garden pole has a rotor 2 m across. How much power can it take, at most, from a wind of 6 m/s?
    AnswerIts disc has the area $\pi\times 1^2$ m², so the wind carries $\tfrac12\rho A v^3$ = {{n:p0_small_wind}} W through it (6.1). No turbine takes more than 16/27 of that (6.3): {{n:p0_small_betz}} W.
  2. Show that when a rotor runs at its best tip-speed ratio $\lambda^*$ and slows the wind by a third, the inflow angle at its tips does not depend on the wind speed. What is it for the 5 MW turbine?
    AnswerAt the tip $\Omega r_{\rm rot} = \lambda^* v$, so (6.4) gives $\tan\gamma = (1-a)/\lambda^*$: the wind speed cancels. With $a = \tfrac13$ and $\lambda^* = 7.55$, $\gamma$ = {{n:inflow8}}° in every wind in which the rotor is held at $\lambda^*$.
  3. Two points of the disc 12.6 m apart, at 8 m/s: at what frequency has the coherence (6.7) of their $u$ fallen to a half? What length of eddy is that?
    AnswerLeaving out the small second term under the root, $12fr/U = \ln 2$ gives $f = U\ln2/(12r)$ = {{n:p6_coh_f}} Hz, an eddy $U/f$ = {{n:p6_coh_L}} m long: about seventeen times the distance between the points.
