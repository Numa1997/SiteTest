Part IIThe real wind

6 The wind

Part I held the rotor at a steady speed in a steady wind. Real wind is neither. It blows harder higher up, it comes in gusts of every size, and the rotor answers by speeding up and slowing down. This chapter builds the wind that a turbine meets, from the energy it carries to a turbulent field that can be drawn and animated, and then asks what the rotor, and with it the second blade, makes of it. On the way it explains the one fact about turbines that Part I took on trust: why the rotor turns at a tip-speed ratio of 7.55.

6.1 Moving air carries energy

Hold your flat hand out of the window of a car at motorway speed and the air shoves it back hard. At walking pace you feel nothing. Air has mass, about a kilogram in every cubic metre, and mass in motion carries kinetic energy.

A cubic metre of air has the mass $\rho$, its density ({{n:rho}} kg/m³), so moving at the speed $v$ it carries the kinetic energy $\tfrac12\rho v^2$. Through a frame of area $A$ facing the wind passes, every second, the air that was within the distance $v$ upwind of it, a volume $Av$. So the wind carries through the frame the power

$$P_{\rm wind} = \tfrac12\rho v^2 \times A v = \tfrac12\,\rho\,A\,v^3. \tag{6.1}$$

The speed enters three times: twice in the energy of each kilogram, once more in how many kilograms arrive each second. Double the wind and its power grows eightfold. An open doorway, 2 m by 1 m, in a wind of 10 m/s passes {{n:door_P}} W, about one electric kettle. The 5 MW turbine's blades sweep a disc of $\pi\times 63^2$ = {{n:disc_area}} m², about two football pitches, which at 6, 8 and 10 m/s receives {{n:Pwind_6}}, {{n:Pwind_8}} and {{n:Pwind_10}} MW.

6.2 No turbine can take all of it

If a turbine took all of the wind's energy, the air leaving it would stand still, and still air cannot get out of the way of the air arriving behind it, which would flow around the turbine instead. A turbine that does not slow the wind takes nothing either. The best turbine slows the wind part of the way. To find how far, treat the rotor as a thin disc of area $A$ that slows the air through it to $v(1-a)$; the number $a$ is the fraction of the speed lost at the disc. Far behind, the air has slowed further, to the wake speed $v_{\rm w}$. Three laws fix everything. Mass: every second the mass $\dot m = \rho A v(1-a)$ passes the disc. Momentum: slowing it from $v$ to $v_{\rm w}$ takes the force $F = \dot m(v - v_{\rm w})$, which the air exerts back on the disc as its thrust. (This leaves out the push of the surrounding air on the sides of the widening stream; the derivation that keeps it, with Bernoulli's law in front of and behind the disc, gives the same thrust.) Energy: the air loses kinetic energy at the rate $\tfrac12\dot m(v^2 - v_{\rm w}^2)$, and with no friction anywhere all of it goes into the disc, where the force $F$ acts on air moving at $v(1-a)$:

$$\tfrac12\dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})(v + v_{\rm w}) = \dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})\,v(1-a) \quad\Rightarrow\quad v(1-a) = \frac{v + v_{\rm w}}{2}.$$

The speed at the disc is the average of the speeds far ahead and far behind, so $v_{\rm w} = v(1 - 2a)$, and the power is

$$P = \dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})\,v(1-a) = \tfrac12\rho A v^3\cdot 4a(1-a)^2, \qquad C_p = \frac{P}{P_{\rm wind}} = 4a(1-a)^2. \tag{6.2}$$

The power coefficient $C_p$ is zero at $a = 0$ and rises until $dC_p/da = 4(1-a)(1-3a) = 0$, at $a = \tfrac13$ (the model holds for $a < \tfrac12$, where the wake still moves forward):

$$C_{p,\max} = \frac{16}{27} = {{n:betz}}. \tag{6.3}$$

No turbine can take more than {{n:betz_pct}} % of the power of the wind that crosses its disc: the Betz limit (Betz 1920). At the best setting the wind is slowed by a third at the disc, a fact chapter 7 uses for the air at the second blade. The 5 MW turbine reaches $C_p = 0.482$ at best, {{n:cp_of_betz}} % of the limit, and takes {{n:PT_6}}, {{n:PT_8}} and {{n:PT_10}} MW at its shaft at 6, 8 and 10 m/s. It reaches its full 5 MW at 11.4 m/s; in stronger wind it turns its blades a little out of the wind to spill the excess. These notes stay below that.

6.3 Blades are wings

Tilt your flat hand out of the car window a little, front edge up, and it rises: it turns the oncoming air downwards, and the air pushes back. That force at right angles to the oncoming air is lift; the smaller force along it is drag. The cups of a weather station's wind gauge are pushed round by drag, so they can never move faster than the wind. The tip of a turbine blade moves more than seven times faster than the wind, which only lift can do, because lift acts across the air's motion. Each blade is a long, twisted wing.

Ride on a piece of blade at the distance $r$ from the hub. Two streams of air reach you: the wind from the front at $v(1-a)$, and, because you move sideways at $\Omega r$, a head wind from the side you move towards, as a cyclist feels on a still day. Their sum, the apparent wind, comes in at the inflow angle $\gamma$ to your path, with

$$\tan\gamma = \frac{v(1-a)}{\Omega r}. \tag{6.4}$$

The lift, at right angles to the apparent wind, leans forward by $\gamma$: its part along the blade's path, $L\sin\gamma$, turns the rotor; its part along the wind, $L\cos\gamma$, pushes the rotor downwind. The drag holds the piece back by $D\cos\gamma$. Figure 9 draws it. How fast should the rotor turn? Too slowly, and most of the wind passes through the gaps between the blades unslowed, and what is slowed is left swirling; too fast, and the blades' drag eats the gain. With lift about a hundred times the drag, the best balance falls at a tip speed several times the wind's. The ratio of the two is the tip-speed ratio $\lambda = \Omega r_{\rm rot}/v$, and for the 5 MW turbine the best is $\lambda^* = 7.55$ (Jonkman et al. 2009). That is the ratio Part I used. At 8 m/s the tip moves at {{n:ut8}} m/s while the slowed wind reaches it at {{n:ax8}} m/s: the apparent wind is {{n:app8}} m/s, only {{n:inflow8}}° off the blade's path, and the tip helps turn the rotor only if its lift is more than {{n:ld_min}} times its drag. To the tip of a blade, and to the second blade hinged there, the wind is a gentle side-draught on a fast, nearly head-on stream of air.

6.4 A gusty wind over the rotor

Watch a field of tall wheat on a windy day. The gusts do not arrive everywhere at once: darker patches, where the wind presses the stalks harder, sweep across the field, grow, merge and fade. Large patches last longer than small ones. The rotor of a turbine, 126 m across, is wider than most of them, so at any moment some of its disc is in a gust and some in a lull.

The wind that reaches a turbine is turbulent, and no one can predict it gust by gust. What can be predicted are its statistics, and turbines are designed against a standard set of them, IEC 61400-1. These notes use that standard in the form NREL's program TurbSim implements (Jonkman 2009). It has four parts.

The mean. Near the ground the air is slowed by friction, so the wind blows faster higher up. The standard takes a power law in the height $z$,

$$U(z) = U_{\rm hub}\left(\frac{z}{90\ \mathrm m}\right)^{0.2}, \tag{6.5}$$

so the tip of a blade, which rises from 27 m to 153 m above the ground every turn, meets {{n:shear_bot}} m/s at the bottom of its circle and {{n:shear_top}} m/s at the top when the hub sees 8 m/s.

The size of the gusts. At any point the wind wanders about its mean in three directions: along the shaft ($u$), across ($v$) and up ($w$). Their standard deviations are set by a turbulence intensity; in the standard's strongest class, A, $\sigma_u = 0.16\,(0.75\,U_{\rm hub} + 5.6\ \mathrm{m/s})$, {{n:wf_sig_u}} m/s at 8 m/s, with $\sigma_v = 0.8\sigma_u$ and $\sigma_w = 0.5\sigma_u$.

How the gusts spread over time. A record of $u$ at one point is a mixture of slow swells and quick flickers. Its spectrum $S(f)$ says how the variance is shared among frequencies: $\sigma^2 = \int_0^\infty S(f)\,df$. The standard uses the Kaimal spectrum, fitted to measured winds,

$$S_K(f) = \frac{4\,\sigma_K^2\,L_K/U_{\rm hub}}{\left(1 + 6 f L_K/U_{\rm hub}\right)^{5/3}}, \qquad L_u = 340\ \mathrm m,\quad L_v = 113\ \mathrm m,\quad L_w = 28\ \mathrm m, \tag{6.6}$$

where $L_K$ is the size of the eddies that carry most of the gusts. Below the frequency $U/(6L)$ the spectrum is flat: slow swells of all lengths are equally likely. Above it the spectrum falls as $f^{-5/3}$, the law Kolmogorov found for the cascade in which large eddies break into smaller ones. At 8 m/s, $U_{\rm hub}/(6L_u)$ is {{n:wf_fcorner}} Hz, and half the variance of $u$ lies below {{n:wf_fhalf}} Hz, in swells that take more than {{n:wf_thalf}} s to pass: the gusts along the shaft mostly last minutes.

How the gusts spread over space. Two points close together feel the same large, slow gusts but different small, quick ones. The coherence of two points a distance $r$ apart is the share of their fluctuation at frequency $f$ that they have in common, between 1 (moving together) and 0 (unrelated). The standard gives it for $u$,

$$\mathrm{Coh}_u(r, f) = \exp\left[-12\sqrt{\left(\frac{f r}{U_{\rm hub}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{0.12\,r}{340\ \mathrm m}\right)^2}\,\right]. \tag{6.7}$$

The first term under the root says that a gust of frequency $f$, drifting past at $U_{\rm hub}$, is an eddy of length $U_{\rm hub}/f$, and two points are shared by it only if they lie well within that length. The standard does not specify the coherence of $v$ and $w$, and TurbSim's IEC model leaves them unrelated from point to point. These notes give them a size too, with TurbSim's own default for its smooth model, $\exp(-0.75\,f r\,\mathrm{s/m})$; the three components are independent of each other.

Making a field with these statistics. Put 121 points on a square grid over the disc, 12.6 m apart. At one frequency $f_k$, the parts of the 121 records at that frequency form a random vector whose covariance is known: $S(f_k)\,\Delta f$ times the coherence matrix $C_{pq} = \mathrm{Coh}(r_{pq}, f_k)$. Draw 121 independent complex random numbers $\xi_q$ of unit variance. Factor $C = HH^{\mathsf T}$ by Cholesky's method, with $H$ lower triangular; then $H\xi$ has exactly the covariance $C$, because $\langle H\xi\,(H\xi)^{\dagger}\rangle = H\langle\xi\xi^\dagger\rangle H^{\mathsf T} = HH^{\mathsf T}$. Summing over the frequencies gives the field (Veers 1988):

$$u_p(t) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{k} \sqrt{2\,S(f_k)\,\Delta f}\;\sum_{q\le p} H_{pq}(f_k)\,\xi_{qk}\,e^{2\pi i f_k t}. \tag{6.8}$$

Here the record is 256 s long, with the frequencies $f_k = k/(256\ \mathrm s)$ up to 1 Hz, stored every 0.5 s; it repeats after 256 s. A record of finite length keeps only part of the spectrum: at 8 m/s the band from 1/256 Hz to 1 Hz holds {{n:wf_band_pct}} % of the variance of (6.6) for $u$, the rest lying in swells slower than the record. So, as TurbSim does, each spectrum is scaled up over the kept band until its variance is $\sigma_K^2$; that scaled spectrum is the $S$ of (6.8). Upstream of the rotor, the same field can be drawn with Taylor's frozen turbulence (Taylor 1938): the eddies drift past faster than they change, so the air $x$ metres upstream now is the air that will reach the rotor $x/U$ seconds later, $u(-x, z, t) = u(0, z, t + x/U(z))$.

6.5 What the rotor makes of it

A heavy flywheel turned by a hand that pushes unevenly turns almost evenly: the flywheel smooths the pushes. The turbine's rotor is such a flywheel. At 8 m/s it carries, as kinetic energy, about {{n:store_s}} s of its own output, so a gust lasting a second barely changes its speed; only changes that last tens of seconds do.

The rotor and the main generator together have a moment of inertia $J_{\rm rot} = 4.38\times10^7$ kg m², and the rotor's speed obeys Newton's law for turning, $J_{\rm rot}\dot\Omega = Q_{\rm aero} - Q_{\rm gen}(\Omega)$. The wind's torque is its power (6.2) over the angular speed, $Q_{\rm aero} = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^3v^2C_p(\lambda)/\lambda$, with $C_p(\lambda)$ from NREL's published table and $v$ the wind averaged over the disc, $v_{\rm eff}$. The main generator's torque is set by the control computer from the rotor's speed alone, by a rule called the torque law. Its main part is a square law, $Q_{\rm gen} = k\Omega^2$: at the best tip-speed ratio the wind's torque is proportional to $v^2$, and so to $\Omega^2$, so a square law balances it at the best ratio in every wind, and the rotor settles there by itself. That is how Part I's rule $\Omega = \lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$ comes about. Below 8.98 rpm the law has a kink: the torque ramps down in a straight line to zero at 6.9 rpm, so that the turbine starts and stops gently.

Two corrections to Part I follow. At a steady 8 m/s the two torques balance at {{n:rpm_ctrl_8}} rpm, {{n:ctrl_vs_lam}} % below $\lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$, because NREL set the square law's constant to balance at $\lambda$ = {{n:lam_k2}} rather than at the peak of this table; that raises the second blade's power by about one per cent. At 6 m/s the rule of Part I would put the rotor at 6.87 rpm, below the ramp's start, where the main generator gives no torque at all. So the rotor runs faster, at {{n:rpm_real_6}} rpm ($\lambda$ = {{n:lam_real_6}}), and the second blade, held stiffer, gives {{n:Pst_real_6}} mW instead of the {{n:Pst_6}} mW of (3.5), a change of {{n:Preal_pct_6}} %.

The gusts reach the second blade in two ways. While the rotor speeds up or slows down, the Euler torque of (1.6) acts; it changes the power by about {{n:l2_euler_parts}} parts in 10 000, nothing. More important, the rotor's speed itself changes. The second blade settles to a new speed in about a second (section 4.1), while the rotor's speed changes over tens of seconds, so at every moment the second blade swings as it would at that moment's speed in a steady wind. With the generator's constant $c$ fixed, (3.3) at the speed $\Omega$, divided by its value at the speed $\Omega_0$ for which $c$ is the best brake, is

$$\frac{\bar P(\Omega)}{\bar P(\Omega_0)} = \frac{2}{1 + \Omega^2/\Omega_0^2}. \tag{6.9}$$

(In rotor turns $R$ is the same at every speed, but $\kappa = c/(I\Omega)$ and $G$ change; this formula carries both.) Its slope at $\Omega_0$ is $-1$: a shift of the mean speed by a fraction $\epsilon$ changes the power by $-\epsilon$. And it curves upwards there, so a speed that wanders about a fixed mean raises the average power, whichever way it wanders, by about $s^2/2$ for a relative spread $s$.

What to remember
  • The wind carries the power $\tfrac12\rho Av^3$, and no turbine can take more than 16/27 of it; the best slows the wind by a third at the rotor.
  • Blades are wings, and run fastest where they work best: at 7.55 times the wind's speed at the tip. A square torque law holds them there.
  • Turbulence is described by its statistics: a mean that grows with height, the Kaimal spectrum in time, an exponential coherence in space. Veers' method turns them into a field, with a Cholesky factor per frequency.
  • The rotor is a flywheel: gusts make its speed wander slowly, by about a tenth. The second blade follows the rotor's speed from moment to moment, and its power changes by a few per cent.
Problems
  1. A small turbine on a garden pole has a rotor 2 m across. How much power can it take, at most, from a wind of 6 m/s?
    AnswerIts disc has the area $\pi\times 1^2$ m², so the wind carries $\tfrac12\rho A v^3$ = {{n:p0_small_wind}} W through it (6.1). No turbine takes more than 16/27 of that (6.3): {{n:p0_small_betz}} W.
  2. Show that when a rotor runs at its best tip-speed ratio $\lambda^*$ and slows the wind by a third, the inflow angle at its tips does not depend on the wind speed. What is it for the 5 MW turbine?
    AnswerAt the tip $\Omega r_{\rm rot} = \lambda^* v$, so (6.4) gives $\tan\gamma = (1-a)/\lambda^*$: the wind speed cancels. With $a = \tfrac13$ and $\lambda^* = 7.55$, $\gamma$ = {{n:inflow8}}° in every wind in which the rotor is held at $\lambda^*$.
  3. Two points of the disc 12.6 m apart, at 8 m/s: at what frequency has the coherence (6.7) of their $u$ fallen to a half? What length of eddy is that?
    AnswerLeaving out the small second term under the root, $12fr/U = \ln 2$ gives $f = U\ln2/(12r)$ = {{n:p6_coh_f}} Hz, an eddy $U/f$ = {{n:p6_coh_L}} m long: about seventeen times the distance between the points.
