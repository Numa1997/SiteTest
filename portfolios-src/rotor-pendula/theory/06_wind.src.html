Part IIThe real wind

6 The wind

Part I held the rotor at a steady speed in a steady wind. Real wind blows harder higher up and comes in gusts of every size, and the rotor answers by speeding up and slowing down. This chapter builds that wind, from the energy it carries to a turbulent field that can be drawn and animated, and asks what the rotor, and with it the second blade, makes of it. On the way it shows why a rotor has a best tip-speed ratio, and how the control holds it there.

6.1 Moving air carries energy

Hold your flat hand out of a car window at motorway speed and the air shoves it back hard; at walking pace you feel nothing. Air has mass, about a kilogram per cubic metre, and mass in motion carries energy.

A cubic metre of air of density $\rho$ ({{n:rho}} kg/m³) moving at $v$ carries $\tfrac12\rho v^2$. Through a frame of area $A$ facing the wind passes, every second, the volume $Av$:

$$P_{\rm wind} = \tfrac12\rho v^2 \times A v = \tfrac12\,\rho\,A\,v^3. \tag{6.1}$$

The speed enters three times: twice in each kilogram's energy, once in how many kilograms arrive. Double the wind, eight times the power. An open doorway, 2 m by 1 m, at 10 m/s passes {{n:door_P}} W, an electric kettle. The rotor's disc, $\pi\times 63^2$ = {{n:disc_area}} m², about two football pitches, receives {{n:Pwind_6}}, {{n:Pwind_8}} and {{n:Pwind_10}} MW at 6, 8 and 10 m/s.

6.2 No turbine can take all of it

A turbine that stopped the air completely would take nothing: still air cannot get out of the way of the air behind it, which would flow around instead. A turbine that does not slow the air takes nothing either. The best slows it part of the way. Treat the rotor as a thin disc of area $A$ that slows the air through it to $v(1-a)$; far behind, the wake moves at $v_{\rm w}$. Three laws of bookkeeping then fix everything. Mass: the disc passes $\dot m = \rho Av(1-a)$ kilograms per second. Momentum: slowing that air from $v$ to $v_{\rm w}$ takes the force $F = \dot m(v - v_{\rm w})$, the thrust. (The surrounding air also pushes on the widening stream's sides. The model assumes that push adds up to nothing along the stream's direction, which holds when the balance is taken over a region so large that the pressure on its whole boundary is that of the open air; both routes, the one by energy above and the one by pressures below, rest on this assumption.) Energy: all the kinetic energy the air loses goes into the disc, where $F$ acts on air moving at $v(1-a)$:

$$\tfrac12\dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})(v + v_{\rm w}) = \dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})\,v(1-a) \quad\Rightarrow\quad v(1-a) = \frac{v + v_{\rm w}}{2}.$$

The same result follows from pressures, the route Froude took. Far ahead and far behind, the stream is at the pressure $p_\infty$ of the open air. Bernoulli's law holds along the stream on each side of the disc, but not across it, where the disc takes energy out. With $p_+$ just in front of the disc and $p_-$ just behind it, both at the disc's speed $v(1-a)$,

$$p_\infty + \tfrac12\rho v^2 = p_+ + \tfrac12\rho v^2(1-a)^2, \qquad p_- + \tfrac12\rho v^2(1-a)^2 = p_\infty + \tfrac12\rho v_{\rm w}^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad p_+ - p_- = \tfrac12\rho\,(v^2 - v_{\rm w}^2).$$

The thrust is that pressure step times the disc's area. Setting $A(p_+ - p_-)$ equal to the momentum's $\dot m(v - v_{\rm w}) = \rho Av(1-a)(v - v_{\rm w})$ and dividing by $\rho A(v - v_{\rm w})$ gives $v(1-a) = \tfrac12(v + v_{\rm w})$ again. The speed at the disc is the average of the speeds far ahead and far behind, so $v_{\rm w} = v(1 - 2a)$: the stream loses half its slowing in front of the disc and half behind it, which is why it widens on both sides (Figure {{fig:betz}}, left). The power is

$$P = \dot m\,(v - v_{\rm w})\,v(1-a) = \tfrac12\rho A v^3\cdot 4a(1-a)^2, \qquad C_p = \frac{P}{P_{\rm wind}} = 4a(1-a)^2. \tag{6.2}$$

The power coefficient $C_p$ is zero at $a = 0$ and rises until $dC_p/da = 4(1-a)(1-3a) = 0$, at $a = \tfrac13$ (the model holds for $a < \tfrac12$, where the wake still moves forward):

$$C_{p,\max} = \frac{16}{27} = {{n:betz}}. \tag{6.3}$$

No turbine can take more than {{n:betz_pct}} % of the power of the wind that crosses its disc: the Betz limit (Betz 1920), Figure {{fig:betz}}. At the best setting the wind is slowed by a third at the disc, which chapter 7 uses. The 5 MW turbine reaches $C_p = 0.482$, {{n:cp_of_betz}} % of the limit: {{n:PT_6}}, {{n:PT_8}} and {{n:PT_10}} MW at its shaft at 6, 8 and 10 m/s. Above 11.4 m/s it turns its blades out of the wind to spill the excess; these notes stay below that.

6.3 Blades are wings

Lift and drag
feel
Tilt your flat hand out of the car window, front edge up, and it rises: it turns the oncoming air down, and the air pushes back.
physics
The force across the oncoming air is lift, the force along it drag. A wind gauge's cups are pushed by drag and never outrun the wind; a blade tip runs seven times faster than the wind, which only lift, acting across the air's motion, can do.
measure
In a wind tunnel: the two forces on a wing section, divided by the air's pressure $q = \tfrac12\rho W^2$ and the area $S$, are the coefficients $C_L$ and $C_D$.
maths
$L = qSC_L$, $D = qSC_D$; for a thin wing at small angle of attack $\alpha$, $C_L \approx 2\pi\alpha$: the wing turns the stream down by an angle proportional to $\alpha$, and the factor $2\pi$ comes from the flow round a thin plate (thin-aerofoil theory).

Ride on a piece of blade at the distance $r$ from the hub. Two streams of air reach you: the wind from the front at $v(1-a)$, and, because you move sideways at $\Omega r$, a head wind, as a cyclist feels on a still day. Their sum, the apparent wind, comes in at the inflow angle $\gamma$ to your path:

$$\tan\gamma = \frac{v(1-a)}{\Omega r}. \tag{6.4}$$

The lift, at right angles to the apparent wind, leans forward by $\gamma$: its part $L\sin\gamma$ along the path turns the rotor, $L\cos\gamma$ pushes it downwind; the drag holds the piece back by $D\cos\gamma$ (Figure {{fig:speeds}}). Why is there a best speed? Two losses pull against each other. Turn too slowly, and for a given power $P$ the rotor must take a large torque $Q = P/\Omega$ from the air. By Newton's third law the air takes the opposite torque, and leaves the rotor swirling: angular momentum flows into the wake at the rate $Q$. Carried by the mass flow $\dot m$ at a radius about $r$ (take the tip's, $r_{\rm rot}$, for a rough estimate; the inner rings swirl more, so the estimate is on the low side), it spins the wake at the speed $v_s \approx Q/(\dot m r)$, which costs the kinetic energy $\tfrac12\dot m v_s^2$ per second. As a share of the power, with $P/\dot m = C_pv^2/[2(1-a)]$ from (6.2) and $\lambda = \Omega r_{\rm rot}/v$,

$$\frac{\tfrac12\dot m v_s^2}{Q\Omega} = \frac{P}{2\dot m\,\Omega^2r^2} = \frac{C_p}{4(1-a)}\,\frac{1}{\lambda^2} = \frac{2}{9\lambda^2}\quad\text{at } a = \tfrac13,\ C_p = \tfrac{16}{27}:$$

a large loss for a slow rotor, a small one for a fast rotor. Turn too fast, and drag eats the gain: on a piece of blade the useful part of the lift is $L\sin\gamma \approx L/\lambda_r$, with $\lambda_r = \Omega r/v$ its own speed ratio, while the drag holds the piece back with $D\cos\gamma \approx D$, so the share lost to drag is about $\lambda_rC_D/C_L$, growing with the speed. The drag share depends on the radius through $\lambda_r = \lambda r/r_{\rm rot}$; an ideal rotor takes the same power from every square metre of its disc, so a ring of radius $r$ delivers a share proportional to $r\,dr$, and the drag share averaged over the disc is

$$\lambda\,\frac{C_D}{C_L}\,\frac{\int_0^{r_{\rm rot}}(r/r_{\rm rot})\,r\,dr}{\int_0^{r_{\rm rot}}r\,dr} = \frac23\,\lambda\,\frac{C_D}{C_L}.$$

Together the losses are about

$$\frac{c_s}{\lambda^2} + c_d\,\lambda\,\frac{C_D}{C_L}, \qquad c_s = \frac29,\quad c_d = \frac23,$$

and their sum is smallest where its derivative vanishes, $-2c_s/\lambda^3 + c_dC_D/C_L = 0$:

$$\lambda^* \approx \Big(\frac{2c_s}{c_d}\,\frac{C_L}{C_D}\Big)^{1/3} = \Big(\frac23\,\frac{C_L}{C_D}\Big)^{1/3}.$$

With lift a hundred times the drag this gives about four: several times the wind's speed, and only weakly dependent on the wing, as a cube root. A real rotor turns faster still, because of a third loss the estimate leaves out. At each blade's tip the high pressure on one face leaks round the end to the low pressure on the other, and the air curls into a tip vortex, a whirl trailing from each tip in a helix; near the tips the blade does less work than the simple picture says. The worse the leak, the farther apart the helix's turns, and that spacing, the distance the wind carries the wake while the next blade comes round, is $2\pi r_{\rm rot}/(B\lambda)$ for $B$ blades. So this loss too falls as the rotor turns faster, as $1/\lambda$ rather than $1/\lambda^2$, and it pulls the best ratio up. NREL's full calculation for the 5 MW rotor (blade-element momentum theory, which joins the wing forces of this section to the momentum balance of section 6.2 ring by ring, with a correction for the tip leak) gives $\lambda^* = 7.55$ (Jonkman et al. 2009). That is the ratio Part I uses throughout. At 8 m/s the tip moves at {{n:ut8}} m/s while the slowed wind reaches it at {{n:ax8}} m/s: the apparent wind is {{n:app8}} m/s, only {{n:inflow8}}° off the blade's path, and the tip helps turn the rotor only if its lift is more than {{n:ld_min}} times its drag. To the tip of a blade, and to the second blade hinged there, the wind is a gentle side-draught on a fast, nearly head-on stream of air.

6.4 A gusty wind over the rotor

Watch a field of tall wheat on a windy day. The gusts do not arrive everywhere at once: darker patches, where the wind presses the stalks harder, sweep across the field, grow, merge and fade. Large patches last longer than small ones. The rotor of a turbine, 126 m across, is wider than most of them, so at any moment some of its disc is in a gust and some in a lull.

No one can predict turbulent wind gust by gust; what can be predicted are its statistics. Turbines are designed against a standard set, IEC 61400-1, used here as NREL's program TurbSim implements it (Jonkman 2009), in four parts.

The mean. Near the ground the air is slowed by friction, so the wind blows faster higher up. The standard takes a power law in the height $z$,

$$U(z) = U_{\rm hub}\left(\frac{z}{90\ \mathrm m}\right)^{0.2}, \tag{6.5}$$

so a blade tip, rising from 27 m to 153 m every turn, meets {{n:shear_bot}} m/s at the bottom and {{n:shear_top}} m/s at the top when the hub sees 8 m/s.

The size of the gusts. At any point the wind wanders about its mean in three directions: along the shaft ($u$), across ($v$) and up ($w$). (In this section $v$ is the sideways component of the gusts; elsewhere it is the wind speed.) Their standard deviations are set by a turbulence intensity, the ratio of the gusts' standard deviation to the mean wind, here the reference value 0.16; in the standard's strongest class, A, $\sigma_u = 0.16\,(0.75\,U_{\rm hub} + 5.6\ \mathrm{m/s})$, {{n:wf_sig_u}} m/s at 8 m/s, with $\sigma_v = 0.8\sigma_u$ and $\sigma_w = 0.5\sigma_u$.

How the gusts spread over time. A gust can be a swell lasting minutes or a flicker lasting a second, and the standard says how much of each to expect.

A spectrum
feel
The sliders of a music equaliser: the same song is a little bass, a lot of middle, some treble. A gusty wind is a mixture of slow swells and quick flickers in the same way.
physics
The spectrum says how much of the variance, the mean square wobble, sits at each frequency.
measure
Record the wind at one point with an anemometer, take the Fourier transform of the record, square its size, and average over many records: the periodogram.
maths
$\sigma^2 = \int_0^\infty S(f)\,df$; the variance between $f_1$ and $f_2$ is $\int_{f_1}^{f_2}S\,df$.

The standard uses the Kaimal spectrum, fitted to measured winds,

$$S_K(f) = \frac{4\,\sigma_K^2\,L_K/U_{\rm hub}}{\left(1 + 6 f L_K/U_{\rm hub}\right)^{5/3}}, \qquad L_u = 340\ \mathrm m,\quad L_v = 113\ \mathrm m,\quad L_w = 28\ \mathrm m, \tag{6.6}$$

with $L_K$ the size of the eddies that carry most of the gusts. Below $U/(6L)$ it is flat; above, it falls as $f^{-5/3}$, Kolmogorov's law for the cascade in which large eddies break into smaller ones. Stir a cup of coffee and stop: the one big swirl breaks into smaller swirls, and those into smaller still, until the smallest are rubbed out by the liquid's viscosity and the coffee is a little warmer. Energy runs down this staircase like water down a cascade of falls, the same flow per second over every step. Between the largest eddies and the smallest, an eddy of size $1/k$ (with $k$ the wavenumber) knows nothing about how the energy was put in or where it will be removed; it knows only the rate $\varepsilon$ at which energy passes through, in watts per kilogram, m²/s³. The energy per kilogram held by eddies per unit of wavenumber, $E(k)$, in m³/s², can then be built only from $\varepsilon$ and $k$. Writing $E = C_{\rm Kol}\,\varepsilon^x k^y$ and matching metres and seconds,

$$\mathrm{m}^3\,\mathrm{s}^{-2} = \mathrm{m}^{2x}\,\mathrm{s}^{-3x}\,\mathrm{m}^{-y} \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = \tfrac23,\quad y = -\tfrac53, \qquad E(k) = C_{\rm Kol}\,\varepsilon^{2/3}k^{-5/3},$$

with $C_{\rm Kol}$ a pure number, Kolmogorov's constant. A probe fixed in the wind sees the eddies carried past at $U$ (Taylor's frozen turbulence, below), so an eddy of wavenumber $k$ shows up at the frequency $f = Uk/2\pi$; since $S(f)\,df = E(k)\,dk$ and $dk/df$ is a constant, $S(f) \propto f^{-5/3}$ as well. Eddies much larger than $L_K$ are not part of the cascade: each is made by the terrain and the weather separately, unrelated to its neighbours, and a signal whose values are unrelated beyond a short time $\tau_c$ has a spectrum that is flat below $1/\tau_c$, as the hiss of a radio is flat (the Fourier transform of a narrow correlation is broad): the flat part, below $U/(6L_K)$. Integrated, with $x = 6fL_K/U$,

$$\int_0^f S_K\,df' = \sigma_K^2\int_0^x\frac{\tfrac23\,dx'}{(1+x')^{5/3}} = \sigma_K^2\Big[1 - (1 + x)^{-2/3}\Big],$$

so half the variance lies below $(1+x)^{2/3} = 2$, $f_{1/2} = (2^{3/2} - 1)\,U/(6L_K)$: at 8 m/s, {{n:wf_fhalf}} Hz for $u$, swells longer than {{n:wf_thalf}} s. The gusts along the shaft mostly last minutes (Figure {{fig:spectrum}}).

How the gusts spread over space. The rotor is 126 m across, so it matters how far a gust reaches.

Coherence
feel
Two people standing a step apart feel the same slow swell of a gust, but not the same flicker.
physics
The coherence of two points is the share of their fluctuation at one frequency that they have in common: 1 moving together, 0 unrelated. Big, slow eddies cover both points; small, quick ones do not.
measure
Two anemometers: from the Fourier transforms $X$, $Y$ of their records, averaged over many records, $\mathrm{Re}\langle XY^*\rangle/\sqrt{\langle|X|^2\rangle\langle|Y|^2\rangle}$ in each frequency band.
maths
$\mathrm{Coh}(r,f)$ falls from 1 at $r = 0$; for eddies drifting at $U$, roughly $\exp(-\text{const}\cdot fr/U)$, distance in units of the eddy's length $U/f$.

The standard gives it for $u$,

$$\mathrm{Coh}_u(r, f) = \exp\left[-12\sqrt{\left(\frac{f r}{U_{\rm hub}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{0.12\,r}{340\ \mathrm m}\right)^2}\,\right]. \tag{6.7}$$

The first term under the root is the eddy-length argument; the second keeps the slowest gusts from being perfectly shared. The standard does not specify $v$ and $w$, and TurbSim's IEC model leaves them unrelated from point to point; these notes give them TurbSim's smooth-model default, $\exp(-0.75\,f r\,\mathrm{s/m})$. The three components are independent of each other.

Making a field with these statistics. Put 121 points on a square grid over the disc, 12.6 m apart. At one frequency $f_n$, the 121 records' parts at that frequency form a random vector of known covariance, $S(f_n)\,\Delta f$ times the coherence matrix $C_{pq} = \mathrm{Coh}(r_{pq}, f_n)$. Factor $C = HH^{\mathsf T}$ (the Cholesky factorization: $H$ lower triangular, which exists because a covariance matrix is symmetric and positive definite). Draw independent complex numbers $\xi_q = (X_q + iY_q)/\sqrt2$, with $X_q$ and $Y_q$ independent standard normal numbers, so that $\langle\xi_q\rangle = 0$ and $\langle|\xi_q|^2\rangle = 1$: a random size and a uniformly random phase. Then

$$\big\langle (H\xi)(H\xi)^\dagger\big\rangle = H\,\langle\xi\xi^\dagger\rangle\,H^{\mathsf T} = HH^{\mathsf T} = C,$$

exactly the covariance wanted. Summing over the frequencies gives the field (Veers 1988):

$$u_p(t) = \mathrm{Re}\sum_{n} \sqrt{2\,S(f_n)\,\Delta f}\;\sum_{q\le p} H_{pq}(f_n)\,\xi_{qn}\,e^{2\pi i f_n t}. \tag{6.8}$$

The factor 2 under the root is there because the real part keeps half of the mean square: $\mathrm{Re}\,[X e^{2\pi i ft}]$ with $\langle|X|^2\rangle = 2S\,\Delta f$ has, averaged over time, the variance $\tfrac12\cdot 2S\,\Delta f = S\,\Delta f$, the spectrum's share of the band $\Delta f$. The record is 256 s long, $f_n = n/(256\ \mathrm s)$ for $n = 1$ to 255, stored every 0.5 s, and repeats after 256 s. Its 255 frequencies carry {{n:wf_band_pct}} % of the variance of (6.6) for $u$ at 8 m/s, the rest lying in swells slower than the record; so each spectrum is scaled up over those frequencies until its variance is $\sigma_K^2$, and that is the $S$ of (6.8). (TurbSim reaches the same end by rescaling the series it has made.) Upstream, the field is drawn with Taylor's frozen turbulence (Taylor 1938): eddies drift past faster than they change, so $u(-x, z, t) = u(0, z, t + x/U(z))$.

The wind averaged over the disc, which turns the rotor (section 6.5), has the variance {{n:wf_var_eff}} m²/s², against {{n:wf_var_chi}} m²/s² from the averaging over a continuous disc that the gust runs of section 6.5 use; the grid's 81 points on the disc are a coarser average.

6.5 What the rotor makes of it

A heavy flywheel turned by a hand that pushes unevenly turns almost evenly: the flywheel smooths the pushes. The turbine's rotor is such a flywheel. At 8 m/s it carries, as kinetic energy, about {{n:store_s}} s of its own output, so a gust lasting a second barely changes its speed; only changes that last tens of seconds do.

The rotor and main generator have $J_{\rm rot} = 4.38\times10^7$ kg m², and

$$J_{\rm rot}\dot\Omega = Q_{\rm aero} - Q_{\rm gen}(\Omega), \qquad Q_{\rm aero} = \frac{P}{\Omega} = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^3\,v^2\,\frac{C_p(\lambda)}{\lambda},$$

with $C_p(\lambda)$ from NREL's ROSCO table and $v$ the wind averaged over the disc, $v_{\rm eff}$. (That table peaks at $C_p$ = 0.466, at $\lambda$ = 7.64, a little below the 0.482 at 7.55 of section 6.2, which Jonkman et al. 2009 report for the same rotor from a separate calculation. A power coefficient is flat near its peak, so the two best ratios describe the same broad top of the curve.) The control sets the main generator's torque from the rotor's speed alone, the torque law, mainly $Q_{\rm gen} = k\Omega^2$. At a fixed tip-speed ratio $\lambda$, put $v = \Omega r_{\rm rot}/\lambda$ into $Q_{\rm aero}$:

$$Q_{\rm aero} = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^5\,\frac{C_p(\lambda)}{\lambda^3}\,\Omega^2,$$

a square law too. So the choice $k = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^5\,C_p(\lambda^*)/\lambda^{*3}$ balances the wind at the best ratio in every wind. The balance is stable. Near the peak of $C_p$, the factor $C_p/\lambda^3$ falls as $\lambda$ grows (its slope there is $-3C_p/\lambda^4$, since $C_p$ itself is flat). A rotor that runs a little too fast for its wind has a larger $\lambda$, so the wind's torque falls below $k\Omega^2$ and the rotor slows; one that runs too slowly speeds up. The rotor settles at $\lambda^*$ by itself. That is Part I's rule $\Omega = \lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$. Below 8.98 rpm the torque ramps down to zero at 6.9 rpm, so the turbine starts and stops gently (Figure {{fig:torque}}).

Part I uses $\lambda^* = 7.55$ throughout. The two other ratios that follow, 7.64 and {{n:lam_k2}}, belong to this section alone: the first is the peak of the table used here, the second the ratio at which NREL's square law balances that table.

Two corrections to Part I follow. At a steady 8 m/s the two torques balance at {{n:rpm_ctrl_8}} rpm, {{n:ctrl_vs_lam}} % below $\lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$, because NREL set the square law's constant to balance at $\lambda$ = {{n:lam_k2}} rather than at the peak of this table; that raises the second blade's power by about one per cent. At 6 m/s the rule of Part I would put the rotor at 6.87 rpm, below the ramp's start, where the main generator gives no torque at all. So the rotor runs faster, at {{n:rpm_real_6}} rpm ($\lambda$ = {{n:lam_real_6}}), and the second blade, turning faster, is pushed less hard by gravity against its spring ($G \propto 1/\Omega^2$) and gives {{n:Pst_real_6}} mW instead of the {{n:Pst_6}} mW of (3.5), a change of {{n:Preal_pct_6}} %.

The gusts reach the second blade in two ways. The Euler torque of (1.6), while the rotor changes speed, changes the power by about {{n:l2_euler_parts}} parts in 10 000 (measured in the runs described below, by solving each one with and without that term): nothing. More important, the rotor's speed itself changes, over tens of seconds, while the second blade settles in about a second (section 4.1): at every moment it swings as in a steady wind at that moment's speed. The brake $c$ stays fixed, because the load's resistance does not change with the wind. So put $\kappa = \kappa^*\Omega_0/\Omega$ and $G = G_0\Omega_0^2/\Omega^2$ into (3.3), with $\kappa^* = R - 1$ at $\Omega_0$:

$$\bar P(\Omega) = I\Omega^3\,\frac{(R-1)\frac{\Omega_0}{\Omega}\,G_0^2\frac{\Omega_0^4}{\Omega^4}}{2(R-1)^2\big(1 + \frac{\Omega_0^2}{\Omega^2}\big)} = \frac{I\,G_0^2\,\Omega_0^5}{2(R-1)\,(\Omega^2 + \Omega_0^2)},$$

and divided by its value at $\Omega_0$,

$$\frac{\bar P(\Omega)}{\bar P(\Omega_0)} = \frac{2}{1 + \Omega^2/\Omega_0^2}. \tag{6.9}$$

With $x = \Omega/\Omega_0$: $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{2}{1+x^2} = -1$ and $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\frac{2}{1+x^2} = +1$ at $x = 1$. So a shift of the mean speed by a fraction $\Delta$ changes the power by $-\Delta$, and a speed that wanders about a fixed mean with a relative spread $s$ raises it by $\tfrac12 s^2$ whichever way it wanders (the average of a curve that bends up lies above its value at the average).

To measure both, the wander and the shift of the mean, the full equation of the second blade was run through {{n:l2_records}} ten-minute records of the disc-averaged wind at a mean of 8 m/s, synthesised from the spectrum (6.6), scaled to its band as in section 6.4 and averaged over a continuous disc with the coherence (6.7). The rotor's speed came from its own equation. Two torque laws were tried: a pure square law, and the turbine's real one, which has a kink where the ramp of low speeds begins.

On our blade
Under the turbine's real torque law, the speed wanders with a standard deviation of {{n:sigOm}} % of its mean, and the wander alone adds {{n:jensen}} % by the curvature of (6.9). With a pure square law the speed wanders more, by {{n:sigOm_k2}} %, and the second blade's power changes by {{n:l2_k2_tot}} % against steady operation. Of that, the wander adds {{n:l2_k2_wnd}} %, exactly the curvature's $\tfrac12s^2$ = {{n:jensen_k2}} %; the mean speed moves by {{n:l2_k2_dOm}} % and changes the power by {{n:l2_k2_msh}} %, as the slope of $-1$ predicts. With the turbine's real torque law the total turns to {{n:l2_nrel_tot}} %. In these runs the rotor runs just above the kink of Figure {{fig:torque}}. In a lull it drops onto the ramp, where the main generator brakes much less, so it slows less than it speeds up in a gust. Its mean speed rises by {{n:l2_nrel_dOm}} %, which changes the power by {{n:l2_nrel_msh}} %, while the wander still adds {{n:l2_nrel_wnd}} %.

The field of section 6.4 tells the same story. In its first record at 8 m/s, class A, the rotor's speed wanders by {{n:wf_om_sd}} % and the second blade gives {{n:wf_P_none}} mW over 256 s, {{n:wf_P_none_pct}} % below the {{n:wf_P_calm}} mW the same solver gives in the steady wind with its shear and no gusts. (There the shear lowers the wind averaged over the disc to {{n:wf_vbar8}} m/s, so the rotor turns at {{n:wf_rpm_calm8}} rpm rather than {{n:rpm_ctrl_8}}, and the slower rotor gives a little more, as (3.5) says.) Gusts change the second blade's power by a few per cent, with a sign set by the main generator's torque law, not by the wind.

What to remember
  • The wind carries the power $\tfrac12\rho Av^3$, and no turbine can take more than 16/27 of it; the best slows the wind by a third at the rotor.
  • Blades are wings, and run fastest where they work best: at 7.55 times the wind's speed at the tip. A square torque law holds them there.
  • Turbulence is described by its statistics: a mean that grows with height, the Kaimal spectrum in time (flat, then $f^{-5/3}$), an exponential coherence in space. Veers' method turns them into a field, with a Cholesky factor per frequency.
  • The rotor is a flywheel: gusts make its speed wander slowly, by about a tenth. The second blade follows the rotor's speed from moment to moment, and its power changes by a few per cent.
Problems
  1. A small turbine on a garden pole has a rotor 2 m across. How much power can it take, at most, from a wind of 6 m/s?
    AnswerIts disc has the area $\pi\times 1^2$ m², so the wind carries $\tfrac12\rho A v^3$ = {{n:p0_small_wind}} W through it (6.1). No turbine takes more than 16/27 of that (6.3): {{n:p0_small_betz}} W.
  2. Show that when a rotor runs at its best tip-speed ratio $\lambda^*$ and slows the wind by a third, the inflow angle at its tips does not depend on the wind speed. What is it for the 5 MW turbine?
    AnswerAt the tip $\Omega r_{\rm rot} = \lambda^* v$, so (6.4) gives $\tan\gamma = (1-a)/\lambda^*$: the wind speed cancels. With $a = \tfrac13$ and $\lambda^* = 7.55$, $\gamma$ = {{n:inflow8}}° in every wind in which the rotor is held at $\lambda^*$.
  3. Two points of the disc 12.6 m apart, at 8 m/s: at what frequency has the coherence (6.7) of their $u$ fallen to a half? What length of eddy is that?
    AnswerLeaving out the small second term under the root, $12fr/U = \ln 2$ gives $f = U\ln2/(12r)$ = {{n:p6_coh_f}} Hz, an eddy $U/f$ = {{n:p6_coh_L}} m long: about seventeen times the distance between the points.
