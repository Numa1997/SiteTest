7 The air on the second blade

Part I left out the air. At the tip the apparent wind is some 60 m/s, almost head-on (section 6.3); its dynamic pressure $\tfrac12\rho v_{\rm tip}^2$ is {{n:q_8}} Pa at 8 m/s; on only {{n:air_area_cm2}} cm², four fifths of a playing card, that pressure pushes as hard as the second blade weighs, $mg = {{n:mg}}$ N. This chapter puts the air back, as an estimate and then solved in time in the gusty wind, and asks: how hard it pushes in the direction that can turn the second blade, how much of that push comes round once per turn, and whether it slows the swing or feeds it. Three shapes are compared: a bare steel rod, a streamlined blade that makes no lift in the steady wind, and a small wing.

7.1 What the air can turn

Hold a flat board out of a car window at motorway speed, edge first, and it slices through the air; turn it flat and it is torn from your hand. At the blade tip the air rushes past at twice motorway speed. Which way the force points matters as much as its size.

A force along the shaft has no lever arm about the hinge's axle and only loads the hinge; only a force in the rotor's plane, across the second blade, can turn it. With $U_t$ the head wind along the blade's path and $U_a$ the wind along the shaft, the apparent wind is $W = \sqrt{U_t^2 + U_a^2}$ at $\tan\gamma = U_a/U_t$ (6.4). Lift $L = qSC_L$ is at right angles to it and drag $D = qSC_D$ along it; their parts along the path, forwards positive, add to

$$F_t = q\,S\left(C_L\,\frac{U_a}{W} - C_D\,\frac{U_t}{W}\right) = q\,S\left(C_L\sin\gamma - C_D\cos\gamma\right), \qquad q = \tfrac12\rho W^2, \tag{7.1}$$

with $S$ the area (chord times length). Since $\gamma$ is small, drag lies almost wholly in the plane and holds the second blade back. Only lift's small part $\sin\gamma$ lies in the plane, and it pushes forwards, the way the mother blade's lift turns the rotor. A steady $F_t$ acting at the middle has the torque $F_t\,\tfrac l2\cos\theta$ about the hinge. The outward pull's torque, $\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\sin\theta$, balances it when the second blade leans by

$$\tan\theta_s = \frac{F_t}{m\Omega^2r_p},$$

and the rotor, carrying it round at $\Omega r_p$, gains $F_t\Omega r_p$, or pays it if $F_t < 0$ (Figure {{fig:lean}}). Gravity's once-per-turn push then swings the second blade about this lean instead of about the mother blade's line.

7.2 What changes once per turn

A steady force only makes a steady lean. The harvest changes through the part of the air's push that comes round once per turn, and through how the lean turns gravity's own push. Two causes make the air at the tip change once per turn.

Wind shear. The wind blows faster higher up, as the power law (6.5); the tip rises from 27 m to 153 m above the ground and back every turn, so the wind it meets along the shaft swings once per turn, by $\delta u$ = {{n:air_du}} m/s at 8 m/s: the first harmonic of the power law met round the circle, $\delta u = (1-a)\cdot2\langle U(z_{\rm hub} + r_p\sin\varphi)\sin\varphi\rangle$ over a turn, slowed by a third at the rotor. That turns the inflow angle by {{n:air_dg}}°. The push changes most at the top and the bottom of the circle, a quarter turn away from where gravity's push on a straight second blade is largest.

Shaft tilt. The turbine's shaft points 5° above the horizontal, so the wind has a part $v\sin5°$ = {{n:air_dWt}} m/s lying in the rotor's plane, pointing up it. A blade climbing on one side of the circle meets less head wind, a blade falling on the other meets more: the head wind changes by $-v\sin5°\cos\varphi$, in step with gravity's push.

Either change alters $U_t$ or $U_a$ a little, and the question is how much $F_t$ follows. Write (7.1) as $F_t = \tfrac12\rho SW(C_LU_a - C_DU_t)$, using $\sin\gamma = U_a/W$, $\cos\gamma = U_t/W$, and let the lift follow the angle of attack, $\partial C_L/\partial\gamma = 2\pi$ (thin-aerofoil theory, while the flow stays attached), with $\partial\gamma/\partial U_a = U_t/W^2$ and $\partial\gamma/\partial U_t = -U_a/W^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_a} &= \tfrac12\rho S\Big[\frac{U_a}{W}(C_LU_a - C_DU_t) + W C_L + 2\pi\,\frac{U_aU_t}{W}\Big],\\ \frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t} &= \tfrac12\rho S\Big[\frac{U_t}{W}(C_LU_a - C_DU_t) - W C_D - 2\pi\,\frac{U_a^2}{W}\Big] \end{aligned}$$

The $2\pi$ terms count for the streamlined blade and the wing, aerofoils whose lift follows the angle, and not for the rod. Each change of force gives a torque $\tfrac l2\cos\theta_s$ times it.

The steady force also stiffens the spring. To see by how much, take the two steady torques about the hinge, the outward pull's and the air's, and differentiate their sum at the lean, where $F_t = m\Omega^2r_p\tan\theta_s$. The estimate takes $F_t$ as independent of the angle: the air meets the second blade from the direction of its path, whatever its lean, so only the lever arm changes.

$$-\frac{d}{d\theta}\Big[-\frac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\sin\theta + F_t\,\frac l2\cos\theta\Big]_{\theta_s} = \frac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\Big(\cos\theta_s + \frac{\sin^2\theta_s}{\cos\theta_s}\Big) = \frac{ml}{2}\,\frac{\Omega^2r_p}{\cos\theta_s}.$$

The spring becomes $R/\cos\theta_s$: the air's force and the outward pull add as arrows to a stronger field, $\Omega^2r_p/\cos\theta_s$ per kilogram, along the leaning blade.

Now collect every once-per-turn torque about the lean. Gravity's torque on the leaning blade, with $G_\tau = \tfrac{ml}{2}g$ its largest value, splits into a part with $\cos\varphi$ and a part with $\sin\varphi$:

$$-G_\tau\cos(\varphi + \theta_s) = -G_\tau\cos\theta_s\cos\varphi + G_\tau\sin\theta_s\sin\varphi.$$

The shear changes $U_a$ by $\delta u\sin\varphi$ (largest with the mother blade pointing up, $\varphi = 90°$); the tilt changes $U_t$ by $-v\sin5°\cos\varphi$. Altogether the push is $A_c\cos\varphi + A_s\sin\varphi$ with

$$\begin{aligned} A_c &= -G_\tau\cos\theta_s\ \underbrace{-\ \frac l2\cos\theta_s\,\frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,v\sin5°}_{\text{tilt}},\\ A_s &= G_\tau\sin\theta_s\ \underbrace{+\ \frac l2\cos\theta_s\,\frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_a}\,\delta u}_{\text{shear}}. \end{aligned}$$

As turning arrows (section 3.2) the two parts are a quarter turn apart, at right angles, so together they make a push of the length $\sqrt{A_c^2 + A_s^2}$, whatever its timing. By (3.5) the best power goes as the push squared over the net stiffness; gravity's push alone, on a blade that does not lean, is $G_\tau$, and so

$$\frac{\bar P_{\rm with\ air}}{\bar P} = \frac{A_c^2 + A_s^2}{G_\tau^2}\cdot\frac{R - 1}{R/\cos\theta_s - 1}. \tag{7.2}$$

A lean on its own only turns gravity's arrow, since $(G_\tau\cos\theta_s)^2 + (G_\tau\sin\theta_s)^2 = G_\tau^2$; it changes the power only through the stiffer spring, and through how the air's own pushes line up with gravity's parts.

The rod loses on both counts. Its lean of {{n:air_rod3_lean}}° stiffens the spring to $R/\cos\theta_s$, and its tilt torque, positive, shortens the $-G_\tau\cos\theta_s$ of $A_c$: the side of the circle where the blade falls meets more head wind, and so more drag, just where gravity pushes the second blade forward. The streamlined blade hardly changes. The wing gains most: its lift makes the shear's push large, and its forward lean puts gravity's $G_\tau\sin\theta_s$ in step with it, so the two add directly in $A_s$. The air can cut the harvest by a quarter or raise it by half; the shape decides which.

7.3 The air as a brake

Swinging forward, the piece at $s$ meets a head wind faster by $s\dot\theta$. Spread the force evenly along the second blade, $F_t/l$ per metre; the piece's share changes by $(\partial F_t/\partial U_t)\,s\dot\theta\,ds/l$, with the arm $s$:

$$\int_0^l s\,\frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,\frac{s\dot\theta}{l}\,ds = \frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,\frac{l^2}{3}\,\dot\theta \equiv -c_{\rm air}\,\dot\theta:$$ $$c_{\rm air} = -\frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,\frac{l^2}{3}. \tag{7.3}$$

For a draggy shape it is positive, a real brake: more head wind, more drag. For a lifting shape the lift's forward part grows with the head wind and works the other way. The numbers: {{n:air_rod3_cair}}, {{n:air_sym3_cair}} and {{n:air_wing3_cair}} N m s for the rod, the streamlined blade and the wing, against the generator's {{n:cst_8}} N m s: about one per cent for the rod, less for the others, and for the wing slightly negative.

Here the wing's negative $c_{\rm air}$ is more than a thousand times smaller than the generator's brake, so there is no flutter; a second blade with more lift and a lighter brake would need checking.

7.4 Solved in time, in the gusty wind

Formula (7.2) assumes small swings and a pure once-per-turn push. The Wind tab does without both: it solves (1.6) with the air's torque $F_t\tfrac l2\cos\theta$ added, $F_t$ from (7.1) at the wind the second blade's middle meets at that instant in the field of section 6.4 (mean wind at its height, gusts, shaft tilt, slowing by a third), the wing's lift following the angle, $C_L = 1 + 2\pi(\gamma - \gamma_0)$ ($\gamma_0$ the inflow angle in the steady wind), and the rotor turning with the torque law of section 6.5. It integrates the rotor's work on the second blade and the air's, and checks that their sum equals the change of the second blade's energy plus the harvest: over a record the books close to {{n:wf_book}}.

On our blade

With no air the second blade gives {{n:wf_calm_none}} mW, the $P^*$ of its rotor speed to {{n:wf_calm_none_err}} %. The streamlined blade gives {{n:wf_calm_sym}} mW ({{n:wf_calm_sym_pct}} %) and leans {{n:wf_calm_sym_lean}}° (more than the {{n:air_sym3_lean}}° the air alone makes in the estimate: the trail of section 4.2, {{n:lean_deg_8}}° at 8 m/s, adds to it and accounts for more than half of the difference); the small wing gives {{n:wf_calm_wing}} mW ({{n:wf_calm_wing_pct}} %) and leans {{n:wf_calm_wing_lean}}°. Then in the gusts of class A, record 1, measured over the second time round its 256 s record, the first letting the start die away: with no air, {{n:wf_P_none}} mW; streamlined, {{n:wf_P_sym}} mW; small wing, {{n:wf_P_wing}} mW. Against the steady wind, the gusts cost the blade with no air {{n:wf_none_gust_pct}} % (the rotor's wandering speed, section 6.5); the streamlined blade, whose lift follows every gust though it has none on average, gains {{n:wf_sym_gust_pct}} %; the wing gains {{n:wf_wing_gust_pct}} %.

The aerofoils gain because lift answers every gust at the tip. A change of 1 m/s in the wind speed (two thirds of it reaching the tip along the shaft) turns the inflow angle by about 0.6°. That does two things, the two lift terms of $\partial F_t/\partial U_a$: it tips the lift that is already there further forward, so a larger part of it lies in the rotor's plane (the term $WC_L$, the larger one for the wing), and it raises the angle of attack, so the lift grows by about 7 % (the $2\pi$ term, the only one for the streamlined blade, which has no lift on average). Together they make a torque about the hinge of {{n:wf_wing_dtau}} N m, {{n:wf_wing_dtau_frac}} of gravity's largest.

Why should gusts help at all? Ride with the tip. It is carried round a circle 126 m across, through a field of gust patches that drift past and change over tens of seconds, while one turn takes a few seconds. So in each turn the tip runs through nearly the same patches at the same rotor angles: a strong patch on the left of the disc is met once per turn, turn after turn, for as long as the patch lives (Figure {{fig:sampling}}). The wind the tip feels therefore repeats itself every turn; a field that is gusty in space becomes, for the tip, a push at the rotor's own rhythm. This is called rotational sampling. And once per turn is exactly the rhythm the second blade was built for: chapter 3's brake, set for gravity's once-per-turn push, harvests any other once-per-turn push with the same efficiency.

The two pushes add as turning arrows: gravity's $G_\tau$, steady, and the air's $\mathbf A_{\rm air}$, whose size and timing change from one patch to the next. The power goes as the square of the total arrow, and on average

$$\big\langle|G_\tau + \mathbf A_{\rm air}|^2\big\rangle = G_\tau^2 + 2G_\tau\langle A_\parallel\rangle + \langle|\mathbf A_{\rm air}|^2\rangle = G_\tau^2 + \langle|\mathbf A_{\rm air}|^2\rangle,$$

since a push that comes at a random time is as often with gravity's as against it, $\langle A_\parallel\rangle = 0$ ($A_\parallel$ the part of the air's arrow along gravity's). The square term survives: a random push adds power whichever way it points, as section 8.3 will picture with coffee sloshing in a bus on a rough road. It is large for the aerofoils, whose lift turns a small change of wind into a large torque, and small for a shape without lift.

7.5 What this does and does not say

The shape decides everything here: the air's steady push costs or adds power, and its once-per-turn push changes the harvest by between {{n:air_rod3_pct}} % and {{n:air_wing3_pct}} % (up to {{n:air_wing0_pct}} % for the wing if the air at the tip is not slowed), as the solution in time confirms for the streamlined blade and the wing in the slowed air. Both aerofoils harvest the gusts, the wing far more; in steady wind a streamlined one barely notices the air, and Part I holds for it. Left out: the slower air in front of the tower, stall at larger angles (the flow tearing away from the wing's upper side, so that lift stops growing with the angle and drops), the delay with which lift follows a change of angle, and the true flow at the rotor's edge. Those need a model of a real design.

What to remember
  • Only the air's force in the rotor's plane, across the second blade, can swing it. Drag lies almost wholly in that plane and holds the second blade back; lift lies almost along the shaft, and only its small in-plane part pushes it forward.
  • For a bare rod or a lifting wing the steady air torque is larger than gravity's. A bare rod leans back about 31° and costs the turbine about 2 kW; a streamlined blade costs about 135 W; a small wing gives about 1 kW.
  • Through the once-per-turn push and the lean, the air changes the harvest by between {{n:air_rod3_pct}} % (rod) and {{n:air_wing3_pct}} % (wing), and by more for a wing if the air at the tip is less slowed.
  • The air barely damps the swing; for a lifting shape it even feeds it a little.
  • Solved in time in the gusty field, the estimate holds in steady wind; gusts add little for a streamlined second blade and a great deal for a lifting one.
Problems
  1. Why can a force along the rotor's shaft, however large, not swing the second blade?
    AnswerThe hinge's axle points along the shaft. A force parallel to an axle has no lever arm about it, so its torque about the hinge is zero; it only presses on the hinge's bearings.
  2. A streamlined second blade has twice the chord, 0.2 m. How do its drag, its steady lean and the power it costs the rotor change?
    AnswerThe drag and the cost are proportional to the area at the same coefficients: the drag doubles to about 4.5 N and the cost to about 270 W. The lean, $\arctan(F_t/m\Omega^2r_p)$, roughly doubles to about 4.4°, still small.
  3. Why does a forward lean make the wind shear's push add to the harvest more than it would on a straight second blade?
    AnswerOn a straight second blade gravity's push is largest with the blade level, a quarter turn from the shear's, and two pushes a quarter turn apart add like the sides of a right-angled triangle: the small one barely lengthens the large one. Leaning forward by $\theta_s$ turns part of gravity's push, $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\sin\theta_s$, in step with the shear's, and pushes in step add directly.
