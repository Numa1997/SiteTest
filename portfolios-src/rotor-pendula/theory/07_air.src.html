7 The air on the second blade

Part I left out the air that rushes past the second blade, and section 6.3 showed how fast it rushes: at the tip the apparent wind is some 60 m/s, almost head-on. Its pressure, $\tfrac12\rho v_{\rm tip}^2$, is {{n:q_8}} Pa at 8 m/s, so it matches the second blade's weight, $mg = {{n:mg}}$ N, on an area of only {{n:air_area_cm2}} cm², four fifths of a playing card. A real second blade is far larger than that. This chapter puts the air back, first as an estimate, then solved in time in the gusty wind of chapter 6. It asks three questions: how hard the air pushes the second blade in the direction that can turn it, how much of that push changes once per turn, and whether the air slows the swing or feeds it. The answers depend on the second blade's shape, so three shapes are compared: a bare steel rod, a streamlined blade that makes no lift, and a small wing.

7.1 What the air can turn

Hold a flat board out of the window of a car at motorway speed, edge first, and it slices through the air; turn it flat to the air and it is torn from your hand. At the tip of the turbine's blade the air rushes past at twice motorway speed and more. Which way the force points matters as much as how large it is.

The hinge's axle points along the rotor's shaft. A force along the shaft, whatever its size, has no lever arm about that axle and cannot swing the second blade; it only loads the hinge. Only a force in the rotor's plane, across the second blade, can turn it. Split the air's force on the second blade as in section 6.3: the lift $L$, at right angles to the apparent wind, and the drag $D$, along it. Let $U_t$ be the head wind along the blade's path and $U_a$ the wind along the shaft, so that the apparent wind has the speed $W = \sqrt{U_t^2 + U_a^2}$ and comes in at the inflow angle $\gamma$, $\tan\gamma = U_a/U_t$ (6.4). The force along the path, positive forwards, is

$$F_t = q\,S\left(C_L\,\frac{U_a}{W} - C_D\,\frac{U_t}{W}\right) = q\,S\left(C_L\sin\gamma - C_D\cos\gamma\right), \qquad q = \tfrac12\rho W^2. \tag{7.1}$$

$q$ is the pressure of the apparent wind, $S$ the second blade's area (chord times length), and $C_L$ and $C_D$ its lift and drag coefficients, the lift and drag in units of $qS$. Because $\gamma$ is small, the two parts behave very differently. Drag points along the apparent wind, so almost all of it, $\cos\gamma$, lies in the plane and holds the second blade back. Lift points almost along the shaft; only its small part $\sin\gamma$ lies in the plane, and it pushes forwards. That is how the mother blade turns the rotor (section 6.3).

A steady force $F_t$ at the middle of the second blade has the lever arm $\tfrac l2\cos\theta$ when the second blade leans by $\theta$; the outward pull answers with $\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\sin\theta$ (2.3). So the second blade settles at a steady lean $\theta_s$ with

$$\tan\theta_s = \frac{F_t}{m\Omega^2r_p}.$$

The rotor, which carries the second blade round at the speed $\Omega r_p$, gains the power $F_t\Omega r_p$ from it, or pays that power if $F_t$ is negative.

One assumption here is soft. The second blade sits at the very edge of the rotor's disc, from 63 to 64 m, where the air is not slowed by the disc's average third: near the tips the flow curls round into the tip vortex, and outside the disc the wind is hardly slowed at all. The truth lies between, and only a model of the flow at the tip can say where.

7.2 What changes once per turn

A steady force only makes a steady lean. What changes the harvest is the part of the air's push that changes once per turn, as gravity's does, and, through the lean, how gravity's own push meets the second blade. Two causes make the air at the tip change once per turn.

Wind shear. The wind blows faster higher up, as the power law (6.5); the tip rises from 27 m to 153 m above the ground and back every turn, so the wind it meets along the shaft swings once per turn, by $\delta u$ = {{n:air_du}} m/s at 8 m/s (the first harmonic, slowed by a third at the rotor). That turns the inflow angle by {{n:air_dg}}°. The push changes most at the top and the bottom of the circle, a quarter turn away from where gravity's push on a straight second blade is largest.

Shaft tilt. The turbine's shaft points 5° above the horizontal, so the wind has a part $v\sin5°$ = {{n:air_dWt}} m/s lying in the rotor's plane, pointing up it. A blade climbing on one side of the circle meets less head wind, a blade falling on the other meets more: the head wind changes by $-v\sin5°\cos\varphi$, in step with gravity's push.

Either change alters $U_t$ or $U_a$ a little, and (7.1) changes with them. With $dC_L/d\alpha = 2\pi$ per radian, the lift's growth with the angle of attack from thin-aerofoil theory (valid while the air flows smoothly round the section), the derivatives $\partial F_t/\partial U_a$ and $\partial F_t/\partial U_t$ follow from (7.1). Each gives a torque $\tfrac l2\cos\theta_s$ times the force's change.

The lean matters too. Gravity's torque on a second blade leaning by $\theta_s$ is $-\tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi + \theta_s)$ (1.6), which has a part $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\sin\theta_s\sin\varphi$ in step with the shear's push. And the steady force adds to the outward pull: the two make a field $\Omega^2r_p/\cos\theta_s$, so the centrifugal stiffness becomes $R/\cos\theta_s$. For small swings about the lean, the best power goes as the push squared over the net stiffness (3.5). With $c$ and $s$ the amplitudes of all the once-per-turn torques in step with $\cos\varphi$ and $\sin\varphi$, and $G_\tau = \tfrac{ml}{2}g$,

$$\frac{\bar P_{\rm with\ air}}{\bar P} = \frac{c^2 + s^2}{G_\tau^2}\cdot\frac{R - 1}{R/\cos\theta_s - 1}. \tag{7.2}$$

The rod loses because its lean turns gravity's push away and its drag's tilt term works against gravity. The streamlined blade hardly changes. The wing gains most, for two reasons that point the same way: its forward lean puts part of gravity's push in step with the shear's, and its lift makes the shear's push large. So the air can cut the harvest by a quarter or raise it by half, and more if the air at the tip is less slowed; the shape decides which.

7.3 Does the air slow the swing?

When the second blade swings forward, each piece of it at the distance $s$ from the hinge meets a head wind faster by $s\dot\theta$. The force (7.1) changes by $\partial F_t/\partial U_t$ per unit of that speed, so, summed along the second blade, the air acts on the swing like a brake of constant

$$c_{\rm air} = -\frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,\frac{l^2}{3}. \tag{7.3}$$

For a draggy shape it is positive, a real brake: more head wind, more drag. For a lifting shape the lift's forward part grows with the head wind too and works the other way. The numbers: {{n:air_rod3_cair}}, {{n:air_sym3_cair}} and {{n:air_wing3_cair}} N m s for the rod, the streamlined blade and the wing, against the generator's best brake of {{n:cst_8}} N m s. The air brakes the rod and the streamlined blade by about one per cent of what the generator does, and it feeds the wing's swing very slightly. Next to the generator's brake, both are negligible; but a negative air brake is how flutter starts, and a second blade with less generator brake and more lift would need checking for it.

7.4 Solved in time, in the gusty wind

Formula (7.2) is an estimate: small swings about the lean, a push that is a pure once-per-turn wave. The Wind tab does without both. It solves (1.6) with the air's torque $F_t\tfrac l2\cos\theta$ added, $F_t$ from (7.1) at the wind the second blade's middle meets at that instant, taken from the field of section 6.4: the mean wind at its height, the gusts there, the shaft's tilt and the slowing by a third. 7.5 What this does and does not say

The air's steady push on the second blade is larger than gravity's for a draggy or a lifting shape, and the shape decides whether that push costs the turbine power or adds some. Its once-per-turn push, with the lean it causes, changes the harvest by between {{n:air_rod3_pct}} % and {{n:air_wing3_pct}} % for the shapes tried (up to {{n:air_wing0_pct}} % for the wing if the air at the tip is not slowed), and the solution in time agrees. In gusty wind a lifting second blade harvests the gusts too, and gains much more; a streamlined one is barely affected, and the mechanics of Part I holds for it. The air barely damps the swing. What a real second blade would do is not settled here. The model leaves out the slower air in front of the tower, which the blade passes once per turn; air that stops flowing smoothly round the section (stall) at larger angles; the delay with which lift follows a change in angle; and the true flow at the rotor's edge. Those need a model of a real design.

What to remember
  • Only the air's force in the rotor's plane, across the second blade, can swing it. Drag lies almost wholly in that plane and holds the second blade back; lift lies almost along the shaft, and only its small in-plane part pushes it forward.
  • For a bare rod or a lifting wing the steady air torque is larger than gravity's. A bare rod leans back about 31° and costs the turbine about 2 kW; a streamlined blade costs about 135 W; a small wing gives about 1 kW.
  • Through the once-per-turn push and the lean, the air changes the harvest by between {{n:air_rod3_pct}} % (rod) and {{n:air_wing3_pct}} % (wing), and by more for a wing if the air at the tip is less slowed.
  • The air barely damps the swing; for a lifting shape it even feeds it a little.
  • Solved in time in the gusty field, the estimate holds in steady wind; gusts add little for a streamlined second blade and a great deal for a lifting one.
Problems
  1. Why can a force along the rotor's shaft, however large, not swing the second blade?
    AnswerThe hinge's axle points along the shaft. A force parallel to an axle has no lever arm about it, so its torque about the hinge is zero; it only presses on the hinge's bearings.
  2. A streamlined second blade has twice the chord, 0.2 m. How do its drag, its steady lean and the power it costs the rotor change?
    AnswerThe drag and the cost are proportional to the area at the same coefficients: the drag doubles to about 4.5 N and the cost to about 270 W. The lean, $\arctan(F_t/m\Omega^2r_p)$, roughly doubles to about 4.4°, still small.
  3. Why does a forward lean make the wind shear's push add to the harvest more than it would on a straight second blade?
    AnswerOn a straight second blade gravity's push is largest with the blade level, a quarter turn from the shear's, and two pushes a quarter turn apart add like the sides of a right-angled triangle: the small one barely lengthens the large one. Leaning forward by $\theta_s$ turns part of gravity's push, $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\sin\theta_s$, in step with the shear's, and pushes in step add directly.
