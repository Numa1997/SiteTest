7 The air on the second blade

Part I left out the air. At the tip the apparent wind is some 60 m/s, almost head-on (section 6.3); its pressure $\tfrac12\rho v_{\rm tip}^2$ = {{n:q_8}} Pa at 8 m/s matches the second blade's weight, $mg = {{n:mg}}$ N, on only {{n:air_area_cm2}} cm², four fifths of a playing card. This chapter puts the air back, as an estimate and then solved in time in the gusty wind, and asks: how hard it pushes in the direction that can turn the second blade, how much of that push comes round once per turn, and whether it slows the swing or feeds it. Three shapes are compared: a bare steel rod, a streamlined blade that makes no lift in the steady wind, and a small wing.

7.1 What the air can turn

Hold a flat board out of a car window at motorway speed, edge first, and it slices through the air; turn it flat and it is torn from your hand. At the blade tip the air rushes past at twice motorway speed. Which way the force points matters as much as its size.

A force along the shaft has no lever arm about the hinge's axle and only loads the hinge; only a force in the rotor's plane, across the second blade, can turn it. With $U_t$ the head wind along the blade's path and $U_a$ the wind along the shaft, the apparent wind is $W = \sqrt{U_t^2 + U_a^2}$ at $\tan\gamma = U_a/U_t$ (6.4). Lift $L = qSC_L$ is at right angles to it and drag $D = qSC_D$ along it; their parts along the path, forwards positive, add to

$$F_t = q\,S\left(C_L\,\frac{U_a}{W} - C_D\,\frac{U_t}{W}\right) = q\,S\left(C_L\sin\gamma - C_D\cos\gamma\right), \qquad q = \tfrac12\rho W^2, \tag{7.1}$$

with $S$ the area (chord times length). Since $\gamma$ is small, drag lies almost wholly in the plane and holds the second blade back. Only lift's small part $\sin\gamma$ lies in the plane, and it pushes forwards, the way the mother blade's lift turns the rotor. A steady $F_t$ acting at the middle has the torque $F_t\,\tfrac l2\cos\theta$ about the hinge. The outward pull's torque, $\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\sin\theta$, balances it when the second blade leans by

$$\tan\theta_s = \frac{F_t}{m\Omega^2r_p},$$

and the rotor, carrying it round at $\Omega r_p$, gains $F_t\Omega r_p$, or pays it if $F_t < 0$ (Figure {{fig:lean}}). Gravity's once-per-turn push then swings the second blade about this lean instead of about the mother blade's line.

7.2 What changes once per turn

A steady force only makes a steady lean. The harvest changes through the part of the air's push that comes round once per turn, and through how the lean turns gravity's own push. Two causes make the air at the tip change once per turn.

Wind shear. The wind blows faster higher up, as the power law (6.5); the tip rises from 27 m to 153 m above the ground and back every turn, so the wind it meets along the shaft swings once per turn, by $\delta u$ = {{n:air_du}} m/s at 8 m/s (the first harmonic, slowed by a third at the rotor). That turns the inflow angle by {{n:air_dg}}°. The push changes most at the top and the bottom of the circle, a quarter turn away from where gravity's push on a straight second blade is largest.

Shaft tilt. The turbine's shaft points 5° above the horizontal, so the wind has a part $v\sin5°$ = {{n:air_dWt}} m/s lying in the rotor's plane, pointing up it. A blade climbing on one side of the circle meets less head wind, a blade falling on the other meets more: the head wind changes by $-v\sin5°\cos\varphi$, in step with gravity's push.

Either change alters $U_t$ or $U_a$ a little, and the question is how much $F_t$ follows. Write (7.1) as $F_t = \tfrac12\rho SW(C_LU_a - C_DU_t)$, using $\sin\gamma = U_a/W$, $\cos\gamma = U_t/W$, and let the lift follow the angle of attack, $\partial C_L/\partial\gamma = 2\pi$ (thin-aerofoil theory, while the flow stays attached), with $\partial\gamma/\partial U_a = U_t/W^2$ and $\partial\gamma/\partial U_t = -U_a/W^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_a} &= \tfrac12\rho S\Big[\frac{U_a}{W}(C_LU_a - C_DU_t) + W C_L + 2\pi\,\frac{U_aU_t}{W}\Big],\\ \frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t} &= \tfrac12\rho S\Big[\frac{U_t}{W}(C_LU_a - C_DU_t) - W C_D - 2\pi\,\frac{U_a^2}{W}\Big] \end{aligned}$$

The $2\pi$ terms count for the streamlined blade and the wing, aerofoils whose lift follows the angle, and not for the rod. Each change of force gives a torque $\tfrac l2\cos\theta_s$ times it. The lean matters too: gravity's torque on a leaning second blade, $-\tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi + \theta_s)$, has a part $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\sin\theta_s\sin\varphi$ in step with the shear's push, and the steady force stiffens the spring. To see by how much, take the two steady torques about the hinge, the outward pull's and the air's, and differentiate their sum at the lean, where $F_t = m\Omega^2r_p\tan\theta_s$:

$$-\frac{d}{d\theta}\Big[-\frac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\sin\theta + F_t\,\frac l2\cos\theta\Big]_{\theta_s} = \frac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\Big(\cos\theta_s + \frac{\sin^2\theta_s}{\cos\theta_s}\Big) = \frac{ml}{2}\,\frac{\Omega^2r_p}{\cos\theta_s}.$$

The spring becomes $R/\cos\theta_s$: the air's force and the outward pull add as arrows to a stronger field, $\Omega^2r_p/\cos\theta_s$ per kilogram, along the leaning blade. By (3.5) the best power goes as the push squared over the net stiffness. Write all the once-per-turn torques together as $A_c\cos\varphi + A_s\sin\varphi$. As turning arrows (section 3.2) the two parts are a quarter turn apart, at right angles, so the push they make together has the length $\sqrt{A_c^2 + A_s^2}$, whatever its timing. With $G_\tau = \tfrac{ml}{2}g$, gravity's push alone,

$$\frac{\bar P_{\rm with\ air}}{\bar P} = \frac{A_c^2 + A_s^2}{G_\tau^2}\cdot\frac{R - 1}{R/\cos\theta_s - 1}. \tag{7.2}$$

The streamlined blade hardly changes. The wing gains most: its forward lean puts part of gravity's push in step with the shear's, and its lift makes the shear's push large. The air can cut the harvest by a quarter or raise it by half; the shape decides which.

7.3 The air as a brake

Swinging forward, the piece at $s$ meets a head wind faster by $s\dot\theta$. Spread the force evenly along the second blade, $F_t/l$ per metre; the piece's share changes by $(\partial F_t/\partial U_t)\,s\dot\theta\,ds/l$, with the arm $s$:

$$\int_0^l s\,\frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,\frac{s\dot\theta}{l}\,ds = \frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,\frac{l^2}{3}\,\dot\theta \equiv -c_{\rm air}\,\dot\theta:$$ $$c_{\rm air} = -\frac{\partial F_t}{\partial U_t}\,\frac{l^2}{3}. \tag{7.3}$$

For a draggy shape it is positive, a real brake: more head wind, more drag. For a lifting shape the lift's forward part grows with the head wind and works the other way. The numbers: {{n:air_rod3_cair}}, {{n:air_sym3_cair}} and {{n:air_wing3_cair}} N m s for the rod, the streamlined blade and the wing, against the generator's {{n:cst_8}} N m s: about one per cent for the rod, less for the others, and for the wing slightly negative.

Here the wing's negative $c_{\rm air}$ is more than a thousand times smaller than the generator's brake, so there is no flutter; a second blade with more lift and a lighter brake would need checking.

7.4 Solved in time, in the gusty wind

Formula (7.2) assumes small swings and a pure once-per-turn push. The Wind tab does without both: it solves (1.6) with the air's torque $F_t\tfrac l2\cos\theta$ added, $F_t$ from (7.1) at the wind the second blade's middle meets at that instant in the field of section 6.4 (mean wind at its height, gusts, shaft tilt, slowing by a third), the wing's lift following the angle, $C_L = 1 + 2\pi(\gamma - \gamma_0)$, and the rotor turning with the torque law of section 6.5. It integrates the rotor's work on the second blade and the air's, and checks that their sum equals the change of the second blade's energy plus the harvest: over a record the books close to {{n:wf_book}}.

The aerofoils gain because lift answers every gust at the tip. A change of 1 m/s in the wind speed (two thirds of it reaching the tip along the shaft) turns the inflow angle by about 0.6° and the lift by about 7 %, a torque about the hinge of {{n:wf_wing_dtau}} N m, {{n:wf_wing_dtau_frac}} of gravity's largest. The tip sweeps through the disc's gusts once per turn, so the field it meets changes as fast as the second blade turns round, and the brake harvests part of that.

7.5 What this does and does not say

The shape decides everything here: the air's steady push costs or adds power, and its once-per-turn push changes the harvest by between {{n:air_rod3_pct}} % and {{n:air_wing3_pct}} % (up to {{n:air_wing0_pct}} % for the wing if the air at the tip is not slowed), as the solution in time confirms for the streamlined blade and the wing in the slowed air. Both aerofoils harvest the gusts, the wing far more; in steady wind a streamlined one barely notices the air, and Part I holds for it. Left out: the slower air in front of the tower, stall at larger angles (the flow tearing away from the wing's upper side, so that lift stops growing with the angle and drops), the delay with which lift follows a change of angle, and the true flow at the rotor's edge. Those need a model of a real design.

What to remember
  • Only the air's force in the rotor's plane, across the second blade, can swing it. Drag lies almost wholly in that plane and holds the second blade back; lift lies almost along the shaft, and only its small in-plane part pushes it forward.
  • For a bare rod or a lifting wing the steady air torque is larger than gravity's. A bare rod leans back about 31° and costs the turbine about 2 kW; a streamlined blade costs about 135 W; a small wing gives about 1 kW.
  • Through the once-per-turn push and the lean, the air changes the harvest by between {{n:air_rod3_pct}} % (rod) and {{n:air_wing3_pct}} % (wing), and by more for a wing if the air at the tip is less slowed.
  • The air barely damps the swing; for a lifting shape it even feeds it a little.
  • Solved in time in the gusty field, the estimate holds in steady wind; gusts add little for a streamlined second blade and a great deal for a lifting one.
Problems
  1. Why can a force along the rotor's shaft, however large, not swing the second blade?
    AnswerThe hinge's axle points along the shaft. A force parallel to an axle has no lever arm about it, so its torque about the hinge is zero; it only presses on the hinge's bearings.
  2. A streamlined second blade has twice the chord, 0.2 m. How do its drag, its steady lean and the power it costs the rotor change?
    AnswerThe drag and the cost are proportional to the area at the same coefficients: the drag doubles to about 4.5 N and the cost to about 270 W. The lean, $\arctan(F_t/m\Omega^2r_p)$, roughly doubles to about 4.4°, still small.
  3. Why does a forward lean make the wind shear's push add to the harvest more than it would on a straight second blade?
    AnswerOn a straight second blade gravity's push is largest with the blade level, a quarter turn from the shear's, and two pushes a quarter turn apart add like the sides of a right-angled triangle: the small one barely lengthens the large one. Leaning forward by $\theta_s$ turns part of gravity's push, $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\sin\theta_s$, in step with the shear's, and pushes in step add directly.
