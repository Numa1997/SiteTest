1 Sitting on the blade

Before any equation, ride along. Almost everything the pendulum does follows from two pulls it feels in the blade's own frame: one outwards, one that turns round.

1.1 Two pulls

Sit at the rim of a fast Ferris wheel. You are pressed outwards, away from the hub, and the faster the wheel turns the harder you are pressed. Gravity still pulls you towards the ground, but the wheel carries you round, so as seen from your seat "down" sweeps round once per turn: outwards at the bottom, towards the hub at the top, sideways in between.

A turbine rotor turns in a vertical plane. A pivot fixed at distance $r_p$ from the hub moves on a circle at angular speed $\Omega$. Seen from the blade, a mass there feels a centrifugal pull $\Omega^2 r_p$ per kilogram, straight out along the blade, and gravity $g$ per kilogram, whose direction turns once per turn. The ratio of the two,

$$\Gamma = \frac{\Omega^2 r_p}{g},$$

says which wins. On Sol's mountings $\Gamma$ runs from {{n:Gam_min}} (a 67 m rotor at 8 rpm, pivot at 30 %) to {{n:Gam_max}} (a 1.3 m rotor at 400 rpm, pivot at 90 %): the outward pull always wins, from barely to overwhelmingly. Add the two pulls and you get an apparent gravity that points roughly out along the blade and tilts to one side, then to the other, once per turn, by up to $\arcsin(1/\Gamma)$, which is about $1/\Gamma$ radian when $\Gamma$ is large: {{n:tilt_min_deg}}° on the fastest mounting, but up to {{n:tilt_max_deg}}° on the slowest. A rod hung from the pivot behaves like a weathervane in that apparent wind: it lies along the blade and leans with the tilt.

In a turning frame there are two more apparent forces. The Coriolis force acts on anything moving relative to the blade; the bob moves at right angles to its rod, so the Coriolis force points along the rod and has no lever arm about the pivot: it cannot turn the rod. The Euler force appears only while the rotor speeds up or slows down; it is kept in the equations below and studied in chapter 5.

1.2 Coordinates

Put the hub at the origin, with $y$ pointing up. The rotor's angle is $\varphi(t)$, so the pivot sits at $r_p(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$. A bob of mass $m$ hangs on a massless rigid rod of length $L$; let $\theta$ be the rod's angle from the blade's outward direction, so the rod points along the absolute angle $\varphi + \theta$. The bob is at

$$x = r_p\cos\varphi + L\cos(\varphi+\theta), \qquad y = r_p\sin\varphi + L\sin(\varphi+\theta). \tag{1.1}$$

The rotor's motion $\varphi(t)$ is imposed from outside: the rotor is enormously heavier than the bob, so the bob cannot change it noticeably. We treat $\varphi(t)$ as a given function of time, not as a second unknown, and do not yet say what it is, so the same equations will hold later when gusts make the rotor speed up and slow down.

1.3 The Lagrangian and the generator

Why Lagrange and not Newton? Newton would need the force in the rod and the force at the pivot, which we do not know and do not want. Lagrange's method avoids them. Write the kinetic energy $T$ and the potential energy $V$ in terms of the one angle the bob is free to move through, $\theta$, and form $\mathcal L = T - V$. Then Lagrange's equation, $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\theta} = Q$, is Newton's second law for the angle: the left side is the rate of change of the bob's angular momentum about the pivot minus the torques that come from the energies, and $Q$ is any other torque, here the generator's. The rod's forces never appear, because they do no work when the bob moves the way the rod allows. (The same equation also follows from torques about the pivot in the turning frame, with the centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler forces; the Check below compares the two.)

Differentiate (1.1) and square. The kinetic energy is

$$T = \tfrac12 m\left[r_p^2\dot\varphi^2 + L^2(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)^2 + 2r_pL\,\dot\varphi(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\cos\theta\right]. \tag{1.2}$$

Read it as a sum: the pivot's motion, the bob's motion about the pivot, and a cross term that couples them through the angle between the rod and the blade. The potential energy is $V = mgy$, and $\mathcal L = T - V$.

The generator's stator is fixed to the blade and its rotor to the rod, so it brakes only the rod's motion relative to the blade. In an ideal generator the voltage is proportional to the speed, $k_e\dot\theta$; it drives a current $i = k_e\dot\theta/(R_c + R_L)$ through the coil (resistance $R_c$) and the load ($R_L$); and the current makes a braking torque $k_t\,i$. So the torque is $-c\dot\theta$ with $c = k_tk_e/(R_c + R_L)$: a pure brake proportional to speed. A brake of this kind enters Lagrange's equation as $Q = -c\dot\theta$, which can be written as the derivative of Rayleigh's dissipation function $\mathcal F$, half the brake's power:

$$\mathcal F = \tfrac12 c\,\dot\theta^2, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\theta} = -\frac{\partial\mathcal F}{\partial\dot\theta} = -c\,\dot\theta.$$

The two derivatives, from (1.2) and $V$:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta} = mL^2(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta) + mr_pL\,\dot\varphi\cos\theta, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\theta} = -mr_pL\,\dot\varphi(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\sin\theta - mgL\cos(\varphi+\theta).$$

Take the time derivative of the first, $mL^2(\ddot\varphi+\ddot\theta) + mr_pL(\ddot\varphi\cos\theta - \dot\varphi\dot\theta\sin\theta)$, subtract the second, and the two $\dot\varphi\dot\theta\sin\theta$ terms cancel:

$$mL^2(\ddot\theta+\ddot\varphi) + mr_pL\left(\ddot\varphi\cos\theta + \dot\varphi^2\sin\theta\right) + mgL\cos(\varphi+\theta) + c\,\dot\theta = 0. \tag{1.3}$$

Every term has a meaning. $mL^2\ddot\theta$ is the bob's inertia about the pivot. $mr_pL\dot\varphi^2\sin\theta$ is the centrifugal pull trying to bring the rod back along the blade: a restoring torque, like a spring. $mgL\cos(\varphi+\theta)$ is gravity's torque, which turns with the rotor. The two terms with $\ddot\varphi$ appear only when the rotor speeds up or slows down. $c\dot\theta$ is the generator.

Check
Units: every term is a torque, kg m²/s² = N m. Limit: stop the rotor ($\dot\varphi = \ddot\varphi = 0$) with the blade pointing along $\varphi_0$. Then (1.3) is $mL^2\ddot\theta + mgL\cos(\varphi_0+\theta) + c\dot\theta = 0$: an ordinary damped pendulum, at rest with the rod hanging straight down, $\varphi_0+\theta = -\pi/2$, and swinging at $\omega^2 = g/L$ about it. The derivation was repeated by computer in two ways, by Lagrange in SymPy and by Newton's law in the turning frame with its centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler forces; the two results agree identically.

1.4 Who pays

The rotor must push the pivot along its path. The torque it needs is Lagrange's expression for the other angle, $\varphi$: the torque that makes $\varphi(t)$ happen,

$$\tau_{\rm rot} = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\varphi} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\varphi}.$$

Now follow the bob's energy $E = T + V$ in time. The quickest route treats $\varphi$ as a second coordinate, driven by the torque $\tau_{\rm rot}$. $\mathcal L$ does not contain $t$ explicitly, so the energy function $h = \dot\varphi\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\varphi + \dot\theta\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\theta - \mathcal L$ changes only through the outside torques: $dh/dt = \tau_{\rm rot}\dot\varphi - c\dot\theta^2$. And because $T$ is a quadratic form in $(\dot\varphi, \dot\theta)$, the first two terms of $h$ add up to $2T$, so $h = T + V = E$. (SymPy confirms it the long way, by differentiating $E$ and using (1.3).) So

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \tau_{\rm rot}\,\dot\varphi - c\,\dot\theta^2. \tag{1.4}$$

The first term is the work the rotor does on the pendulum; the second is the power the generator takes. In a steady swing $E$ returns to the same value every turn, so over a turn the two balance: the rotor pays for every joule the generator harvests. Gravity does no net work over a turn, since the bob comes back to the same height. Seen from the blade, gravity is the hand that pushes the rod; seen from the ground, it only steers energy that the rotor supplies. Section 3.2 shows where, physically, the rotor's extra torque comes from. Both pictures will be used: whenever these notes say that gravity feeds the swing, they speak from the blade, and the energy still comes from the rotor.

1.5 The rotor as a clock

Now let the rotor turn steadily, $\dot\varphi = \Omega$. A natural clock is the rotor's own angle: one turn is $2\pi$. Write $\theta' = d\theta/d\varphi = \dot\theta/\Omega$ and divide (1.3) by $mL^2\Omega^2$:

$$\boxed{\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi+\theta) = 0,} \qquad R = \frac{r_p}{L},\quad G = \frac{g}{\Omega^2L},\quad \kappa = \frac{c}{mL^2\Omega}. \tag{1.5}$$

Three numbers say everything. $R$ is the stiffness of the centrifugal spring: seen from the blade, a free small swing makes $\sqrt R$ oscillations per rotor turn (gravity's own stiffening changes sign every half turn and averages out). $G$ is the strength of gravity's push. $\kappa$ is the generator. Their ratio $R/G$ is the $\Gamma$ of section 1.1. Two pendula with the same $R$, $G$ and $\kappa$ do exactly the same thing, turn for turn, whatever their size.

On our blade
Across Sol's 18 mountings $R$ runs from {{n:R_min}} to {{n:R_max}} and $G$ from {{n:G_min}} to {{n:G_max}}. The rod's own frequency is therefore {{n:ratio_f_min}} to {{n:ratio_f_max}} times the once-per-turn push of gravity: always above it, never at it.
