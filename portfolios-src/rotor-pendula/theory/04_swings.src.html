4 Big swings, and the bill

Chapter 3 kept only the terms of (1.8) that are linear in $\theta$. This chapter solves the full equation on a computer, finds out how exactly the rotor pays, and asks where along the second blade its mass is best placed.

4.1 The full equation, solved numerically

Push a spoon slowly through thick honey and it moves calmly; flick it and the honey stops it almost at once. A heavily braked second blade has both kinds of motion: a slow swing, and a very fast death of any jerk the brake fights. An equation with two such different speeds in it is called stiff.

How different are the two speeds? A small disturbance of the braked spring behaves as $e^{\mu\varphi}$, with $\mu^2 + \kappa\mu + R = 0$. At the best brake, $\kappa^* = R - 1$, the two roots are far apart. The fast one, $\mu \approx -\kappa^*$, says that a jerk of the second blade against the generator dies in $1/(\kappa^*\Omega)$: {{n:relax_8}} s at 8 m/s. The slow one, $\mu \approx -R/\kappa^*$, says that a second blade pushed off its path creeps back to it in $\kappa^*/(R\Omega)$: {{n:relax_slow8}} s. One turn lasts {{n:turn_8}} s.

A method that computes each step only from the present state (an explicit method, such as the classical Runge–Kutta method) must take steps shorter than the fast time, or its errors grow without bound. An implicit method instead solves, at each step, an equation for the state at the end of the step, and stays stable with steps as long as the slow swing allows. The simulation that comes with these notes uses one, the three-stage Radau IIA method, which is of fifth order: halving the step should cut the error by $2^5$, and on this equation it cuts it on average by $2^{ {{n:radau_order}} }$. The torques it uses agree with a direct sum of Newton's law over the pieces of the second blade to {{n:newton_agree}}, and after the start it agrees with an independent high-accuracy solver to {{n:scipy_agree}} in the harvested energy. Its first turns carry an error of {{n:startup_err}}, because a step, 1/240 of a turn, is longer than the fast time; the runs let 60 turns pass before they measure, and by then it has died away.

The closed form (3.5) is within {{n:lin_err_max}} % of the full equation at every wind, although the second blade swings up to {{n:Th_max}} rad ({{n:Th_max_deg}}°). Why so close? At second order in the swing three effects appear, and the computer can switch each on alone, holding the brake at $\kappa^*$. At 6 m/s, where the swing is largest, the term section 3.1 dropped, $-G\theta\sin\varphi$, changes the power by {{n:trm_param_6}} %; the softer spring of $\sin\theta$ changes it by {{n:trm_sin_6}} %; and the push's own weakening at large angles, the term $-\tfrac12G\theta^2\cos\varphi$ of $\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, changes it by about {{n:trm_th2_6}} %, the largest of the three. Together, in the full equation, {{n:trm_full_6}} %: the effects pull both ways and nearly cancel. At 8 and 10 m/s each is three and eight times smaller, as the swing squared (the full equation: {{n:trm_full_8}} % and {{n:trm_full_10}} %).

4.2 The second blade trails, and that is the bill

Keep the term that section 3.1 dropped, $-G\theta\sin\varphi$, and average (1.8) over a steady turn. The averages of $\theta''$, $\theta'$ and $\cos\varphi$ vanish, and what is left is

$$R\,\bar\theta = G\,\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle, \tag{4.1}$$

with the swing (3.2), $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi-\delta)$, on the right. Its average with $\sin\varphi$ is $-\tfrac12\Theta\sin\delta$, and (3.2) gives $\sin\delta = \kappa\Theta/G$, so

$$\bar\theta = -\frac{\kappa\,\Theta^2}{2R}, \qquad\text{at the best brake}\quad \bar\theta = -\frac{G^2}{4R(R-1)}. \tag{4.2}$$

The minus sign means the second blade trails behind the mother blade's line, against the rotation, like a weight towed behind a boat. Now compare (4.2) with the harvested power (3.3), $\bar P = I\Omega^3\cdot\tfrac12\kappa\Theta^2$. The same combination $\kappa\Theta^2$ appears in both, and since $IR = \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p$, for every brake

$$\boxed{\bar P = -\,\frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\Omega^3\,\bar\theta.} \tag{4.3}$$

The trail is the bill. The exact version holds for any steady swing, large or small. Write out the torque of (1.5) for a steadily turning rotor, the torque $\tau$ the shaft must supply, and use (1.6) to remove the second blade's angular acceleration. A page of algebra (checked in SymPy) gives

$$\tau = -\frac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta + (M_1d_1 + mr_p)\,g\cos\varphi + \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{ml}{2}r_p\,\frac{d\sin\theta}{dt} + ml\,r_p\Omega\cos\theta - c\,\theta\right].$$

The second term is the rotor carrying the weight of the first arm and of the second blade round, which averages to zero over a turn (on a turbine the other two blades balance the first arm's). The bracket is a total time derivative of something that returns to its value every turn, so it averages to zero too; it holds the generator's torque, $-c\dot\theta = -d(c\theta)/dt$. Only the first term survives, so by (1.7) $\bar P = \langle\tau\rangle\Omega = -\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^3\langle\sin\theta\rangle$, exactly. (With the geared generator of chapter 5 the identity changes, because the gearbox's own torque enters the rotor's.)

Figure 8 shows the mechanism. The second blade, flung outwards, pulls on the hinge with the force $m\Omega^2$ times its centre of mass's distance from the hub. The pull acts at the hinge and points the way the centre of mass lies as seen from the hub. When the second blade trails, its centre of mass sits a little behind the mother blade's line, so that direction tilts backwards by a small angle, and a line through the hinge in that direction misses the hub by about $\tfrac l2\sin\theta$. That is a lever arm: the pull holds the rotor back with the torque $\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta$, negative while the second blade trails. The rotor must push against it all the time, and that steady push is the power it pays. Could the second blade lead instead? Then its pull would push the rotor on while it also fed the generator, energy from nothing; with a brake taking energy, it must trail.

4.3 Where the mass sits

Swing a hammer held at the very end of its handle, then grip the same hammer halfway up. The second swing is easier and weaker: the same mass now sits nearer your hand, with less leverage. Spreading the second blade's mass along its length does the same to it.

Nothing in (1.6) depends on the second blade's shape except through its moment of inertia and its first moment, so it holds for any rigid body on the hinge, with its own moment of inertia $I_p$ about the hinge and its own first moment $Ml_c$, its mass times the distance from the hinge to its centre of mass. Redo chapter 3 for such a body: $I$ becomes $I_p$, $ml/2$ becomes $Ml_c$, and (3.5) becomes

$$P^*_{\rm rigid} = \frac{(M\,l_c\,g)^2}{4\,\Omega\,(M\,l_c\,r_p - I_p)}. \tag{4.4}$$

On a large rotor this is simply proportional to the first moment, as (3.7) said for the rod. So the same mass placed farther from the hinge gives more, in proportion to the distance of its centre of mass. A uniform blade has its centre of mass halfway out; the same mass as a lump at its end has it all the way out, and gives, by (4.4), {{n:rod_vs_point}} times as much, almost exactly twice. A real blade-shaped second blade tapers towards its free end like the mother blade, which puts its centre of mass nearer the hinge than halfway: for harvesting, the wrong way round.

Check
For the lump on a light rod, $I_p = mL^2$ and $Ml_c = mL$, and (4.4) is the $mLg^2/(4\Omega|r_p - L|)$ of section 3.3. For the uniform rod, $I_p = ml^2/3$ and $Ml_c = ml/2$, and (4.4) is (3.5).
What to remember
  • The braked second blade has two time scales: a jerk dies in about a hundredth of a second, a displaced swing creeps back in about a second. That makes its equation stiff, and an implicit solver handles it.
  • The full equation agrees with the small-swing power (3.5) to a third of a per cent.
  • On average the second blade trails slightly behind the mother blade. Its outward pull then holds the rotor back, and that torque times $\Omega$ is exactly the harvested power: the trail is the bill.
  • Mass far from the hinge is worth more: the same mass as a lump at the end gives twice what a uniform rod gives.
