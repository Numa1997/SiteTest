4 Big swings, and the bill

Chapter 3 kept only the terms of (1.8) linear in $\theta$. This chapter solves the full equation, finds exactly how the rotor pays, and asks where along the second blade its mass is best placed.

4.1 The full equation, solved numerically

A stiff equation
feel
Push a spoon slowly through thick honey and it moves calmly; flick it and the honey stops it almost at once. One spoon, two speeds.
physics
An equation is stiff when it holds motions on very different time scales. The fast one dies at once, but a careless method of solution must still take steps short enough to follow it, or blow up.
measure
At 8 m/s and the best brake, a jerk of the second blade against the generator dies in {{n:relax_8}} s; a second blade pushed off its path creeps back in {{n:relax_slow8}} s; a turn lasts {{n:turn_8}} s.
maths
On $y' = \mu y$ with $\mu < 0$, the simplest explicit step (forward Euler) $y_{n+1} = (1 + h\mu)y_n$ is stable only for $h \le 2/|\mu|$; the implicit step $y_{n+1} = y_n + h\mu y_{n+1}$, that is $y_{n+1} = y_n/(1 - h\mu)$, is stable for every $h$. Higher-order explicit methods stretch the limit only by a small factor.

The two speeds are the roots of the braked spring, $\mu^2 + \kappa\mu + R = 0$. Their sum is $-\kappa$ and their product $R$; at the best brake $\kappa^{*2} = (R-1)^2 \gg 4R$, so

$$\mu_{\rm fast} \approx -\kappa^*, \qquad \mu_{\rm slow} \approx -\frac{R}{\kappa^*}, \qquad \text{times}\ \ \frac{1}{\kappa^*\Omega}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \frac{\kappa^*}{R\,\Omega}.$$

The simulation of these notes uses an implicit method, the three-stage Radau IIA method (Hairer and Wanner, Solving Ordinary Differential Equations II, §IV.5). It belongs to the family of Runge–Kutta methods, which advance $y' = F(t, y)$ by one step $h$ through a few trial rates inside the step. In an explicit method each trial rate uses only the ones before it; in Radau IIA the three trial values $Y_i$, at the fractions $c_i = (4-\sqrt6)/10$, $(4+\sqrt6)/10$ and $1$ of the step, are found together from

$$Y_i = y_n + h\sum_{j=1}^3 a_{ij}\,F(t_n + c_jh,\,Y_j), \qquad y_{n+1} = Y_3,$$

a set of equations that each step solves by Newton's method. The weights $a_{ij}$ are chosen so that the last row integrates every polynomial up to degree four exactly over the step; the method is then of fifth order. On the test equation $y' = \mu y$ one step multiplies $y$ by

$$\mathcal R(z) = \frac{1 + \tfrac25 z + \tfrac1{20}z^2}{1 - \tfrac35 z + \tfrac3{20}z^2 - \tfrac1{60}z^3}, \qquad z = h\mu,$$

which agrees with $e^z$ up to $z^5$, has $|\mathcal R| \le 1$ for every decaying $\mu$, and tends to zero as $z \to -\infty$. That last property is what a stiff equation needs: a fast motion that the step cannot resolve is wiped out in a single step, as the real one dies at once, instead of being carried along or flipped from step to step. Halving the step should cut the error by $2^5$, and here it cuts it on average by $2^{ {{n:radau_order}} }$. Its torques agree with a direct sum of Newton's law over the pieces of the second blade to {{n:newton_agree}}, and after the start it agrees with an independent high-accuracy solver to {{n:scipy_agree}} in the harvested energy. Its first turns carry an error of {{n:startup_err}}, because a step, 1/240 of a turn, is longer than the fast time; the runs let 60 turns pass before they measure.

The closed form (3.5) is within {{n:lin_err_max}} % of the full equation at every wind, although the second blade swings with an amplitude of up to {{n:Th_max}} rad ({{n:Th_max_deg}}°). At second order three effects appear. The computer switches them on one at a time, at $\kappa^*$, and measures how much each changes the power at 6 m/s, where the swing is largest:

$$\begin{aligned} -G\,\theta\sin\varphi:&\quad \text{the spring rocks once per turn} &&{{n:trm_param_6}}\ \%,\\ R\sin\theta \ne R\theta:&\quad \text{the spring softens at large angles} &&{{n:trm_sin_6}}\ \%,\\ -\tfrac12 G\theta^2\cos\varphi + \dots:&\quad \text{the rest of gravity's term (all of it, less the rocking)} &&{{n:trm_th2_6}}\ \%, \end{aligned}$$

They pull both ways and nearly cancel: together, in the full equation, they change it by {{n:trm_full_6}} %. At 8 and 10 m/s each is three and eight times smaller, as the swing squared ({{n:trm_full_8}} % and {{n:trm_full_10}} %).

4.2 The second blade trails, and that is the bill

Keep the dropped term and average (1.8) over a steady turn. $\langle\theta''\rangle = \langle\theta'\rangle = 0$ (both are rates of something periodic), $\langle\sin\theta\rangle \approx \bar\theta$, and $\langle\cos(\varphi+\theta)\rangle \approx \langle\cos\varphi\rangle - \langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle$ $= -\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle$:

$$R\,\bar\theta = G\,\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle. \tag{4.1}$$

With the swing (3.2), $\langle -\Theta\cos(\varphi-\delta)\sin\varphi\rangle = -\tfrac12\Theta\sin\delta$, and (3.2) gives $\sin\delta = \kappa\Theta/G$:

$$\bar\theta = -\frac{\kappa\,\Theta^2}{2R}, \qquad\text{at the best brake}\quad \bar\theta = -\frac{G^2}{4R(R-1)}. \tag{4.2}$$

The second blade trails behind the mother blade's line, like a weight towed behind a boat. The harvested power (3.3) is $I\Omega^3\cdot\tfrac12\kappa\Theta^2 = -I\Omega^3R\,\bar\theta$, and $IR = \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p$, so for every brake

$$\boxed{\bar P = -\,\frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\Omega^3\,\bar\theta.} \tag{4.3}$$

The trail is the bill. The exact version holds for any steady swing, large or small. Take the shaft's torque from (1.5) with $\dot\varphi = \Omega$, $\ddot\varphi = 0$:

$$\tau = \frac{d}{dt}\Big[(J_1 + I + mr_p^2 + mlr_p\cos\theta)\,\Omega + \Big(I + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\cos\theta\Big)\dot\theta\Big] + (M_1d_1 + mr_p)\,g\cos\varphi + \frac{ml}{2}\,g\cos(\varphi+\theta).$$

Differentiate. The first arm's own inertia $J_1$ drops out, since $\Omega$ is constant:

$$\tau = -ml\,r_p\Omega\sin\theta\,\dot\theta + I\ddot\theta + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\big(\cos\theta\,\ddot\theta - \sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2\big) + (M_1d_1 + mr_p)\,g\cos\varphi + \frac{ml}{2}\,g\cos(\varphi+\theta).$$

Two of these terms, $I\ddot\theta + \tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, are exactly what the second blade's own equation (1.6) gives as $-\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta - c\dot\theta$. Put that in, and gather everything that is a rate of change of something into one time derivative:

$$\tau = -\frac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta + (M_1d_1 + mr_p)\,g\cos\varphi + \frac{d}{dt}\Big[\frac{ml}{2}r_p\,\frac{d\sin\theta}{dt} + ml\,r_p\Omega\cos\theta - c\,\theta\Big].$$

(Check the bracket: $\tfrac{d}{dt}\tfrac{d\sin\theta}{dt} = \cos\theta\,\ddot\theta - \sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2$, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\cos\theta = -\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\theta = \dot\theta$.) Over a steady turn $\langle\cos\varphi\rangle = 0$, so the weight term averages to zero, and the bracket returns to its value, so its derivative averages to zero too. Only the first term survives, and by (1.7)

$$\bar P = \langle\tau\rangle\,\Omega = -\frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\Omega^3\,\langle\sin\theta\rangle, \qquad\text{exactly.}$$

(With the geared generator of chapter 5 the identity changes, because the gearbox's own torque enters the rotor's.) Figure {{fig:trail}} shows the mechanism. The second blade, flung outwards, pulls on the hinge, apart from its weight and from the parts that are rates of change of something periodic (the two kinds of term the exact torque above showed to average to zero over a turn), with the force $m\Omega^2\mathbf r_{\rm cm}$, pointing the way from the hub to its centre of mass $\mathbf r_{\rm cm} = r_p\hat{\mathbf e} + \tfrac l2\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$. That force acts at the hinge, so its line runs through the hinge parallel to $\mathbf r_{\rm cm}$; when the second blade trails, that line misses the hub by about $\tfrac l2\sin\theta$, a lever arm. Its torque about the hub is $r_p\hat{\mathbf e}\times m\Omega^2\mathbf r_{\rm cm} = \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf z}$, and for $\theta < 0$ it holds the rotor back. A second blade that led would push the rotor on while feeding the generator: energy from nothing. With a brake taking energy, it must trail.

4.3 Where the mass sits

Swing a hammer held at the very end of its handle, then grip it halfway up. The second swing is easier and weaker: the same mass sits nearer your hand.

Nothing in (1.6) depends on the second blade's shape except its moment of inertia about the hinge, $I_p$, and its first moment, $Ml_c$ (mass times the distance from the hinge to its centre of mass), with $\theta$ measured to the line through the centre of mass. Chapter 3 with $I \to I_p$ and $\tfrac{ml}{2} \to Ml_c$ gives $R = Ml_cr_p/I_p$, $G = Ml_cg/(I_p\Omega^2)$ and $P^* = I_p\Omega^3G^2/[4(R-1)]$:

$$P^*_{\rm rigid} = \frac{(M\,l_c\,g)^2}{4\,\Omega\,(M\,l_c\,r_p - I_p)}. \tag{4.4}$$

On a large rotor this is proportional to the first moment, as (3.7) said for the rod: the same mass farther out gives more. A uniform blade has its centre of mass halfway out; the same mass as a lump at its end gives, by (4.4), {{n:rod_vs_point}} times as much. A blade-shaped second blade, tapering towards its free end, has its centre of mass nearer the hinge than halfway: for harvesting, the wrong way round.

Check
A lump on a light rod: $I_p = mL^2$, $Ml_c = mL$, and (4.4) is the $mLg^2/(4\Omega|r_p - L|)$ of section 3.3. The uniform rod: $I_p = ml^2/3$, $Ml_c = ml/2$, and (4.4) is (3.5).
What to remember
  • The braked second blade has two time scales, a hundredth of a second and about a second: its equation is stiff, and an implicit solver handles it.
  • The full equation agrees with the small-swing power (3.5) to a third of a per cent; three second-order effects nearly cancel.
  • On average the second blade trails. Its outward pull then holds the rotor back, and that torque times $\Omega$ is exactly the harvested power: the trail is the bill.
  • Mass far from the hinge is worth more: a lump at the end gives twice what a uniform rod of the same mass gives.
Problems
  1. Use (4.3) and the full equation's trail at 6 m/s, {{n:lean_run}} rad, to estimate the harvested power. Compare the full equation's {{n:P6_full}} mW.
    Answer$\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^3|\bar\theta|$ = {{n:p3_bill}} mW, about one per cent above. The exact identity uses $\langle\sin\theta\rangle$ over the full swing rather than the small-swing relation (4.3), and with it the agreement is exact.
  2. A 1 kg lump on a massless 1 m rod is compared with a uniform 1 kg rod of 1 m. Which needs the stronger generator, and by how much?
    AnswerThe lump: its first moment, $mL$ = 1 kg m, is twice the rod's, $ml/2$ = 0.5 kg m, and the generator's torque at the best brake, (first moment)$\cdot g/\sqrt2$, is proportional to it: twice as large.
  3. With the forward Euler step, what is the longest stable step at the best brake, in fractions of a turn?
    Answer$h \le 2/|\mu_{\rm fast}|$ radians of rotor angle, $1/(\pi|\mu_{\rm fast}|)$ of a turn, with $\mu_{\rm fast} = -\tfrac{\kappa^*}{2} - \sqrt{\kappa^{*2}/4 - R} \approx -\kappa^*$: 1/{{n:p4_hmax_inv}} of a turn at every wind, since $\kappa^* = R - 1$ = {{n:Rm1}} does not depend on the wind. The simulation's implicit method takes 1/240 of a turn. (The classical Runge–Kutta method would just manage that step too; its limit is about 2.8/$|\mu|$. An implicit method has no limit at all.)
