Part IA second blade on a turning blade

0 The machine and the question

0.1 A sensor that nobody can reach

The blades of a large wind turbine are some 60 m long and turn ten times a minute for twenty years. Engineers would like to know how each blade bends and shakes while it works: a crack or a loose bolt changes how a blade vibrates long before it fails. A small sensor glued inside the blade can measure that, but it needs electricity, and the far end of a blade is the worst place on the machine to bring it. A cable must pass through the rotating hub; a battery 60 m out along a blade cannot be changed without stopping the turbine and sending a technician up a rope.

The blade itself is full of motion. So here is an idea. At the very tip of the blade, put a hinge whose axis is parallel to the rotor's shaft, and hang on it a second, much lighter blade, free to turn in the rotor plane. As the big blade (the mother blade) turns, the second blade swings back and forth about its hinge. A small generator in the hinge brakes that swing and turns it into electricity, and the sensor runs on that.

Ride a Ferris wheel holding a ruler loosely by one end, so that it can turn in the plane of the wheel. The wheel presses everything outwards, so the ruler lies along the spoke, pointing away from the hub. But gravity keeps pulling down while the wheel carries you round: near the top and the bottom it pulls along the ruler, on the way up and on the way down it pulls across it, first to one side and then to the other. Once per turn the ruler is pushed one way and then the other. That once-per-turn push is what we harvest.

0.2 Four questions, and the short answers

Four questions run through these notes: how much power, who pays for it, how big the second blade should be, and whether the wind's randomness helps. In short:

  • The power. A second blade of {{n:mB}} kg and {{n:lB}} m, hinged at the 63 m tip of a 5 MW turbine, takes between {{n:P_low}} and {{n:P_high}} mW of mechanical power from the swing, at steady winds of 10 and 6 m/s (chapters 2 and 3). A sensor node that samples an accelerometer continuously (ADXL362) and once a second wakes a radio chip (CC2652R) for 1 ms of computing and 1 ms of sending needs about {{n:sensor_uW}} µW, from the typical currents of the two datasheets (section 8.3); that budget leaves out the radio's start-up and receive windows and the losses of a power converter. A real motor and gearbox from a catalogue deliver {{n:g4_min}} to {{n:g4_max}} mW of it to the wires, {{n:g4_margin}} times the sensor's need or more (chapter 7).
  • Who pays. The rotor. Every joule the generator takes, the rotor supplies, through a slightly larger torque on its shaft (sections 1.4 and 3.2). The second blade takes at most {{n:share_max}} parts in a million of the turbine's power.
  • How big. What counts is the second blade's first moment, its mass times the distance from the hinge to its centre of mass, $ml/2$: the power grows in proportion to it (chapter 2). Where the mass sits matters as much as how much there is: a uniform blade of mass $m$ and length $l$ gives half of what the same mass would give in a lump at its end (chapter 3). The size these notes use, {{n:mB}} kg on {{n:lB}} m, is the one the generator of chapter 7 serves best. Over the sizes of real tip devices, up to three metres long and as heavy as the blade's own tip, the best power at 6 m/s runs from {{n:sz_P_lo}} mW to {{n:sz_P_hi}} W, but the generator must grow with it (section 7.6).
  • The wind's randomness. Mostly it does not help. Random shaking of white-noise type has a hard ceiling that no motion, chaotic or not, can beat (chapter 4). Gusts change the rotor's speed, and that changes the power by a few per cent at most (chapter 5). The blade's sag at its tip adds a few per cent; its shaking, strongest at the tip, adds a little at tip vibrations of 5 mm and more than gravity gives at 100 mm, a level no source we found confirms (chapter 6).

0.3 Where the numbers come from

Every number in these notes is computed here: the equations are derived in the text, solved by a stiff integrator checked against an independent solver, and the results are put into the text by a script.

The turbine is the NREL 5 MW reference turbine, a published design of a utility turbine (Jonkman, Butterfield, Musial and Scott 2009), with a rotor radius of 63 m. Its blade tip moves about 7.55 times faster than the wind when it works best (its tip-speed ratio $\lambda^* = 7.55$, where its power coefficient peaks at $C_p = 0.482$), and in moderate wind its controller holds it close to that. At a steady wind $v$ the rotor then turns at

$$\Omega = \frac{\lambda^*\,v}{r_{\rm rot}}, \qquad r_{\rm rot} = 63\ \mathrm{m},$$

which for the three winds of these notes, 6, 8 and 10 m/s, is {{n:rpm_6}}, {{n:rpm_8}} and {{n:rpm_10}} rpm. The hinge sits at the tip, $r_p = r_{\rm rot}$, and moves at $\lambda^*v$: {{n:vtip_6}}, {{n:vtip_8}} and {{n:vtip_10}} m/s. The turbine's own power, for the share quoted above, is $P_T = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^2v^3C_p$: the kinetic energy of the air that flows through the rotor's disc each second, $\tfrac12(\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^2v)v^2$, of which the fraction $C_p$ is caught, with air density $\rho = {{n:rho}}$ kg/m³.

The second blade is a thin uniform rod, a modelling choice that keeps the mathematics clean; its length $l = {{n:lB}}$ m is a choice too, and its mass $m = {{n:mB}}$ kg is set in chapter 7 by matching it to a real generator. The drawing of the simulation gives it a blade's tapered outline, but the physics treats it as a uniform rod.

One thing these notes leave out on purpose: the air. At the tip the hinge moves through the air at {{n:vtip_6}} to {{n:vtip_10}} m/s, and the air pushes on anything moving that fast with a pressure of order $\tfrac12\rho v_{\rm tip}^2$, {{n:q_8}} Pa at 8 m/s. To match gravity's whole pull on the second blade, $mg = {{n:mg}}$ N, that pressure needs to act on an area, times a force coefficient, of only $mg/(\tfrac12\rho v_{\rm tip}^2) = {{n:air_area_cm2}}$ cm². A real blade is far larger than that, so air would shape its motion at least as strongly as gravity does. These notes answer the question for the mechanics alone; a model of the air on the second blade is the next step, and section 8.2 says what it could change.

0.4 How to read these notes

Each step is built in the same order. A Picture box gives something to imagine with your hands and eyes. The working follows, one line at a time, with the reason for each line. A green Check box tests the result: its units, its sign, and a limit where we already know the answer. A wine-coloured On our blade box gives the numbers of the real machine, all computed from the model; the few inputs typed by hand (datasheet values and the chosen sizes) are listed with their sources, and a script checks every number in the text. Where a long piece of algebra was done by computer (SymPy, a program for symbolic algebra), the text says so and shows the steps a reader needs.

Conventions
SI units throughout. Gravity $g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, the standard value. A dot is a derivative with respect to time $t$; a prime is a derivative with respect to the rotor's angle $\varphi$ (section 1.5). The second blade's angle $\theta$ is measured from the mother blade's outward direction, positive in the direction the rotor turns.
