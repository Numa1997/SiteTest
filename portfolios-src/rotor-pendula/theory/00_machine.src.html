Part IA pendulum on a turning blade

0 The machine and the question

0.1 A sensor that nobody can reach

The blades of a large wind turbine are some 60 m long and turn ten times a minute for twenty years. Engineers would like to know how each blade bends and shakes while it works: a crack or a loose bolt changes how a blade vibrates long before it fails. A small sensor glued inside the blade can measure that, but it needs electricity, and the inside of a blade is the worst place on the machine to bring it. A cable must pass through the rotating hub; a battery 50 m out along a blade cannot be changed without stopping the turbine and sending a technician up a rope.

The blade itself is full of motion. So here is an idea: hang a small weight on a short rod from a point inside the blade, let it swing as the blade turns, and brake the swing with a tiny generator at the pivot. The generator turns the swing into electricity, and the sensor runs on that.

Sit on a playground roundabout with a bag hanging from your hand. As the roundabout turns, the bag is flung outwards, and it also sways a little, first one way and then the other, once per turn. That sway is what we want to harvest.

0.2 The questions, and the short answers

Three questions run through these notes.

  • How much power? For a weight of 100 g on a rod of 20 cm, between {{n:P_sol_min}} mW and {{n:P_sol_max}} mW, depending on the rotor and on where along the blade the pivot sits (chapters 2 and 3). A low-duty sensor node needs about {{n:sensor_uW}} µW. Even if the electronics lose half of it, the worst mounting gives {{n:margin_min}} times what the sensor needs.
  • Who pays? The rotor. Every joule the generator takes, the rotor supplies, through a slightly larger torque on its shaft (section 1.4). The pendulum takes at most {{n:share_max}} parts in 100 000 of the turbine's power.
  • Can chaos help? A double pendulum can move chaotically. With the same mass and the same reach, it gives {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}} times what a single pendulum gives, and the reason is simple: its mass sits closer to the pivot (chapter 3). When it does turn chaotic, it harvests less than its own regular motion, not more. Random shaking has a hard ceiling that no motion, chaotic or not, can beat (chapter 4).

0.3 Where the numbers come from

These notes are built on a study by Sol, Wind-driven rotor pendula (14 October 2026), which posed the first question and answered it with numerical runs at 18 mountings; his report is kept, word for word, beside these notes. Everything he computed has been rebuilt from scratch here: the equations derived again by two routes, the 36 runs of his mapping repeated with a different solver, and every one reproduced to the last digit he printed. Where these notes go further (why the double loses, the ceiling on random shaking, the effect of gusty wind), they say so.

The 18 mountings are Sol's. A turbine blade's tip moves about seven times faster than the wind (the tip-speed ratio, $\lambda_T = 7$). At a wind of 8 m/s that fixes the rotor radius once the rotor's angular speed $\Omega$ is chosen:

$$R_{\rm rot} = \frac{\lambda_T v}{\Omega} = \frac{56\ \mathrm{m/s}}{\Omega}.$$

A fast rotor is small and a slow rotor is large. Sol takes six speeds, 400, 200 and 100 rpm (rotors 1.3 to 5.3 m in radius) and 20, 14 and 8 rpm (27 to 67 m, the size of real utility turbines), and puts the pivot at 30, 60 or 90 % of the blade.

0.4 How to read these notes

Each step is built in the same order. A Picture box gives something to imagine with your hands and eyes. The Working follows, one line at a time, with the reason for each line. A green Check box tests the result: its units, its sign, and a limit where we already know the answer. A wine-coloured On our blade box gives the numbers of the real mountings, all computed from the model, none typed in by hand.

Conventions
SI units throughout. Gravity $g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, the standard value Sol used. A dot is a derivative with respect to time $t$; a prime is a derivative with respect to the rotor's angle $\varphi$ (section 1.5). The pendulum's angle $\theta$ is measured from the blade's outward direction, positive in the direction the rotor turns.
