Part IA pendulum on a turning blade

0 The machine and the question

0.1 A sensor that nobody can reach

The blades of a large wind turbine are some 60 m long and turn ten times a minute for twenty years. Engineers would like to know how each blade bends and shakes while it works: a crack or a loose bolt changes how a blade vibrates long before it fails. A small sensor glued inside the blade can measure that, but it needs electricity, and the inside of a blade is the worst place on the machine to bring it. A cable must pass through the rotating hub; a battery 50 m out along a blade cannot be changed without stopping the turbine and sending a technician up a rope.

The blade itself is full of motion. So here is an idea: hang a small weight on a short rod from a point inside the blade, let it swing as the blade turns, and brake the swing with a tiny generator at the pivot. The generator turns the swing into electricity, and the sensor runs on that.

Ride a Ferris wheel holding a bag on a short string. The wheel turns in a vertical plane, so as you go round, "down" swings round you: at the bottom of the wheel the bag hangs away from the hub, at the top it hangs towards it, and on the way up and down it hangs off to one side. Once per turn the bag is pulled first one way and then the other. On a turbine blade the turning is much faster, and that once-per-turn sideways pull is what we harvest.

0.2 Four questions, and the short answers

Four questions run through these notes: how much power, who pays for it, whether a double pendulum is better, and whether the wind's randomness helps. In short:

  • The power. For a weight of 100 g on a rod of 20 cm, between {{n:P_sol_min}} mW and {{n:P_sol_max}} mW of mechanical power, depending on the rotor and on where along the blade the pivot sits (chapters 2 and 3). A sensor node that samples an accelerometer continuously (ADXL362) and once a second wakes a radio chip (CC2652R) for 1 ms of computing and 1 ms of sending needs about {{n:sensor_uW}} µW, using the typical currents of the two datasheets. Even if the electronics lose half of the power, the worst mounting gives {{n:margin_min}} times what the sensor needs. A real motor and gearbox from a catalogue lose more than that, mostly to friction, and on a large turbine still deliver {{n:g4_big_min}} to {{n:g4_big_max}} mW to the wires, at least {{n:g4_margin}} times the need (chapter 7).
  • Who pays. The rotor. Every joule the generator takes, the rotor supplies, through a slightly larger torque on its shaft (sections 1.4 and 3.2). The pendulum takes at most {{n:share_max}} parts in 100 000 of the turbine's power.
  • One rod or two. A double pendulum is not better. With the same mass and the same reach it gives {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}} times what a single pendulum gives, and the reason is plain: its mass sits closer to the pivot (chapter 3). In every case tried it moved chaotically only near resonance, where no real mounting is, and the best regular setting found beat every chaotic one.
  • The wind's randomness. It hardly helps. Random shaking of white-noise type has a hard ceiling that no motion, chaotic or not, can beat (chapter 4). Gusts change the rotor's speed, and that changes the power by a few per cent at most, up or down depending on the turbine's controller (chapter 5). The blade's bending adds a couple of per cent at most; its shaking adds little to a short pendulum at tip vibrations of 5 to 20 mm, but more than gravity gives at 100 mm, the top of the range, a level no source we found confirms. A long pendulum tuned to the blade's vibration looks better on paper, but gravity detunes it every turn, and these notes have not shown that it works (chapter 6).

0.3 Where the numbers come from

These notes are built on a study by Sol, Wind-driven rotor pendula (14 October 2026), written for this site; his report is kept, word for word, beside these notes (the link at the top of the page). Sol posed the first question and answered it with numerical runs: one single and one double pendulum at each of 18 mountings, 36 runs in all. Everything he computed has been rebuilt from scratch here: the equations derived again by two routes, and all 36 runs repeated with a different solver, each reproduced to the last digit he printed. Where these notes go further, they say so.

The 18 mountings are Sol's. A turbine blade's tip moves about seven times faster than the wind: the tip-speed ratio is $\lambda_T = 7$. At a wind speed $v = 8$ m/s that fixes the rotor's radius $r_{\rm tip}$ once its angular speed $\Omega$ is chosen:

$$r_{\rm tip} = \frac{\lambda_T\,v}{\Omega} = \frac{56\ \mathrm{m/s}}{\Omega},$$

with $\Omega$ in radians per second: 400 rpm is $400\cdot2\pi/60 = 41.9$ rad/s, so $r_{\rm tip} = 1.34$ m.

A fast rotor is small and a slow rotor is large. Sol takes six speeds, 400, 200 and 100 rpm (rotors 1.3 to 5.3 m in radius) and 20, 14 and 8 rpm (27 to 67 m, the size of real utility turbines), and puts the pivot at 30, 60 or 90 % of the way to the tip. The turbine's own power, for the share quoted above, is Sol's estimate $P_T = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm tip}^2v^3C_p$: the kinetic energy of the air that flows through the rotor's disc each second, $\tfrac12(\rho\pi r_{\rm tip}^2v)v^2$, of which the fraction $C_p$ is caught; with air density $\rho = {{n:rho}}$ kg/m³ and power coefficient $C_p = {{n:cp_sol}}$; the largest share is at {{n:share_where}}, on the smallest rotor, whose own power is smallest.

0.4 How to read these notes

Each step is built in the same order. A Picture box gives something to imagine with your hands and eyes. The working follows, one line at a time, with the reason for each line. A green Check box tests the result: its units, its sign, and a limit where we already know the answer. A wine-coloured On our blade box gives the numbers of the real mountings, all computed from the model, none typed in by hand. Where a long piece of algebra was done by computer (SymPy, a program for symbolic algebra), the text says so and shows the steps a reader needs.

Conventions
SI units throughout. Gravity $g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, the standard value Sol used. A dot is a derivative with respect to time $t$; a prime is a derivative with respect to the rotor's angle $\varphi$ (section 1.5). The pendulum's angle $\theta$ is measured from the blade's outward direction, positive in the direction the rotor turns.
