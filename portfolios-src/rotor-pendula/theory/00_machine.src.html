Part IA pendulum on a turning blade

0 The machine and the question

0.1 A sensor that nobody can reach

The blades of a large wind turbine are some 60 m long and turn ten times a minute for twenty years. Engineers would like to know how each blade bends and shakes while it works: a crack or a loose bolt changes how a blade vibrates long before it fails. A small sensor glued inside the blade can measure that, but it needs electricity, and the inside of a blade is the worst place on the machine to bring it. A cable must pass through the rotating hub; a battery 50 m out along a blade cannot be changed without stopping the turbine and sending a technician up a rope.

The blade itself is full of motion. So here is an idea: hang a small weight on a short rod from a point inside the blade, let it swing as the blade turns, and brake the swing with a tiny generator at the pivot. The generator turns the swing into electricity, and the sensor runs on that.

Ride a Ferris wheel holding a bag on a short string. The wheel turns in a vertical plane, so as you go round, "down" swings round you: at the bottom of the wheel the bag hangs away from the hub, at the top it hangs towards it, and on the way up and down it hangs off to one side. Once per turn the bag is pulled first one way and then the other. On a turbine blade the turning is much faster, and that once-per-turn sideways pull is what we harvest.

0.2 Four questions, and the short answers

Four questions run through these notes: how much power, who pays for it, whether a double pendulum is better, and whether the wind's randomness helps. In short:

  • The power. For a bob of {{n:M}} kg on a rod of {{n:L_cm}} cm, between {{n:P_min}} mW and {{n:P_max}} mW of mechanical power, depending on the wind and on where along the blade the pivot sits (chapters 2 and 3). A sensor node that samples an accelerometer continuously and once a second wakes a radio chip for 1 ms of computing and 1 ms of sending needs about {{n:sensor_uW}} µW (section 0.3 adds it up from the two datasheets); this budget leaves out the radio's start-up and receive windows and the losses of a power converter, which is why the margins below are quoted with room to spare. Even if the electronics lose half of the power, the worst mounting gives {{n:margin_min}} times what the sensor needs. A real motor and gearbox from a catalogue lose more than that, mostly to friction, and still deliver {{n:g4_big_min}} to {{n:g4_big_max}} mW to the wires, at least {{n:g4_margin}} times the need before the losses of the sensor's own electronics (chapter 7).
  • Who pays. The rotor. Every joule the generator takes, the rotor supplies, through a slightly larger torque on its shaft (sections 1.4 and 3.2). The pendulum takes at most {{n:share_max}} parts in a hundred million of the turbine's power.
  • One rod or two. A double pendulum is not better. With the same mass and the same reach it gives {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}} times what a single pendulum gives, and the reason is plain: its mass sits closer to the pivot (chapter 3). In every case tried it moved chaotically only near resonance, where no real mounting is, and the best regular setting found beat every chaotic one.
  • The wind's randomness. Mostly it does not help. Random shaking of white-noise type has a hard ceiling that no motion, chaotic or not, can beat (chapter 4). Gusts change the rotor's speed, and that changes the power by a few per cent at most, up or down depending on the turbine's controller (chapter 5). The blade's bending adds a couple of per cent at most; its shaking adds little to a short pendulum at tip vibrations of 5 to 20 mm, but more than gravity gives at 100 mm, the top of the range, a level no source we found confirms. A long pendulum tuned to the blade's vibration looks better on paper, but gravity detunes it every turn, and these notes have not shown that it works (chapter 6).

0.3 The turbine, the pendulum and the sensor

These notes put the pendulum on one real machine, the NREL 5 MW reference turbine (section 8.3, source 1): three blades on a rotor of radius $r_{\rm tip} = 63$ m, designed by the US National Renewable Energy Laboratory so that engineers everywhere can compute with the same turbine. In moderate wind its controller keeps the blade tips moving $\lambda^* = {{n:lam_star}}$ times faster than the wind, the tip-speed ratio at which the rotor catches the largest share of the wind's power, $C_p = {{n:cp_used}}$. So a steady wind $v$ sets the rotor's angular speed:

$$\Omega = \frac{\lambda^*\,v}{r_{\rm tip}}.$$

We take three winds, 6, 8 and 10 m/s, which turn the rotor at {{n:rpm_6}}, {{n:rpm_8}} and {{n:rpm_10}} rpm (revolutions per minute): from the turbine's cut-in speed of 6.9 rpm to just below its rated 12.1 rpm. The pivot sits at 30, 60 or 90 % of the rotor's radius, {{n:rp_30}}, {{n:rp_60}} or {{n:rp_90}} m from the hub. That makes nine mountings. The turbine's own power, for the share quoted above, is $P_T = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm tip}^2v^3C_p$: the kinetic energy of the air that flows through the rotor's disc each second, $\tfrac12(\rho\pi r_{\rm tip}^2v)v^2$, of which the fraction $C_p$ is caught; with air density $\rho = {{n:rho}}$ kg/m³ that is {{n:PT_6}}, {{n:PT_8}} and {{n:PT_10}} MW at the three winds. The largest share is at {{n:share_where}}.

The rod is $L = {{n:L}}$ m long, short enough that the whole device fits in a box about 20 cm across inside the blade's shell at every pivot; the thinnest of the three sections, at 90 %, is 0.41 m thick and 2.26 m wide. The bob's mass is not picked but sized, in chapter 7: the harvest grows with the bob's mass, but a real generator takes a fixed toll in friction, so a light bob loses most of its swing to it. The rule is to take the smallest mass in the series 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1 and 2 kg whose worst mounting still gives the sensor ten times its need through the real generator, even if the gearbox has twice the friction its catalogue suggests and passes on only half of the power. That gives $M = {{n:M}}$ kg ({{n:design_times}} times the need at the worst mounting; {{n:design_prev}} kg would give only {{n:design_prev_times}} times). The double pendulum of chapter 3 has the same mass and reach: {{n:Mhalf}} kg on {{n:Lhalf}} m, twice.

The sensor node is an ADXL362 accelerometer from Analog Devices and a CC2652R radio chip from Texas Instruments (section 8.3, source 7). The accelerometer samples continuously at 100 Hz and draws 1.8 µA at 2.0 V: {{n:sens_acc}} µW. The radio chip sleeps in standby at 0.94 µA and 3.0 V, {{n:sens_sb}} µW, and once a second wakes for 1 ms of computing at 3.39 mA and 1 ms of sending at 7.0 mA, both at 3.0 V: $(3.39 + 7.0)$ mA × 3.0 V × 1 ms per second, {{n:sens_burst}} µW. Together the node needs {{n:sensor_uW}} µW.

0.4 How to read these notes

Each step is built in the same order. A Picture box gives something to imagine with your hands and eyes. The working follows, one line at a time, with the reason for each line. A green Check box tests the result: its units, its sign, and a limit where we already know the answer. A wine-coloured On our blade box gives the numbers of the real mountings, all computed from the model; the few inputs typed by hand (datasheet values, the turbine's data and the chosen winds and pivots) are listed with their sources, and a script checks every number in the text. Where a long piece of algebra was done by computer (SymPy, a program for symbolic algebra), the text says so and shows the steps a reader needs.

Conventions
SI units throughout. Gravity $g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, the standard value. A dot is a derivative with respect to time $t$; a prime is a derivative with respect to the rotor's angle $\varphi$ (section 1.5). The pendulum's angle $\theta$ is measured from the blade's outward direction, positive in the direction the rotor turns.
