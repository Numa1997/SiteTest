3 The best brake, and how much it gives

Equation (1.8) cannot be solved in closed form, but for small swings it can, and the answer holds the whole design rule: how hard the generator should brake, and how much power it then gets. The answer has the shape of an old question about batteries. A battery makes 12 V and has an inner resistance of 2 Ω; which lamp, connected to it, gets the most power? A bare wire carries a large current but has no resistance to turn it into heat, and gets nothing; an open gap carries no current, and gets nothing; in between, the lamp of 2 Ω gets the most, 18 W, the one whose resistance matches the battery's own. Nothing at either extreme, and a best value in between where the load matches the source: keep that shape in mind.

3.1 Small swings: a driven, braked oscillator

Hang a weight on a stiff spring and move your hand slowly up and down, far slower than the spring's own bounce. The weight simply follows your hand. Put the weight in honey and it follows a little late. It never bounces, because you never push at its own rhythm.

Let $\theta$ be small, so $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta) \approx \cos\varphi - \theta\sin\varphi$. Keep the first piece, gravity's push $G\cos\varphi$. The second piece, $-G\theta\sin\varphi$, makes the spring's stiffness go up and down once per turn. It is not small because the swing is small: beside the spring's own term $R\theta$ it has the size $G/R = 1/\Gamma$, up to {{n:invGam_max}} at 6 m/s. We may drop it because of when it acts. Multiplying a swing at once per turn by $\sin\varphi$ makes motion at zero and twice per turn, and that motion acts back on the once-per-turn swing only by passing through the same term a second time. So its effect is of second order, and section 4.1 measures it. Without it, (1.8) becomes

$$\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\,\theta = -G\cos\varphi. \tag{3.1}$$

This is the driven, damped oscillator: a spring of stiffness $R$ (natural frequency $\sqrt R$ per turn), a damper $\kappa$, and a push of amplitude $G$ at the frequency 1 per turn.

3.2 The swing

Once the start has died away the second blade swings at the frequency of the push. Write $\theta = \mathrm{Re}\,[A\,e^{i\varphi}]$, so that $\theta' \to iA$ and $\theta'' \to -A$. Then (3.1) is one line of algebra:

$$(R - 1 + i\kappa)A = -G \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi - \delta), \qquad \Theta = \frac{G}{\sqrt{(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{\kappa}{R-1}. \tag{3.2}$$

The $-1$ beside $R$ is the second blade's own inertia: shaking any body to and fro at the frequency 1 takes a torque proportional to its angular acceleration, $-\theta''$, which here works against the spring. So $R - 1$ is the spring less the inertia at the push's frequency, the net stiffness the push meets. (This is the $-1$ that the equal arms of section 1.4 cancel.) With no brake the second blade moves exactly in step with the push and swings by $G/(R-1)$; a brake makes the swing smaller and makes it lag the push by the angle $\delta$. Draw each torque as an arrow that turns once per turn: gravity's push is one arrow; the net stiffness's torque points along the swing; the brake's opposes the velocity, so it stands at right angles to the swing. The two must balance gravity's push, closing a right-angled triangle whose hypotenuse is gravity's arrow.

3.3 The harvested power

The generator takes the power $c\dot\theta^2$. Averaged over a turn, $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle = \Omega^2\langle\theta'^2\rangle = \Omega^2\Theta^2/2$, so

$$\bar P = \tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2\Theta^2 = I\Omega^3\,\frac{\kappa\,G^2}{2\left[(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2\right]}. \tag{3.3}$$

Read it as the battery. A weak brake ($\kappa \to 0$) is the open gap: the second blade swings freely, but nothing is taken. A strong brake ($\kappa \to \infty$) is the bare wire: it locks the second blade to the mother blade, nothing moves, and again nothing is taken. Between them lies a best brake. Set $d\bar P/d\kappa = 0$:

$$(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2 - 2\kappa^2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \kappa^* = |R - 1|. \tag{3.4}$$

The best brake equals the net stiffness: the load matches the source. At that setting $\tan\delta = 1$, and the second blade lags gravity's push by exactly 45°; in the triangle of torques the brake's arrow and the net stiffness's arrow are equal. Put $\kappa^*$ back into (3.3) and return to physical units with $I\Omega^2(R-1) = \left(\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p - I\right)\Omega^2$ and $I\Omega^2G = \tfrac{ml}{2}g$:

$$\boxed{P^* = \frac{\left(\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g\right)^2}{4\,\Omega\left(\tfrac{ml}{2}\,r_p - I\right)} = \frac{m\,g^2\,l}{8\,\Omega\left(r_p - \tfrac23 l\right)}.} \tag{3.5}$$

How strong is this brake? A spring of stiffness $R$ with a damper $\kappa$ just stops ringing when nudged, the brake called critical damping, at $\kappa = 2\sqrt R$. The best brake is $\kappa^*/(2\sqrt R)$ = {{n:zeta_star}} times that. Nudge the second blade and it does not ring; it creeps back. It does not need to ring, because it is pushed far below its own rhythm: it creeps after the moving bottom of the bowl, the marble in thick honey. The electrical twin is exact: an alternating supply feeds a resistor through a coil; the coil opposes the current with its reactance but takes no power, and the resistor takes the most when its resistance equals that reactance. The generator is the resistor, the net stiffness $R - 1$ the coil's reactance, and gravity's push the supply. So, unlike the lamp on the battery, the second blade wastes nothing at its best brake: the net stiffness stores energy and gives it back each swing, and all of $P^*$ goes into the generator.

Three results follow from (3.5).

The generator's torque. At the best brake the generator's torque swings with the amplitude

$$\tau^* = c^*\Omega\,\Theta^* = I\Omega^2(R-1)\cdot\frac{G}{\sqrt2\,(R-1)} \quad=\quad \frac{\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g}{\sqrt2},$$

{{n:tau_star}} N m for our second blade, at every wind. The triangle says why: at the best brake its two short sides are equal, so each is the hypotenuse, gravity's largest torque $\tfrac{ml}{2}g$, divided by $\sqrt2$. That is the torque the generator must be built to hold. The power still changes with the wind, because power is torque times angular speed, and the swing's angular speed, $\Omega\Theta^*$, falls as the rotor speeds up, since $\Theta^*$ falls as $1/\Omega^2$.

The best brake in physical units. $c^* = \kappa^*I\Omega = \left(\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p - I\right)\Omega$, and on a large rotor, where $r_p \gg l$,

$$c^* \approx \frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\Omega: \tag{3.6}$$

the first moment times the speed of the hinge. For our second blade the exact value is {{n:cst_6}} to {{n:cst_10}} N m s from 6 to 10 m/s, a hard brake for a small machine; chapter 5 finds that building it is the hardest part of the generator.

The power on a large rotor.

$$P^* \approx \frac{\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g^2}{4\,\Omega r_p} = \frac{\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g^2}{4\,v_{\rm tip}}. \tag{3.7}$$

It depends on the second blade only through its first moment, and on the turbine only through the speed of the hinge, $v_{\rm tip} = \Omega r_p = \lambda^*v$. The faster the hinge moves, the less power: the outward pull grows as $\Omega^2$ and holds the second blade stiffer, so gravity, which does not change, moves it less. The second blade gives the most in light wind.

3.4 On our blade, and how far to trust it

Worked example
Take 8 m/s, with the {{n:mB}} kg, {{n:lB}} m second blade at the 63 m tip. The rotor turns at $\Omega$ = {{n:acc8_om}} rad/s, one turn every {{n:turn8_s}} s. The stiffness is $R = 3r_p/2l$ = {{n:R}} and gravity's number is $G = 3g/(2l\Omega^2)$ = {{n:G_8}}. The best brake is $\kappa^* = R - 1$ = {{n:Rm1}}, that is $c^* = \kappa^* I\Omega$ = {{n:cst_8}} N m s. The swing is $\Theta^* = G/(\sqrt2\,\kappa^*)$ = {{n:Th_8}} rad, {{n:Th_8_deg}}°: the free end of the second blade sways {{n:Th_8_cm}} cm to either side of the mother blade's line, once every {{n:turn8_s}} s, a slow, gentle sway. The generator holds a torque swinging by {{n:tau_star}} N m, and (3.5) gives $P^*$ = {{n:Pst_8}} mW.

3.5 Resonance is out of reach

Any oscillator gives the most when it is pushed at its own rhythm, at resonance. Can the second blade get there? Its net stiffness $R - 1 = 3r_p/(2l) - 1$ falls to zero only for $l = \tfrac32 r_p$, a second blade half as long again as the mother blade: the equal-armed pendulum of section 1.4, whose bob is a point, sits exactly on it ($R = l_1/l_2 = 1$); a second blade on a turbine never comes near. For ours $R - 1$ = {{n:Rm1}}. The centrifugal spring of a body hinged at the tip of a long blade is always far stiffer than its own inertia at the push's frequency. To reach resonance, extra inertia would have to cancel it, for example a heavy flywheel turned by the generator through a gearbox (chapter 5): it would have to add about {{n:Rm1_round}} times the second blade's own moment of inertia. These notes do not pursue that, and chapter 1 says why one might not want to: near resonance, the double pendulum's chaos comes back.

What to remember
  • For small swings the second blade is a driven, braked oscillator: stiffness $R$, push $G$ once per turn, brake $\kappa$.
  • The best brake equals the net stiffness, $\kappa^* = R - 1$: the load matches the source. The swing then lags the push by 45°.
  • The best brake is about five times critical damping: the second blade does not ring, it creeps after the moving bottom of the bowl.
  • $P^* \approx \tfrac{ml}{2}g^2/(4v_{\rm tip})$: proportional to the first moment, and smaller in stronger wind. At 6, 8 and 10 m/s, {{n:Pst_6}}, {{n:Pst_8}} and {{n:Pst_10}} mW for 1 kg on 1 m.
  • The generator must hold $\tfrac{ml}{2}g/\sqrt2$ = {{n:tau_star}} N m at every wind.
Problems
  1. Show from (3.3) that a brake twice as strong as the best one, or half as strong, gives the same power. What fraction of $P^*$ is it?
    AnswerWith $x = \kappa/\kappa^*$, (3.3) and (3.5) give $\bar P/P^* = 2x/(1 + x^2)$, unchanged when $x \to 1/x$. At $x = 2$ or $\tfrac12$ it is 4/5, 80 % of $P^*$. The hump of Figure 6 is flat-topped: the brake need not be set exactly.
  2. At 10 m/s, how far does the free end of our second blade sway, and how much power does it give at the best brake?
    Answer$\Theta^* = G/(\sqrt2\,\kappa^*)$ with $G$ = {{n:G_10}}: {{n:Th_10}} rad, so the free end sways {{n:Th_10_cm}} cm either way. (3.5) gives {{n:Pst_10}} mW.
  3. The second blade's mass is doubled, at the same length. What happens to $P^*$, to the best brake $c^*$, and to the torque the generator must hold?
    AnswerAll three double. In (3.5) the first moment appears squared on top and once below; $c^*$ and $\tau^*$ are each proportional to the first moment.
