5 From swing to watts

Chapters 2 to 4 treated the generator as a perfect brake, $-c\dot\theta$, losing nothing. A real one is a small electric motor driven backwards, behind a gearbox. This chapter takes one from a catalogue, follows the power from the swinging second blade to the sensor's wires, and sizes the second blade to suit it. Figure {{fig:chain}} shows the chain the power runs along.

5.1 A motor driven backwards

A permanent-magnet motor is the generator of section 2.4 with two catalogue constants: turning at $\omega_m$ it makes the voltage $e = k_e\omega_m$, the back-EMF (electromotive force); a current $i$ gives the torque $k_ti$; and $k_t = k_e$ in SI units. (The coil's inductance delays the current by $L_c/R_c = 0.12$ ms, nothing next to a swing of seconds.) A gearbox of ratio $N$ makes the motor turn $N$ times per turn of the hinge, $\omega_m = N\dot\theta$, and hands its torque on multiplied by $N$. So the ratio enters twice:

$$e = k_eN\dot\theta, \quad i = \frac{e}{R_c + R_L}, \quad \text{torque at the hinge} = N k_t i \quad\Rightarrow\quad c_e = \frac{N^2\,k_t\,k_e}{R_c + R_L}. \tag{5.1}$$

The power $c_e\dot\theta^2 = ei = i^2(R_c + R_L)$ is shared in proportion to the resistances, and the load gets $R_L/(R_c + R_L)$. With $c_{\rm sc} = N^2k_tk_e/R_c$, the brake with the wires joined ($R_L = 0$, the strongest this motor can give), $c_e/c_{\rm sc} = R_c/(R_c + R_L)$ and

$$P_{\rm load} = c_e\left(1 - \frac{c_e}{c_{\rm sc}}\right)\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle. \tag{5.2}$$

Section 3.3's contest once more, inside the generator: near the short circuit it brakes hard but burns the power in its own coil; with a light load it sends nearly all to the load but takes little.

On our blade
The maxon RE 25, 25 mm across, 20 W, 24 V winding: $R_c = 1.53$ Ω, $k_t = 20.8$ mN m/A, speed constant 460 rpm/V, so $k_e = 60/(2\pi\cdot460) = 0.02076$ V s. Without a gearbox, $c_{\rm sc} = k_tk_e/R_c = {{n:csc1}}$ N m s; the best brake, $c^* = (R-1)I\Omega$ (3.4), is {{n:cstar_min}} to {{n:cstar_max}} N m s, some {{n:cstar_x}} times more, so $N \ge \sqrt{c^*/c_{\rm sc}}$ = {{n:Nneed_min}} to {{n:Nneed_max}} just to reach it with the wires joined. The reason is the shape of the job: the second blade pushes with {{n:tau_star}} N m while its hinge turns at {{n:w_hinge_lo}} to {{n:w_hinge_hi}} rad/s at most; the motor is built for a hundredth of that torque (rated 30.4 mN m) at thousands of rpm.

5.2 What the gearbox costs

A gearbox seen from the slow side
feel
Crank a hand-held egg beater: the beaters are light, yet the crank feels heavy when you start and stop it, because every turn of the crank spins them four times.
physics
Through a gear of ratio $N$, a torque on the fast side is felt $N$ times larger on the slow side, and an inertia $N^2$ times larger: once for the faster spin, once for the longer lever.
measure
Turn the hinge with a known torque, the motor's wires apart, and measure its angular acceleration: torque over acceleration is the inertia felt at the hinge, which includes $JN^2$.
maths
Kinetic energy $\tfrac12J(N\dot\theta)^2 = \tfrac12(JN^2)\dot\theta^2$: the motor's rotor counts as an inertia $JN^2$ at the hinge.

Three costs. Inertia: in the units of (1.8), where the second blade's own is $I$, the motor's rotor adds

$$j = \frac{J\,N^2}{I}. \tag{5.3}$$

It sits on the hinge's axis, so it feels neither the outward pull nor gravity, and the net stiffness becomes $R - 1 - j$. Friction: brushes and bearings resist with a torque $T_0$ that hardly depends on speed, felt as $NT_0$ at the hinge. Gear losses: a gearhead passes on the fraction $\eta$ of the power, whichever way it flows, so the brake felt at the hinge is $c_e/\eta$ and the friction $NT_0/\eta$ (section 5.4 does this exactly; this estimate leaves the small inertia term $j$ undivided, since $j \ll R - 1$). In (1.8):

$$(1 + j)\,\theta'' + \frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\theta' + f\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi + \theta) = 0, \qquad \kappa_e = \frac{c_e}{I\Omega},\quad f = \frac{N\,T_0}{\eta\,I\Omega^2}. \tag{5.4}$$

Here $f/G = NT_0/(\eta\tfrac{ml}{2}g)$ compares the friction with gravity's largest push, the same at every wind. If $f \ge G$ gravity can never move a second blade at rest: the gearbox has jammed it.

On our blade
Rotor $J = 14.7$ g cm², and the GP 32 C gearhead adds 0.7 to 1.5 g cm² at its input (a planetary gearhead: small gears, the planets, run round a central sun inside a ring; stages in a row multiply their ratios). Through the largest ratio, 492 : 1, $j = {{n:j_492}}$, against $R - 1 = {{n:Rm1}}$: nothing. Running free with no load, the motor's whole torque goes into its own friction, so the no-load current, 40.2 mA at 10 900 rpm, gives $T_0 \approx k_ti_0 = {{n:T0_mNm}}$ mN m. Catalogue efficiencies: 80, 75, 70 and 60 % for one to four stages. Through 492 : 1 (four stages) the friction at the hinge is {{n:fric_492}} N m, {{n:fricG_492}} % of $\tfrac{ml}{2}g = {{n:mlg}}$ N m; jamming would need a ratio above {{n:Njam}} on three stages and {{n:Njam4}} on four: no catalogue ratio jams it.

Two numbers are soft. The catalogue's efficiency is for driving forwards at the best load; driven backwards and lightly loaded, a gearhead can do worse. And friction measured at 10 900 rpm includes losses that grow with speed, so at our slow speeds it may be lower. The first makes the results optimistic, the second pessimistic; a bench test settles both. Section 5.4 tries both ways.

5.3 Friction as a brake that weakens

The describing function
feel
Push a heavy box across the floor: the floor charges the same force whether you walk or run, the same toll for every metre. A dynamo charges more the faster you go.
physics
A swing at one frequency only feels the part of a torque at that frequency. Replace the friction by the damper that has the same part: a linear brake, but one whose strength depends on the swing's size.
measure
Energy per cycle (from one end to the other and back, a path of $4\Theta$): friction takes $4f\Theta$, a damper $\pi\kappa\Theta^2$. Equal when $\kappa = 4f/(\pi\Theta)$.
maths
$f\,\mathrm{sgn}(\sin x)$ has the first Fourier coefficient $\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\,\mathrm{sgn}(\sin x)\sin x\,dx$ $= \frac{2f}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx$ $= \frac{4f}{\pi}$.

With $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi-\delta)$ the friction acts, at the swing's frequency (Figure {{fig:friction}}), like a damper of

$$\kappa_f = \frac{4f}{\pi\,\Theta}, \tag{5.5}$$

a brake that weakens as the swing grows. Now the triangle of Figure {{fig:torques}} still closes: its stiffness side is $(R-1-j)\Theta$, its brake side $(\kappa_e/\eta)\Theta + 4f/\pi$, its long side $G$. Pythagoras:

$$\Theta^2(R - 1 - j)^2 + \left(\frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\Theta + \frac{4f}{\pi}\right)^2 = G^2, \tag{5.6}$$

a quadratic for $\Theta$ with a positive root only if $4f/\pi < G$, a slightly stricter jamming condition. The load gets (5.2) with $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle = \Omega^2\Theta^2/2$; the rotor pays for the brake and for friction's toll, torque times the mean speed $\langle|\theta'|\rangle = 2\Theta/\pi$:

$$P_{\rm load} = I\Omega^3\,\kappa_e\left(1 - \frac{c_e}{c_{\rm sc}}\right)\frac{\Theta^2}{2}, \qquad P_{\rm rotor} = I\Omega^3\left[\frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\frac{\Theta^2}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\,f\,\Theta\right]. \tag{5.7}$$
Check
Remove the losses, $f = j = 0$, $\eta = 1$, $c_{\rm sc}\to\infty$: (5.6) is (3.2) and both powers in (5.7) are (3.3). Units: $\kappa_e$, $j$, $f$, $G$ are pure numbers and $I\Omega^3$ is a power.

5.4 On our blade

The design takes two steps. First the formulas (5.6) and (5.7) pick, at each wind and each catalogue ratio, the load resistance that gives the load the most power. Two limits apply: the gearhead's continuous torque (6 N m for the largest ratios) and a swing below 0.5 rad (the best settings swing at most {{n:g4_th_max}} rad). Ratios that jam are out.

Then the full equation is solved at those settings, with the gear loss charged on every flow of power. Let $\tau_m$ be the torque the motor side would need through a perfect gear, in the units $I\Omega^2$ of (1.8), with $f_0 = NT_0/(I\Omega^2)$ its friction. When $\tau_m$ and $\theta'$ have the same sign, the motor side resists the swing and power flows from the second blade into the motor; the second blade then feels $\tau_m/\eta$, the gearhead's loss added. When they have opposite signs, the motor's inertia is giving energy back, and only $\eta\tau_m$ arrives:

$$\theta'' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi + \theta) + \nu\,\tau_m = 0, \qquad \tau_m = j\,\theta'' + f_0\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta' + \kappa_e\,\theta', \qquad \nu = \eta^{-\mathrm{sgn}(\tau_m\theta')}. \tag{5.8}$$

Since $\theta''$ appears on both sides, each step solves for it (the left side grows steadily with $\theta''$: one answer). Each sign is smoothed over a narrow band, and the rotor's work is integrated so the energy book closes.

5.5 Sizing the second blade to its generator

The power and the best brake both grow with the first moment, $c^* \approx \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega$. A given generator brakes only so hard, so it has a second blade it serves best: a lighter one takes little; a heavier one asks for more brake or torque than the motor and gearhead can give.

On our blade
With the length fixed at {{n:lB}} m, the formula of section 5.3 at each mass of the series 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 kg, best ratio and load within the gearhead's rating, gives these shares of $P^*$ (mean over the three winds): {{table:sizing}} The share does not rise smoothly with the mass, because the gearhead comes only in a set of catalogue ratios (the table's third column jumps from one to the next): a mass whose ideal ratio falls between two of them must take the nearer one and loses a little, and at 0.5 kg the formula even takes a smaller ratio at 6 m/s: there the best brake is weakest ($c^* \propto \Omega$), the smaller ratio already brakes enough, and it carries half the friction ($NT_0$ at the hinge). The share peaks at {{n:mB}} kg, the mass these notes use. Lighter second blades lose a larger share to the motor's friction (at 0.5 kg the friction is twice as large against gravity's push); heavier ones ask for more brake than the motor gives (at 2 kg even the short circuit at 492 : 1 falls below the best brake at 10 m/s), and beyond 2 kg the largest ratio would exceed the gearhead's continuous torque. With twice the friction and a gear efficiency of 50 %, it still gives the sensor {{n:design_margin}} times its need at the poorest wind.

5.6 Bigger second blades

Ours is light for a blade tip: the NREL blade weighs {{n:tip_kgm}} kg per metre at its very tip and {{n:kgm_1m_in}} kg per metre a metre further in, and its outer 3 m weigh {{n:outer3_kg}} kg. Real tip devices are of the same build: retrofit tip extensions about three metres long reaching about one metre past the old tip (Vestas, US 10 927 809), and the tip brakes of older stall-regulated turbines (turbines that limit their power by letting the flow tear away from the blades in strong wind, rather than by turning the blades), outer blade sections that turn across the wind to stop the rotor, 5 to 20 % of the rotor radius long (US 8 403 641). What to remember
  • A motor driven backwards is a brake, $c_e = N^2k_tk_e/(R_c + R_L)$; the load gets $R_L/(R_c + R_L)$ of it, so a stronger brake and a larger share pull against each other.
  • A gearbox turns a small, fast motor into a strong, slow brake; seen from the hinge it multiplies torque by $N$ and inertia by $N^2$, and it costs friction and gear losses.
  • Friction is a brake that weakens as the swing grows, $\kappa_f = 4f/(\pi\Theta)$; with it the triangle of torques still closes, (5.6).
  • Through a catalogue motor and gearhead the load receives {{n:g4_min}} to {{n:g4_max}} mW, {{n:g4_eff_min}} to {{n:g4_eff_max}} % of what the rotor pays; the gearhead takes the largest share.
  • Bigger second blades give proportionally more, but need a generator that holds a proportionally larger torque.
Problems
  1. Without a gearbox, the RE 25's strongest brake is {{n:csc1}} N m s. What gear ratio does it need, at the least, to reach the best brake of 6 and of 10 m/s?
    AnswerThe brake grows as $N^2$, so $N \ge \sqrt{c^*/c_{\rm sc}}$: {{n:Nneed_min}} at 6 m/s and {{n:Nneed_max}} at 10 m/s. And that is with the wires joined, when the coil burns everything; a load needs a still larger ratio.
  2. With the wires joined ($R_L = 0$), how much power reaches the load? And with the wires apart ($R_L \to \infty$)?
    AnswerNone either way. Joined, the brake is strongest but all its power heats the coil; apart, no current flows and the motor does not brake at all. In between lies a best load, the contest of section 3.3 once more.
  3. The motor's friction proved ten times the catalogue's estimate. Would the four-stage gearhead at 492 : 1 jam the second blade?
    AnswerAt the catalogue's friction it jams only beyond a ratio of {{n:Njam4}}; the jamming ratio falls in proportion to the friction, so ten times the friction jams it beyond about a tenth of that, below 492. Yes, it would jam. A bench test of the friction comes first.
  4. Show that the energy-per-cycle argument and the Fourier argument give the same $\kappa_f$.
    AnswerPer cycle the swing travels $4\Theta$, so friction takes $4f\Theta$; a damper $\kappa$ takes $\oint\kappa\theta'\,d\theta = \kappa\Theta^2\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2 = \pi\kappa\Theta^2$. Equal for $\kappa = 4f/(\pi\Theta)$, which is $4f/\pi$, the first Fourier coefficient, divided by $\Theta$.
