5 From swing to watts

The second blade now has its rule: brake it at $c^* \approx \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega$ and it gives $P^*$, a fifth of a watt, which the rotor pays for through a slight trail. Chapters 2 to 4 treated the generator as a perfect brake, a torque $-c\dot\theta$ that takes the power $c\dot\theta^2$ and loses none of it. A real one is a small electric motor driven backwards, behind a gearbox. This chapter takes one from a catalogue, puts it in the hinge, follows the power from the swinging second blade to the sensor's wires, and then sizes the second blade to suit it. After it, the question of the steady wind is answered, and Part II turns to the real one.

5.1 A motor driven backwards

A small motor with permanent magnets is the generator of section 2.4 with two named constants. Turning its shaft at the angular speed $\omega_m$ induces the voltage $e = k_e\,\omega_m$ in its coil; in a motor this voltage pushes back against the supply, so it is called the back-EMF (electromotive force). A current $i$ in the coil gives a torque $k_t i$ on the shaft. The two constants are equal in SI units, because the electrical power $ei$ and the mechanical power $k_ti\,\omega_m$ are the same power. With a load resistor $R_L$ and the coil's own resistance $R_c$, the torque is $k_tk_e\,\omega_m/(R_c + R_L)$, the brake (2.4). (The coil's inductance would delay the current by $L_c/R_c = 0.12$ ms, nothing next to a swing that lasts seconds, and is left out.)

Between the second blade and the motor sits a gearbox of ratio $N$: the motor turns $N$ times for each turn of the hinge, $\omega_m = N\dot\theta$, and its torque reaches the second blade multiplied by $N$. The ratio therefore enters twice, once in the voltage and once in the torque, and the brake constant at the hinge is

$$c_e = \frac{N^2\,k_t\,k_e}{R_c + R_L}. \tag{5.1}$$

The power $c_e\dot\theta^2$ is the electrical power $ei$, and the current shares it between the coil and the load in proportion to their resistances. The load gets the fraction $R_L/(R_c + R_L)$. Call $c_{\rm sc} = N^2k_tk_e/R_c$ the brake with the wires joined ($R_L = 0$, the "short circuit"): it is the strongest brake this motor can give through this gearbox. Since $R_L/(R_c+R_L) = 1 - c_e/c_{\rm sc}$,

$$P_{\rm load} = c_e\left(1 - \frac{c_e}{c_{\rm sc}}\right)\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle. \tag{5.2}$$

This is section 3.3's contest once more, now inside the generator: a brake near the short circuit takes plenty of power but burns it in its own coil; a light brake sends nearly all of it to the load but takes little.

On our blade
Take the maxon RE 25, a motor 25 mm across, rated 20 W, in its 24 V winding: $R_c = 1.53$ Ω, $k_t = 20.8$ mN m/A, and a speed constant of 460 rpm/V, which is $k_e = 60/(2\pi\cdot460) = 0.02076$ V s; the two constants agree to the catalogue's rounding, two parts in a thousand. Without a gearbox its strongest brake is $c_{\rm sc} = k_tk_e/R_c = {{n:csc1}}$ N m s. The best brake of chapter 3, (3.6), is {{n:cstar_min}} to {{n:cstar_max}} N m s: some {{n:cstar_x}} times more. Because the brake grows as $N^2$, a ratio of at least {{n:Nneed_min}} to {{n:Nneed_max}} is needed just to reach the best brake with the wires joined. The reason is the shape of the job. At the best brake the second blade pushes with {{n:tau_star}} N m (section 3.3) while its hinge turns at only {{n:w_hinge_lo}} to {{n:w_hinge_hi}} rad/s at its fastest; a small motor is built to give a hundredth of that torque (the RE 25's rated torque is 30.4 mN m) at thousands of rpm. The gearbox converts one into the other.

5.2 What the gearbox costs

Turn the crank of a hand-held egg beater. The beaters are light, yet the crank feels heavy when you start and stop it, because each turn of the crank spins the beaters four times. Seen from the slow side, everything on the fast side of a gearbox is magnified: a torque by $N$, and an inertia by $N^2$.

The gearbox that makes the brake strong enough brings three costs.

Inertia. The motor's rotor, of moment of inertia $J$, turns at $N\dot\theta$, so its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12JN^2\dot\theta^2$: to the second blade it is an extra moment of inertia $JN^2$ about the hinge. In the units of (1.8), where the second blade's own is $I$, it adds

$$j = \frac{J\,N^2}{I}. \tag{5.3}$$

It does not feel the centrifugal pull or gravity, because the motor's rotor sits on the hinge's axis; it only resists being accelerated. In (3.2) the second blade's inertia gave the $-1$ of the net stiffness $R - 1$; the rotor adds its own, and the net stiffness becomes $R - 1 - j$.

Friction. Brushes and bearings resist turning with a torque $T_0$ that hardly depends on speed. Through the gearbox it reaches the second blade as $NT_0$.

Gear losses. A gearhead's efficiency $\eta$ is the fraction of the power it passes on, whichever way the power flows. While the second blade drives the motor, every torque the motor side needs costs the second blade $N/\eta$ times that torque, not $N$ times; while the motor's spinning rotor slows down and gives its energy back, the second blade receives only $\eta$ of it. In the formula below we keep the simple part: the brake the second blade feels is $c_e/\eta$ and the friction $NT_0/\eta$, and the rotor's inertia passes without loss. The full equation of section 5.4 charges the loss on every flow.

Put the three into (1.8): the rotor's inertia joins $\theta''$, the brake becomes $\kappa_e/\eta$, and the friction is a torque of constant size $f$ that always opposes the swing:

$$(1 + j)\,\theta'' + \frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\theta' + f\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi + \theta) = 0, \qquad \kappa_e = \frac{c_e}{I\Omega},\quad f = \frac{N\,T_0}{\eta\,I\Omega^2}. \tag{5.4}$$

In these units gravity's largest torque, $\tfrac{ml}{2}g$, is $G$, so $f/G = NT_0/(\eta\,\tfrac{ml}{2}g)$ compares the friction with the push that drives the swing, and it is the same at every wind. If $f \ge G$ the friction is stronger than gravity can ever push, and a second blade at rest never moves: the gearbox has jammed it.

On our blade
The RE 25's rotor has $J = 14.7$ g cm², and the GP 32 C gearhead adds 0.7 to 1.5 g cm² at its input. (A planetary gearhead is a compact gearbox in which small gear wheels, the planets, run round a central one, the sun, inside a ring; several such stages in a row multiply their ratios.) Through the largest catalogue ratio, 492 : 1, that gives $j = {{n:j_492}}$: against a net stiffness $R - 1 = {{n:Rm1}}$, nothing. The catalogue gives no friction torque; the no-load current, 40.2 mA, gives an estimate $T_0 \approx k_ti_0 = {{n:T0_mNm}}$ mN m, measured at 10 900 rpm. The gearhead's catalogue efficiency is 80 % for one stage, 75 % for two, 70 % for three and 60 % for four. Through 492 : 1 (four stages) the friction at the hinge is {{n:fric_492}} N m, {{n:fricG_492}} % of gravity's largest torque $\tfrac{ml}{2}g = {{n:mlg}}$ N m. At the catalogue's friction the second blade would jam only beyond a ratio of {{n:Njam}} on a three-stage gearhead and {{n:Njam4}} on a four-stage one: no catalogue ratio jams it.

Two of these numbers are less certain than the rest. The catalogue gives the gearhead's best forward efficiency, driven from the motor at its best load; driven backwards and lightly loaded, a gearhead can do worse. And friction measured at 10 900 rpm includes losses that grow with speed, so at the slow speeds here it may be lower. The first makes the numbers below optimistic, the second pessimistic. Only a test on a bench can settle them.

5.3 Friction as a brake that weakens

Push a heavy box across the floor. The floor pushes back with the same force whether you walk or run, so it takes the same toll for every metre. A brake like the dynamo is different: it pushes back harder the faster you go.

Equation (5.4) is no longer linear, because of $\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta'$, but chapter 3's method still works with one more step. Suppose the second blade still swings as $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi - \delta)$. The friction torque $f\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta'$ is then a square wave, $+f$ while the blade moves one way and $-f$ while it moves back, in step with $\theta'$. A square wave of height $f$ is a sum of sine waves at frequencies 1, 3, 5, …, the first of amplitude $4f/\pi$ and the others of $1/3$, $1/5$, … of it. Keep only the first, the one at the frequency of the swing. A damper $\kappa_f\theta'$ gives a torque of amplitude $\kappa_f\Theta$ in step with $\theta'$, so the friction acts like a damper with

$$\kappa_f = \frac{4f}{\pi\,\Theta}. \tag{5.5}$$

This is called the describing function of friction. It is a brake that weakens as the swing grows, since the toll per swing is fixed but the swing is longer. In (3.2), replace $R - 1$ by $R - 1 - j$ and $\kappa$ by $\kappa_e/\eta + \kappa_f$, and multiply through by $\Theta$:

$$\Theta^2(R - 1 - j)^2 + \left(\frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\Theta + \frac{4f}{\pi}\right)^2 = G^2. \tag{5.6}$$

This is a quadratic equation for $\Theta$. It has a positive root only if $4f/\pi < G$, a slightly stricter form of the jamming condition above. The power the load receives is (5.2) with $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle = \Omega^2\Theta^2/2$, and the rotor pays for everything the second blade loses, in the coil, the load, the gears and the friction:

$$P_{\rm load} = I\Omega^3\,\kappa_e\left(1 - \frac{c_e}{c_{\rm sc}}\right)\frac{\Theta^2}{2}, \qquad P_{\rm rotor} = I\Omega^3\left[\frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\frac{\Theta^2}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\,f\,\Theta\right]. \tag{5.7}$$

The last term is the friction's toll: torque times the mean speed $\langle|\theta'|\rangle = 2\Theta/\pi$.

Check
Remove the losses: $f = 0$, $j = 0$, $\eta = 1$ and $c_{\rm sc}\to\infty$ (a coil without resistance). Then (5.6) is (3.2), and both powers in (5.7) become (3.3). Units: $\kappa_e$, $j$, $f$ and $G$ are pure numbers, and $I\Omega^3$ is a power.

5.4 On our blade

Formulas (5.6) and (5.7) give, at each wind and each catalogue ratio of the GP 32 C, the load resistor that gives the load the most power, with the peak torque at the hinge within the torque the gearhead may carry continuously (6 N m for the largest ratios) and the swing below 0.5 rad, the range in which these notes use the formula (the best settings swing at most {{n:g4_th_max}} rad, so that limit never bites); ratios that jam are out. Then, as a second route, the full equation is solved with the method of section 4.1. It is (5.4) with the gear loss charged on every power that flows through the gearhead. Let $x$ be the torque the motor side needs, as felt through a perfect gear (in the units of (5.4), and without $\eta$ in the friction: $f_0 = NT_0/(I\Omega^2)$); the second blade feels $x/\eta$ while power flows into the motor side and $\eta x$ while it flows back:

$$\theta'' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi + \theta) + k\,x = 0, \qquad x = j\,\theta'' + f_0\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta' + \kappa_e\,\theta', \qquad k = \eta^{-\mathrm{sgn}(x\theta')}. \tag{5.8}$$

Because $\theta''$ appears on both sides, each step solves for it (the left side grows steadily with $\theta''$, so there is exactly one answer). Each sign is smoothed over a narrow band: a thousandth of the swing for $\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta'$, and a thousandth of the gear's own power scale for $\mathrm{sgn}(x\theta')$. The full equation is run at the formula's brake and at half and twice it, at every wind, and at 8 m/s with every other ratio at its own best brake; the rotor's work is integrated too, so the energy book closes in the full equation as well.

Turning that into the steady few volts a radio chip needs is a problem of electronics that these notes do not solve. The margins above are therefore before the electronics, and a bench test of the whole chain is the next step.

5.5 Sizing the second blade to its generator

Chapter 3 says the power grows with the second blade's first moment, and (3.6) says the best brake grows with it too: $c^* \approx \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega$. A given generator can brake only so hard, so for a given generator there is a second blade it serves best. Too light a second blade asks for a brake the motor gives easily, but takes little; too heavy a one asks for more brake than the motor can give, or more torque than its gearhead may carry, and the motor takes only a small share of what the second blade could give.

On our blade
With the length fixed at {{n:lB}} m, the formula of section 5.3, at each mass of the series 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 kg and at each wind, with the best ratio and load within the gearhead's rating, gives the load these shares of $P^*$ (mean over the three winds): {{table:sizing}} The share of $P^*$ that reaches the load peaks at {{n:mB}} kg, which is the mass these notes use: the lightest second blade whose best brake this motor can reach. Beyond 2 kg the largest ratio would exceed the gearhead's continuous torque, and the share collapses. With twice the catalogue's friction and a gear efficiency of 50 %, the {{n:mB}} kg second blade still gives the sensor {{n:design_margin}} times its need at the poorest wind.

5.6 Bigger second blades

Hang a ruler and a plank side by side from the rim of a turning wheel. Both lean back by the same few degrees, because gravity's push and the outward pull both grow with how much mass sits how far from the hinge. But to hold the plank against its swing you need a far stronger hand than for the ruler.

Our second blade is light for a blade tip. The NREL blade itself weighs {{n:tip_kgm}} kg per metre at its very tip, and its outer 3 m weigh {{n:outer3_kg}} kg. Real devices fitted at blade tips are of the same build: retrofit tip extensions reach about one metre past the old tip and are about three metres long in all (a Vestas patent, US 10 927 809, in the sources of section 9.3), and the tip brakes of older turbines, the outer metres of each blade mounted on a shaft so that they could turn across the wind and stop the rotor in a storm, on turbines that limit their power in strong wind by letting their blades stall rather than by turning them, are 5 to 20 % of the rotor radius long (US patent 8 403 641). So it is worth asking what the formulas of chapters 3 and 5 give over that range. Table 5 runs them for lengths from half a metre to three metres and for four mass densities: a light hollow sensor blade (1 kg per metre), a light composite (3 kg per metre), the mother blade's own tip (10 kg per metre) and its build a metre further in (30 kg per metre).

Second, the swing does not change: $\Theta^*$ at 6 m/s stays between {{n:sz_th_lo}} and {{n:sz_th_hi}} rad for every size. The reason is in (3.2): at the best brake $\Theta^* = G/[\sqrt2(R-1)]$, and gravity's push $G$ and the centrifugal stiffness $R$ both carry the same factor $\tfrac{ml}{2}/I$, so their ratio is $g/(\Omega^2 r_p)$ for any second blade much shorter than its mother blade, which gives {{n:sz_th_ref}} rad. Third, the torque the generator must hold, $\tau^*$, grows with the first moment too, from {{n:sz_tau_lo}} to {{n:sz_tau_hi}} N m. The RE 25 behind its gearhead cannot follow: once the first moment passes about half a kilogram-metre, it delivers {{n:sz_plat_lo}} to {{n:sz_plat_hi}} mW at 6 m/s whatever the size, because the gearhead's continuous torque, not the second blade, sets the limit. A bigger second blade needs a bigger generator, sized by $\tau^*$ and $c^*$ as in section 5.5. The largest blades of the table also pull on the mother blade's tip with up to {{n:sz_pull_max}} kN at 10 m/s, close to the pull of its own outer 3 m, {{n:outer3_pull}} kN; such a device would need a check of the blade's structure, which these notes do not make. Ours adds {{n:sz_pull_ours_N}} N.

For the minimal sensor, every size in the table is more than enough: through the RE 25 the poorest case, the smallest blade in the strongest wind, still gives {{n:sz_min_margin}} times its need. So the size is not set by that sensor. These notes choose {{n:mB}} kg on {{n:lB}} m because at that mass the catalogue generator passes the largest share of $P^*$ to its load (section 5.5). That is not the most power: Table 4 shows the load's power still rising up to 2 kg, at a smaller share, before the gearhead's torque limit stops it. A sensor that needs more than about a tenth of a watt would take the heavier second blade, or a larger generator.

What to remember
  • A motor driven backwards is a brake: $c_e = N^2k_tk_e/(R_c + R_L)$. The load gets the share $R_L/(R_c + R_L)$ of what it brakes, so a stronger brake and a larger share pull against each other.
  • A gearbox turns a small, fast motor into a strong, slow brake. It costs inertia (negligible here), friction (a brake that weakens as the swing grows) and gear losses.
  • Through a catalogue motor and gearhead the load receives {{n:g4_min}} to {{n:g4_max}} mW, {{n:g4_eff_min}} to {{n:g4_eff_max}} % of what the rotor pays.
  • Bigger second blades give proportionally more, but need a generator that holds a proportionally larger torque.
Problems
  1. Without a gearbox, the RE 25's strongest brake is {{n:csc1}} N m s. What gear ratio does it need, at the least, to reach the best brake of 6 and of 10 m/s?
    AnswerThe brake grows as $N^2$, so $N \ge \sqrt{c^*/c_{\rm sc}}$: {{n:Nneed_min}} at 6 m/s and {{n:Nneed_max}} at 10 m/s. And that is with the wires joined, when the coil burns everything; a load needs a still larger ratio.
  2. With the wires joined ($R_L = 0$), how much power reaches the load? And with the wires apart ($R_L \to \infty$)?
    AnswerNone either way. Joined, the brake is strongest but all its power heats the coil; apart, no current flows and the motor does not brake at all. In between lies a best load, the contest of section 3.3 once more.
  3. The motor's friction proved ten times the catalogue's estimate. Would the four-stage gearhead at 492 : 1 jam the second blade?
    AnswerAt the catalogue's friction it jams only beyond a ratio of {{n:Njam4}}; the jamming ratio falls in proportion to the friction, so ten times the friction jams it beyond about a tenth of that, below 492. Yes, it would jam: the friction would hold the second blade still against gravity's push. A bench test of the friction comes first.
  4. How fast does the motor turn at most, through 492 : 1, and why is that slow for a motor?
    AnswerAt most {{n:motor_rpm_max}} rpm, at 6 m/s, where the swing is fastest. Small motors are built for thousands of rpm; here even a 492-fold gearbox leaves it turning slowly, which is why its voltage peaks at only about a volt.
