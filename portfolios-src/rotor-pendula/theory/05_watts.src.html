7 From swing to watts

Every chapter so far has treated the generator as a perfect brake: a torque $-c\dot\theta$ that takes the power $c\dot\theta^2$ and loses none of it. A real generator is a small electric motor driven backwards, behind a gearbox. This chapter takes one from a catalogue, puts it at the pivot, and follows the power from the swinging rod to the wires of the sensor.

7.1 A generator is a brake

A bicycle dynamo pressed against the tyre makes the wheel harder to turn when the lamp is lit, and harder still if you join its two wires together; with the wires loose it spins almost freely. What pushes back on the wheel is the electric current.

A small motor with permanent magnets has two constants. Turning its shaft at angular speed $\omega_m$ induces a voltage $e = k_e\,\omega_m$ in its coil (the back-EMF). A current $I$ in the coil gives a torque $k_t I$ on the shaft. The two constants are equal in SI units, because the electrical power $eI$ and the mechanical power $k_tI\,\omega_m$ are the same power. Connect a load resistor $R_L$: the current is $I = e/(R_c + R_L)$, where $R_c$ is the resistance of the coil itself. The torque on the shaft is then $k_tk_e\,\omega_m/(R_c + R_L)$, proportional to the speed: exactly the brake of chapter 1. (The coil's inductance would delay the current by $L_c/R_c = 0.12$ ms, nothing next to a swing that lasts seconds, and is left out.)

Between the pendulum and the motor sits a gearbox of ratio $N$: the motor turns $N$ times for each turn of the pendulum's shaft, $\omega_m = N\dot\theta$, and its torque reaches the pendulum multiplied by $N$. The ratio therefore enters twice, once in the voltage and once in the torque, and the brake constant at the pendulum is

$$c_e = \frac{N^2\,k_t\,k_e}{R_c + R_L}. \tag{7.1}$$

The power $c_e\dot\theta^2$ is the electrical power $eI$, and the current shares it between the coil and the load in proportion to their resistances. The load gets the fraction $R_L/(R_c + R_L)$. Call $c_{\rm sc} = N^2k_tk_e/R_c$ the brake with the wires joined ($R_L = 0$, the "short circuit"): it is the strongest brake this motor can give through this gearbox. Since $R_L/(R_c+R_L) = 1 - c_e/c_{\rm sc}$,

$$P_{\rm load} = c_e\left(1 - \frac{c_e}{c_{\rm sc}}\right)\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle. \tag{7.2}$$

This is section 2.3's contest once more, now inside the generator: a brake near the short circuit takes plenty of power but burns it in its own coil; a light brake sends nearly all of it to the load but takes little.

On our blade
Take the maxon RE 25, a 25 mm, 20 W motor, in its 24 V winding (catalogue part 339152): $R_c = 1.53$ Ω, $k_t = 20.8$ mN m/A, and a speed constant of 460 rpm/V, which is $k_e = 60/(2\pi\cdot460) = 0.02076$ V s; the two constants agree, as they must. Without a gearbox its strongest brake is $c_{\rm sc} = k_tk_e/R_c = {{n:csc1}}$ N m s. The best brake of chapter 2, $c^* = mL^2\Omega\,|R-1|$, ranges from {{n:cstar_min}} N m s (400 rpm, pivot at 30 %) to {{n:cstar_max}} N m s: over a thousand times more. Because the brake grows as $N^2$, a ratio of at least {{n:Nneed_min}} to {{n:Nneed_max}} is needed just to reach the best brake with the wires joined. The reason is the shape of the job. At the best brake the pendulum pushes with {{n:torque_star}} N m (section 2.3) while its shaft turns at well under a radian per second; a small motor is built to give a few hundredths of that torque at thousands of rpm. The gearbox converts one into the other.

7.2 What the gearbox costs

Turn the crank of a hand-held egg beater. The beaters are light, yet the crank feels heavy when you start and stop it, because each turn of the crank spins the beaters four times. Seen from the slow side, everything on the fast side of a gearbox is magnified: a torque by $N$, and an inertia by $N^2$.

The gearbox that makes the brake strong enough brings three costs.

Inertia. The motor's rotor, of moment of inertia $J$, turns at $N\dot\theta$, so its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12JN^2\dot\theta^2$: to the pendulum it is an extra moment of inertia $JN^2$ about the pivot. In the units of chapter 1, where the bob's own is $mL^2$, it adds

$$j = \frac{J\,N^2}{m\,L^2}. \tag{7.3}$$

It does not feel the centrifugal pull or gravity, because the rotor's mass sits on the pivot's axis; it only resists being accelerated. In (2.2) the bob's inertia gave the $-1$ of the net stiffness $R - 1$; the rotor adds its own, and the net stiffness becomes $R - 1 - j$.

Friction. Brushes and bearings resist turning with a torque $T_0$ that hardly depends on speed. Through the gearbox it reaches the pendulum as $NT_0$.

Gear losses. A gearhead's efficiency $\eta$ is the fraction of the power it passes on, whichever way the power flows. While the pendulum drives the motor, every torque the motor side needs costs the pendulum $N/\eta$ times that torque, not $N$ times; while the motor's spinning rotor slows down and gives its energy back, the pendulum receives only $\eta$ of it. The teeth rub whatever the power is doing, harvested or only sloshing back and forth. In the formula below we keep the simple part: the brake the pendulum feels is $c_e/\eta$ and the friction $NT_0/\eta$, and the rotor's inertia passes without loss. The full equation of section 7.4 charges the loss on every flow, the sloshing included, and section 7.5 shows where that matters.

Put the three into the pendulum's equation (1.5): the rotor's inertia joins $\theta''$, the brake becomes $\kappa_e/\eta$, and the friction is a torque of constant size $f$ that always opposes the swing:

$$(1 + j)\,\theta'' + \frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\theta' + f\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi + \theta) = 0, \qquad \kappa_e = \frac{c_e}{mL^2\Omega},\quad f = \frac{N\,T_0}{\eta\,mL^2\Omega^2}. \tag{7.4}$$

In these units gravity's largest torque, $mgL$, is $G$, so $f/G = NT_0/(\eta\,mgL)$ compares the friction with the push that drives the swing, and it is the same on every rotor. If $f \ge G$ the friction is stronger than gravity can ever push, and a pendulum at rest never moves: the gearbox has jammed it.

On our blade
The RE 25's rotor has $J = 14.7$ g cm², and the GP 32 C planetary gearhead adds 0.7 to 1.5 g cm² at its input. Through a ratio of 51 that gives $j = {{n:j_51}}$: a rotor 25 mm across weighs on the swing as much as the 100 g bob on its 20 cm rod. The catalogue gives no friction torque; the no-load current, 40.2 mA, gives an estimate $T_0 \approx k_tI_0 = {{n:T0_mNm}}$ mN m, measured at 10 900 rpm. The gearhead's catalogue efficiency is 80 % for one stage, 75 % for two, 70 % for three and 60 % for four. Through 51 : 1 (three stages) the friction at the pendulum is {{n:fric_51}} N m, {{n:fricG_51}} % of gravity's largest torque $mgL = {{n:mgL}}$ N m. The pendulum jams beyond a ratio of {{n:Njam}} on a three-stage gearhead, so the catalogue ratios 190, 246 and 492 are useless here, on every rotor.

Two of these numbers are less certain than the rest. The catalogue gives the gearhead's best forward efficiency, driven from the motor at its best load; driven backwards and lightly loaded, a gearhead can do worse. And friction measured at 10 900 rpm includes losses that grow with speed, so at the slow speeds here it may be lower. The first makes the numbers below optimistic, the second pessimistic. Only a test on a bench can settle them.

7.3 Friction as a brake that weakens

Push a heavy box across the floor. The floor pushes back with the same force whether you walk or run, so it takes the same toll for every metre. A brake like the dynamo is different: it pushes back harder the faster you go.

Equation (7.4) is no longer linear, because of $\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta'$, but chapter 2's method still works with one more step. Suppose the rod still swings as $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi - \delta)$. The friction torque $f\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta'$ is then a square wave, $+f$ while the rod moves one way and $-f$ while it moves back, in step with $\theta'$. A square wave of height $f$ is a sum of sine waves at frequencies 1, 3, 5, …, the first of amplitude $4f/\pi$ and the others of $1/3$, $1/5$, … of it. Keep only the first, the one at the frequency of the swing. A damper $\kappa_f\theta'$ gives a torque of amplitude $\kappa_f\Theta$ in step with $\theta'$, so the friction acts like a damper with

$$\kappa_f = \frac{4f}{\pi\,\Theta}. \tag{7.5}$$

This is called the describing function of friction. It is a brake that weakens as the swing grows, since the toll per swing is fixed but the swing is longer. In (2.2), replace $R - 1$ by $R - 1 - j$ and $\kappa$ by $\kappa_e/\eta + \kappa_f$, and multiply through by $\Theta$:

$$\Theta^2(R - 1 - j)^2 + \left(\frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\Theta + \frac{4f}{\pi}\right)^2 = G^2. \tag{7.6}$$

This is a quadratic equation for $\Theta$. It has a positive root only if $4f/\pi < G$, a slightly stricter form of the jamming condition above. The power the load receives is (7.2) with $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle = \Omega^2\Theta^2/2$, and the rotor pays for everything the pendulum loses, in the coil, the load, the gears and the friction:

$$P_{\rm load} = mL^2\Omega^3\,\kappa_e\left(1 - \frac{c_e}{c_{\rm sc}}\right)\frac{\Theta^2}{2}, \qquad P_{\rm rotor} = mL^2\Omega^3\left[\frac{\kappa_e}{\eta}\,\frac{\Theta^2}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\,f\,\Theta\right]. \tag{7.7}$$

The last term is the friction's toll: torque times the mean speed $\langle|\theta'|\rangle = 2\Theta/\pi$.

Check
Remove the losses: $f = 0$, $j = 0$, $\eta = 1$ and $c_{\rm sc}\to\infty$ (a coil without resistance). Then (7.6) is (2.2), and both powers in (7.7) become (2.3). Units: $\kappa_e$, $j$, $f$ and $G$ are pure numbers, and $mL^2\Omega^3$ is a power.

7.4 On our blade

The formula (7.6) and (7.7) gives, for each mounting and each catalogue ratio of the GP 32 C, the load resistor that gives the load the most power; ratios that jam are out. Then, as a second route, the full equation is solved with the method of section 3.1. It is (7.4) with the gear loss charged on every power that flows through the gearhead, the rotor's sloshing included. Let $x$ be the torque the motor side needs, as felt through a perfect gear (in the units of (7.4), and without $\eta$ in the friction: $f_0 = NT_0/(mL^2\Omega^2)$); the pendulum feels $x/\eta$ while power flows into the motor side and $\eta x$ while it flows back:

$$\theta'' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi + \theta) + k\,x = 0, \qquad x = j\,\theta'' + f_0\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\theta' + \kappa_e\,\theta', \qquad k = \eta^{-\mathrm{sgn}(x\theta')}. \tag{7.8}$$

Because $\theta''$ appears on both sides, each step solves for it (the left side grows steadily with $\theta''$, so there is exactly one answer). Each sign is smoothed over a band of a thousandth of the swing. Near the formula's choice the full equation is searched again, over the ratios 14, 33, 51 and 111 and brakes from a quarter to four times the formula's; a setting counts only if the motion is steady, the rod swings less than 0.5 rad and the motor stays below the gearhead's limit of 8000 rpm. 7.5 The gearbox as a tuning weight, and why it fails

Section 2.5 asked whether the rod could be brought to resonance by lowering its net stiffness $R - 1$, and tilted the hinge to do it. The generator's own inertia lowers it too, from $R - 1$ to $R - 1 - j$, without tilting anything. On a large rotor $R - 1$ is between 40 and 300 and a $j$ of about 1 makes no difference. On a small, fast rotor $R - 1$ is between 1 and 5, and the same $j$ moves the pendulum a long way towards resonance.

On our blade
At 400 rpm with the pivot at 30 % the best ratio is {{n:t30_N}}, with $j = {{n:j_33}}$: the net stiffness falls from {{n:t30_s0}} to {{n:t30_s1}}. The rotor pays {{n:t30_paid}} mW, twice the bare pendulum's best $P^* = {{n:t30_Pstar}}$ mW, and the load receives {{n:t30_load}} mW, {{n:t30_share}} % of $P^*$ (with a swing of {{n:t30_th}} rad, so the play makes this rough). The ratio 51 would do better still on paper: its $j = {{n:j_51}}$ cancels $R - 1 = {{n:t30_s0}}$ almost exactly, to {{n:res_s}}, and puts the rod at resonance. If the gears passed the rotor's inertial torque without loss, the full equation would give {{n:res_v2_load}} mW at the load, {{n:res_v2_times}} times $P^*$. They do not: at resonance the motor's rotor is swung back and forth through the gears with a power that peaks at tens of watts, and the gears' loss on that sloshing leaves at most {{n:res_v3_load}} mW (with a load of {{n:res_v3_RL}} Ω), less than the ratio {{n:t30_N}} gives.

The lesson is general. A resonance stores far more energy than it delivers, and every loss on the stored energy counts. Through a gearbox that loses nearly a third of everything it passes, sloshing power is expensive, and resonance does not pay.

8 The answer

8.1 The ceiling

Lift a bag of shopping onto a table: the work you can get back from it is its weight times the height it falls, however it falls. A pendulum on a blade is the same. Gravity is the only thing that feeds it.

All the chapters meet in one inequality. Watch the pendulum from the turning blade, with the rotor's speed steady and the blade rigid. There the centrifugal pull is a fixed field of force with a potential energy, so over a steady turn it gives as much as it takes; the Coriolis force is always at right angles to the motion and does no work. Gravity is the only force whose direction changes, and the only one that can feed the swing on average. Its torque on the rod is $-mgL\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, never larger than $mgL$, so the power it feeds in, and with it every watt the generator can take, obeys

$$\bar P \le mgL\,\langle|\dot\theta|\rangle. \tag{8.1}$$

It holds for any motion: small swings or large, regular or chaotic, with any brake and any gearbox. (A rotor whose speed wanders, or a blade that shakes, adds a feed of its own; chapters 4 to 6 measured how little that gives.) For several rods the right side becomes $g$ times the sum, over the bobs, of each mass times its mean speed relative to the blade. To harvest more, the rod must move faster, and a stiff centrifugal spring does not let it. At the best brake of chapter 2 the power is $P^* = \tfrac12\,(mgL/\sqrt2)\,\Omega\Theta^*$, a fixed fraction, $\pi/(4\sqrt2) = {{n:ceiling_frac}}$, of the right side of (8.1).

8.2 What the pendulum can and cannot do

The question of these notes was whether pendula on turbine blades, double ones in particular, could turn the wind's disorder to their advantage. Here is the whole answer, chapter by chapter.

  • A single pendulum of 100 g on 20 cm takes {{n:P_sol_min}} to {{n:P_sol_max}} mW from the rotor at Sol's mountings, by the best-brake rule $\kappa^* = |R - 1|$, and the rotor pays for every joule (chapters 2 and 3).
  • A double pendulum of the same mass and reach takes less, {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}} times as much, because its mass sits closer to the pivot. Chaos appears only near resonance, where no real mounting is, and there the best regular setting beats the chaotic ones (chapter 3).
  • The disorder of the wind does not help: random shaking has a hard ceiling set by the pendulum's mass (chapter 4), gusts change the power by a few per cent at most (chapter 5), and the blade's bending gives a short pendulum almost nothing (chapter 6).
  • On a large turbine a real geared generator delivers {{n:g4_big_min}} to {{n:g4_big_max}} mW to the load, {{n:g4_margin}} times or more what the sensor of section 0.2 needs, and still about {{n:sens_margin}} times if the gearbox proves twice as draggy and much less efficient than its catalogue (chapter 7). Turning that slow, low voltage into a steady supply is the open problem.

So the order the wind cannot spoil, gravity turning once per revolution, is what feeds the sensor; the disorder adds almost nothing. The pendulum works because it is simple.
