2 Riding at the tip

Equation (1.6) came out of Lagrange's method in the frame of the ground. It is right, but it does not show why the second blade moves as it does. This chapter reads the same equation from a seat at the tip of the mother blade, where every term has a picture. The idea that makes it easy is one you know from riding in a car.

2.1 A pendulum in a car

An apparent force
feel
A car pulls away briskly and you are pressed into your seat. Nothing pushes you back; the seat pushes you forward, and from inside the car it feels like a pull backwards.
physics
In a frame that accelerates at $\mathbf a_0$, every body moves as if, besides the real forces, a pull $-m\mathbf a_0$ acted on it. No object exerts it; it is the price of describing motion from a frame that is itself speeding up.
measure
Hang a weight on a string from the car's mirror: it leans back by $\alpha$, and $g\tan\alpha$ is the car's acceleration.
maths
With $\mathbf r = \mathbf r_0(t) + \mathbf r'$: $m\ddot{\mathbf r}' = \mathbf F - m\ddot{\mathbf r}_0$.

The string pulls only along itself, so it leans until its pull has a forward part $ma$ besides the upward $mg$:

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{a}{g}. \tag{2.1}$$

From inside the car the weight hangs still along the apparent gravity, real gravity $g$ down plus the apparent pull $a$ backwards, as a plumb line hangs along gravity; nudged, it swings about that tilted line. A family car pulling away at about 3 m/s² tilts it by {{n:p1_car}}°; the second blade will rock by about as much at 6 m/s.

2.2 Two gravities at the tip

Sit at the tip of the mother blade, in the frame that turns with it at $\Omega = \dot\varphi$ about the shaft's axis $\hat{\mathbf z}$, with its origin at the hub. Write $\mathbf r'$ for a point's position measured in that frame, and $\dot{\mathbf r}'$, $\ddot{\mathbf r}'$ for the rates of change you would see from your seat, differentiating its components along the turning axes. A point fixed in the turning frame is carried round: seen from the ground it moves with the velocity $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r'$, $\boldsymbol\omega = \dot\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf z}$. A point that also moves in the frame adds its own velocity, so for any vector

$$\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\Big)_{\rm ground} = \Big(\frac{d}{dt}\Big)_{\rm turning} + \boldsymbol\omega\times, \qquad \dot{\mathbf r} = \dot{\mathbf r}' + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r'.$$

Apply the rule a second time, to $\dot{\mathbf r}$ itself, and remember that $\boldsymbol\omega$ changes when the rotor speeds up:

$$\ddot{\mathbf r} = \big(\ddot{\mathbf r}' + \dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf r' + \boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}'\big) + \boldsymbol\omega\times\big(\dot{\mathbf r}' + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r'\big) = \ddot{\mathbf r}' + 2\dot\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\dot{\mathbf r}' + \dot\varphi^2\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r') + \ddot\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r'.$$

Newton's law holds for $\ddot{\mathbf r}$, the acceleration seen from the ground: $m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F$. Solve for what the rider sees, $m\ddot{\mathbf r}'$, and move the other three terms to the right. In the rotor's plane $\hat{\mathbf z}\times(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r') = -\mathbf r'$, so Newton's law, read in the turning frame, gains three apparent forces:

$$m\ddot{\mathbf r}' = \mathbf F + \underbrace{m\Omega^2\mathbf r'}_{\text{centrifugal}} \underbrace{-\,2m\Omega\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\dot{\mathbf r}'}_{\text{Coriolis}} \underbrace{-\,m\ddot\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r'}_{\text{Euler}}.$$

The first is the pull outwards you feel on a roundabout. Measured in units of gravity at the hinge, it is

$$\Gamma = \frac{\Omega^2 r_p}{g}. \tag{2.2}$$
On our blade
At 8 m/s, $\Omega$ = {{n:acc8_om}} rad/s and the hinge feels $\Omega^2 r_p$ = {{n:acc8_om2}} s⁻² × 63 m = {{n:acc8}} m/s², a pull of {{n:acc8}} N on every kilogram: $\Gamma$ = {{n:Gam_8}}. At 6 and 10 m/s, $\Gamma$ = {{n:Gam_6}} and {{n:Gam_10}}: the outward pull beats gravity three to nine times, as on the stick of section 1.1.

Only torques about the hinge can turn the second blade. Its piece at the distance $s$ from the hinge sits at $\mathbf r' = r_p\hat{\mathbf e} + s\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$, with $\hat{\mathbf e}$ along the mother blade and $\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ along the second blade; its torque arm is $s\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$, mass $(m/l)\,ds$. Using $\hat{\mathbf z}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta\times\hat{\mathbf e}) = -\sin\theta$ and $\hat{\mathbf z}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta\times(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf e})) = \cos\theta$:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{centrifugal:}&\quad \int_0^l \frac{m}{l}\,\Omega^2\,\hat{\mathbf z}\cdot\big(s\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta\times(r_p\hat{\mathbf e} + s\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta)\big)\,ds = -\frac{ml}{2}\,\Omega^2 r_p\sin\theta,\\ \text{Coriolis:}&\quad \dot{\mathbf r}' = s\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta \ \Rightarrow\ -2\Omega\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\dot{\mathbf r}' = 2\Omega s\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta \parallel \text{the arm}: \quad 0,\\ \text{Euler:}&\quad -\ddot\varphi\int_0^l \frac{m}{l}\,\hat{\mathbf z}\cdot\big(s\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta\times(\hat{\mathbf z}\times(r_p\hat{\mathbf e} + s\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta))\big)\,ds = -\Big(I + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\cos\theta\Big)\ddot\varphi,\\ \text{gravity:}&\quad \int_0^l \frac{m}{l}\,\hat{\mathbf z}\cdot\big(s\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta\times\mathbf g\big)\,ds = -\frac{ml}{2}\,g\cos(\varphi+\theta), \qquad \mathbf g = -g\,(\sin\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf e} + \cos\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf e}). \end{aligned}$$

(Gravity points down; in the turning axes, with the mother blade at the angle $\varphi$ above the horizontal, its parts are $-g\sin\varphi$ along the mother blade and $-g\cos\varphi$ across it.) In the turning frame the hinge does not move, so the second blade simply turns about a fixed axle, and its angular momentum about that axle changes at the rate of the torques on it: $I\ddot\theta$ equals the four lines added, plus the brake's $-c\dot\theta$. That sum is (1.6), term for term, now found without a Lagrangian.

The centrifugal pull's part $\Omega^2s\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ points along the second blade and has no arm; what is left, $\Omega^2r_p\hat{\mathbf e}$, is the same for every piece. So for turning the second blade the centrifugal pull is a uniform second gravity, $\Gamma$ times the real one, pointing out along the mother blade: the spring of (1.6). The Coriolis force always pushes along the second blade, through the hinge: no torque, which is why (1.6) has no Coriolis term. The Euler force acts only while the rotor changes speed; section 6.5 shows that gusts keep it tiny. Gravity, uniform and pointing down, goes round once per turn as seen from the seat, and gives the push of (1.6).

So the second blade is a pendulum in two uniform gravities, one strong and fixed, one weak and turning, and it hangs along their sum. In units of $g$, the outward pull is an arrow $\Gamma$ along the mother blade, and gravity an arrow of length 1 with the parts $-\sin\varphi$ along it and $-\cos\varphi$ across. The sum points at

$$\tan\theta_{\rm b} = -\frac{\cos\varphi}{\Gamma - \sin\varphi}, \qquad \frac{d\tan\theta_{\rm b}}{d\varphi} = \frac{\Gamma\sin\varphi - 1}{(\Gamma - \sin\varphi)^2} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sin\varphi = \frac1\Gamma.$$

There $\cos\varphi = \pm\sqrt{1 - 1/\Gamma^2}$ and $\Gamma - \sin\varphi = (\Gamma^2 - 1)/\Gamma$, so $|\tan\theta_{\rm b}|_{\max} = 1/\sqrt{\Gamma^2 - 1}$, which is the same as

$$\sin|\theta_{\rm b}|_{\max} = \frac1\Gamma.$$

Figure {{fig:geff}} shows why without calculus: gravity's arrow sweeps a circle round the end of the long one, and the sum leans furthest along a tangent from the hinge to that circle.

2.3 A bowl that rocks

In a uniform pull a rod acts as if all its mass sat at its centre of mass, $l/2$ out. Its potential energy is its weight in the pull times the height of the centre of mass, measured against the pull: $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\sin(\varphi+\theta)$ for gravity, and $-\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\cos\theta$ for the outward pull, whose "uphill" is towards the hub:

$$U(\theta,\varphi) = \frac{ml}{2}\left[-\,\Omega^2 r_p\cos\theta + g\sin(\varphi+\theta)\right]. \tag{2.3}$$

This is the landscape (1.10) in joules, $U = I\Omega^2u$; minus its slope is the torque of the two pulls in (1.6). Near its bottom it is a bowl whose lowest point, $\partial U/\partial\theta = 0$, sits at $\theta_{\rm b}$ and slides from side to side once per turn (Figure {{fig:bowl}}).

{{table:fig_bowl}}
Put a marble in a salad bowl and tilt the bowl gently one way, then the other. The lowest point slides from side to side, and the marble chases it. Fill the bowl with honey and the marble lags behind, and the honey warms a little with every pass. The second blade is the marble, the generator the honey.
The work of a push that comes round
feel
Push a child on a swing just as the swing reaches you at the top, and you only stop it and start it again; push as it passes you moving away, and it goes higher.
physics
Over a cycle, a push in step with the swing's position gives on the way out what it takes back on the way in. Only its part in step with the velocity, a quarter cycle later, does net work.
measure
Plot the push against the angle over one cycle: the area of the loop is the work per cycle. A push in step with the angle draws a line, no area.
maths
Push $-G\cos\varphi$, swing $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi-\delta)$, so $\theta' = \Theta\sin(\varphi-\delta)$ and $\langle(-G\cos\varphi)\,\theta'\rangle = \tfrac12 G\Theta\sin\delta$: work per turn $\pi G\Theta\sin\delta$, in units of $I\Omega^2$.

A second blade that kept exactly to the moving bottom would move in step with gravity's push, $\delta = 0$, and the push would give nothing. The generator makes it lag, and the lag lets the push work; chapter 3 finds the best lag, 45°.

This also answers the puzzle of section 1.3. Keep the books in the turning frame, the rotor steady. There the second blade has the kinetic energy $\tfrac12I\dot\theta^2$ and, in the outward pull, the potential energy $-\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\cos\theta$, which does not change with time. Multiply (1.6), with $\ddot\varphi = 0$, by $\dot\theta$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\Big[\tfrac12 I\dot\theta^2 - \frac{ml}{2}\,\Omega^2 r_p\cos\theta\Big] = \underbrace{-\frac{ml}{2}\,g\cos(\varphi+\theta)\,\dot\theta}_{\text{gravity's torque}\,\times\,\dot\theta} - c\,\dot\theta^2.$$

The Coriolis force does no work (it is at right angles to the motion), and nothing else appears. Over a steady turn the bracket returns to its value, so on average the generator's $c\dot\theta^2$ equals gravity's torque times $\dot\theta$: seen from the turning blade, gravity's tilting push feeds the swing. Seen from the ground, gravity does no work over a turn, the second blade coming back to the same height, and the rotor pays, (1.7). Both books are right. The turning frame is held to the shaft, so the shaft's work never appears in it, while gravity, whose direction turns in that frame, is no longer a fixed field with a potential; the turning frame's books charge to gravity what the ground's charge to the shaft. Chapter 9 builds its ceiling on the first form.

2.4 The generator is a brake

A generator
feel
Spin an upside-down bicycle's wheel with its dynamo pressed to the tyre: with the lamp off it spins freely; with the lamp on it is harder to turn, and the harder the faster you turn it.
physics
A coil moved past magnets makes a voltage proportional to its speed (Faraday); the current it drives feels a force in the magnets' field that opposes the motion (Lenz).
measure
Spin it at a known speed with the wires apart: voltage over speed is $k_e$. Pass a known current and hold it: torque over current is $k_t$. In SI units the two are equal.
maths
$e = k_e\dot\theta$, $i = e/(R_c + R_L)$, torque $-k_ti$; and $k_ti\,\dot\theta = e\,i$ (power in = power out) gives $k_t = k_e$.

Our generator's case, the stator, is fixed to the mother blade and its turning part to the second blade, so it sees only $\dot\theta$. With the coil's resistance $R_c$ and the sensor's circuit $R_L$, its torque on the second blade is

$$-c\,\dot\theta, \qquad c = \frac{k_t k_e}{R_c + R_L}, \tag{2.4}$$

a brake proportional to speed, a spoon in honey. The power it takes is $c\dot\theta^2 = k_ti\,\dot\theta = e\,i = i^2(R_c + R_L)$: all of it becomes electrical, and the load gets the share $R_L/(R_c + R_L)$ (chapter 5). Chapter 3 finds the best $c$.

What to remember
  • In a frame that accelerates or turns, add apparent forces: centrifugal, Coriolis, Euler. They are bookkeeping, and they turn hard problems into pendulums at rest.
  • For turning the second blade the centrifugal pull is a uniform second gravity, $\Gamma$ = 3 to 9 times the real one, along the mother blade. The Coriolis force has no torque about the hinge; the Euler force acts only while the rotor changes speed.
  • The second blade hangs along the apparent gravity, which rocks by up to $\arcsin(1/\Gamma)$ each way once per turn.
  • Only the part of a push in step with the velocity does work. The generator, a brake $-c\dot\theta$, makes the second blade lag, and the lag lets gravity's push work.
Problems
  1. The car of section 2.1 brakes hard, slowing at 7 m/s². Which way does the string hang, and at what angle?
    AnswerForward: the car's frame now accelerates backwards, so the apparent pull points forward. By (2.1), $\tan\alpha = 7/9.81$, $\alpha$ = {{n:p1_brake}}°.
  2. Show that the Coriolis force on a piece of the second blade points along the second blade.
    AnswerRelative to the mother blade, the piece at $s$ moves with the velocity $s\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}$, where $\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ is the unit vector across the second blade, in the rotor's plane. Then $\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf n} = -\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$, so $-2\Omega\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf v = 2\Omega s\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$: along the second blade, through the hinge.
  3. Look at the rotor from upwind. It turns anticlockwise, and the mother blade points level to the right, so its tip is moving up. Does gravity push a second blade there forward, in the direction the rotor turns, or backward? And half a turn later?
    AnswerThe tip moves up, so forward is up, and gravity, pulling down, pushes the second blade backward. Half a turn later the mother blade points left and its tip moves down: now gravity pushes forward. So gravity pushes the second blade back and forth once per turn.
