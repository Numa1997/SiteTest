2 Riding at the tip

Equation (1.6) came out of Lagrange's method in the frame of the ground, where the only forces are gravity, the hinge and the generator. It is correct, but it does not show why the second blade moves as it does. This chapter reads the same equation from a seat at the tip of the mother blade. From there, every term has a picture, and the picture explains the rest of these notes. The idea that makes it easy is one you know from riding in a car.

2.1 A pendulum in a car

A small weight hangs on a string from the mirror of a car. The car pulls away and keeps speeding up at a steady rate $a$. While it does, the string hangs tilted back. From the road the reason is plain: the weight speeds up with the car, so something must push it forward with the force $ma$, and only the string can, which pulls only along itself. So the string leans back until its pull has a forward part $ma$ as well as the upward part $mg$ that holds the weight up. Its angle $\alpha$ from the vertical follows from

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{a}{g}. \tag{2.1}$$

From inside the car the weight hangs still, at an angle, as if a second gravity pulled everything backwards with the strength $a$ for every kilogram: the same pull that presses you into your seat. Real gravity, $g$ down, and this apparent pull, $a$ backwards, add up to an apparent gravity, and the string hangs along it, as a plumb line hangs along real gravity. Nudge the weight and it swings about that tilted line. Both descriptions are right. The apparent pull is not exerted by any object; it is what must be added when motion is described from a frame that is itself accelerating. The car's description is the convenient one, because in it the problem becomes a pendulum hanging still. A family car pulling away briskly speeds up at about 3 m/s², and (2.1) gives $\alpha$ = {{n:p1_car}}°; the second blade will turn out to rock by about as much at 6 m/s.

2.2 Two gravities at the tip

Whirl a stone on a string over your head and let go: it flies off along the circle, in the direction it was moving, not outwards. Seen from the ground nothing pushed it outwards; anything moving on a circle of radius $r$ at the angular speed $\Omega$ accelerates towards the centre by $\Omega^2r$, because its direction keeps turning, and the string supplied the force for that. Seen by someone who turns with the stone, the stone stays put, held by the string against an apparent pull outwards, the centrifugal pull, of $\Omega^2r$ for every kilogram. It is the turning frame's version of the car's backward pull.

Now sit at the tip of the mother blade. On every kilogram you feel two pulls: the centrifugal pull $\Omega^2r$, straight away from the hub, with $r$ the distance from the hub, and gravity, $g$, straight down. Gravity is fixed to the ground, but you turn, so seen from your seat gravity goes round the other way, once per turn: while the blade climbs, gravity pulls back against its motion; at the top it pulls towards the hub; while the blade falls it pulls forward; at the bottom, outwards. The ratio of the two pulls at the hinge says which one wins:

$$\Gamma = \frac{\Omega^2 r_p}{g}. \tag{2.2}$$
On our blade
At 8 m/s the rotor turns at $\Omega$ = {{n:acc8_om}} rad/s, so the hinge feels an outward pull of $\Omega^2 r_p$ = {{n:acc8_om2}} s⁻² × 63 m = {{n:acc8}} m/s² for every kilogram: $\Gamma$ = {{n:Gam_8}}. At 6 and 10 m/s, $\Gamma$ = {{n:Gam_6}} and {{n:Gam_10}}. The outward pull beats gravity by a factor of three to nine, about as on the stick of section 1.1, whirled once a second.

Only torques about the hinge can turn the second blade; the hinge takes up every other force. So take the pulls one at a time and ask what torque each makes about the hinge.

The centrifugal pull. A piece of the second blade at the distance $s$ from the hinge sits at $r_p\hat{\mathbf e} + s\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ from the hub, where $\hat{\mathbf e}$ points out along the mother blade and $\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ along the second blade. Its centrifugal pull per kilogram is $\Omega^2$ times that position, and it splits in two. The part $\Omega^2 s\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$ points along the second blade, straight away from the hinge: no lever arm, it only stretches the rod. The part left over, $\Omega^2 r_p\hat{\mathbf e}$, is the same for every piece: a uniform pull of strength $\Omega^2 r_p$ straight out along the mother blade. So, as far as turning the second blade goes, the centrifugal pull acts exactly like a second gravity, $\Gamma$ times stronger than the real one, pointing out along the mother blade. Its torque, $-\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p\sin\theta$, is the centrifugal spring of (1.6).

Gravity. A uniform pull $g$ downwards, which, seen from the mother blade, goes round once per turn. Its torque, $-\tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, is the push of (1.6).

The Coriolis force. Per kilogram it is $-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v$, with $\mathbf v$ the velocity relative to the turning frame and $\boldsymbol\Omega$ along the rotor's shaft: always at right angles to the motion. A piece of the second blade can only move about the hinge, at right angles to the second blade, so its Coriolis force points along the second blade, through the hinge. No lever arm, no torque; that is why (1.6) has no Coriolis term.

The Euler force. While the rotor speeds up or slows down, at the angular acceleration $\ddot\varphi$, every kilogram at the position $\mathbf r$ from the hub feels $-\ddot\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r$ ($\hat{\mathbf z}$ along the shaft): a pull against the way the rotor speeds up. Summed along the rod, $\int_0^l(m/l)\,\hat{\mathbf z}\cdot[s\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta\times(-\ddot\varphi\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times(r_p\hat{\mathbf e} + s\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta))]\,ds$, its torque about the hinge is $-\left(I + \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\cos\theta\right)\ddot\varphi$, the $\ddot\varphi$ term of (1.6). It vanishes when the rotor turns steadily; section 6.5 shows that gusts keep it tiny.

So the second blade is a pendulum hanging in two uniform gravities: a strong one, $\Gamma g$, fixed and pointing out along the mother blade, and the ordinary one, $g$, going round once per turn. As in the car, the two add up to an apparent gravity, and the second blade hangs along it like a plumb line.

Where does that plumb line point? Measure pulls in units of $g$. The outward one is an arrow of length $\Gamma$ along the mother blade. Gravity is an arrow of length 1 pointing down; along the mother blade it has the part $-\sin\varphi$, and across it, in the direction of increasing $\theta$, the part $-\cos\varphi$. So the apparent gravity points at the angle $\theta_{\rm b}$ from the mother blade's line, with

$$\tan\theta_{\rm b} = -\frac{\cos\varphi}{\Gamma - \sin\varphi}.$$

The long arrow ends at a fixed point, and the short one, added to its end, sweeps a circle of radius 1 around that point once per turn. Seen from the hinge, the sum points furthest off the mother blade's line when it just touches that circle, along a tangent: the short arrow is then at right angles to the sum, and the tilt is $\arcsin(1/\Gamma)$. It happens at $\sin\varphi = 1/\Gamma$, a little above the horizontal, once while the mother blade climbs and once while it falls.

On our blade
The largest tilt is {{n:tilt_6}}°, {{n:tilt_8}}° and {{n:tilt_10}}° at 6, 8 and 10 m/s, to one side and then the other, once per turn: at 6 m/s about the tilt of the string in the car. In stronger wind the outward pull is stronger and the plumb line rocks less.

2.3 A bowl that rocks

A rod in a uniform pull behaves as if all its mass sat at its centre of mass, $l/2$ along it. Its potential energy is its weight in that pull times the height of the centre of mass measured uphill against the pull. For gravity that is $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\sin(\varphi+\theta)$, dropping the hinge's own height, which does not depend on $\theta$. For the outward pull, uphill is towards the hub, and the centre of mass sits $\tfrac l2\cos\theta$ further out than the hinge, so it contributes $-\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2 r_p\cos\theta$. The second blade's potential energy in the turning frame is the sum:

$$U(\theta,\varphi) = \frac{ml}{2}\left[-\,\Omega^2 r_p\cos\theta + g\sin(\varphi+\theta)\right]. \tag{2.3}$$

Minus its slope along $\theta$ is the torque of the two pulls in (1.6). Read it as a bowl. Its first term is a bowl with its bottom at $\theta = 0$: the second blade lies along the mother blade because that is downhill in the turning frame. Gravity's term tilts the bowl, and the tilt changes as the rotor turns. Setting $\partial U/\partial\theta = 0$ puts the bottom at the angle $\theta_{\rm b}$ of section 2.2: on the mother blade's line when the mother blade points straight up or down, and furthest off it near the horizontal. Put a marble in a salad bowl and tilt the bowl gently one way, then the other. The lowest point slides from side to side, and the marble chases it. Fill the bowl with honey and the marble lags behind the moving bottom, and the honey warms a little with every pass. The second blade is the marble, the generator is the honey, and the bowl is (2.3).

Here is the idea on which the rest of the harvest rests. A push in step with a swing's position does no net work over a cycle: on the way out it pushes along the motion and gives energy, on the way back the motion pushes against it and returns the same energy. Only the part of a push in step with the swing's velocity, a quarter of a cycle later, does net work. A second blade that kept exactly to the moving bottom would move in step with gravity's tilting push, and the push would give nothing. The generator makes it lag. With a lag $\delta$ behind the push, the push has a part $\sin\delta$ of its size in step with the velocity, and that part feeds the swing every turn; the generator takes what it feeds. Chapter 3 finds the best lag, $45°$, and a surprise: the best brake is so strong that the second blade no longer rings on its own at all. It creeps after the moving bottom, like the marble in thick honey.

The same picture answers the puzzle of section 1.3. Seen from the turning blade, gravity's tilting push feeds the swing. Seen from the ground, gravity does no work over a turn and the rotor pays. Both are true: the turning frame's bookkeeping charges to gravity what the ground's charges to the shaft, because in the turning frame the shaft's work does not appear (the frame is held to the shaft) while gravity's direction turns.

2.4 The generator is a brake

Turn the wheel of an upside-down bicycle by hand with its dynamo pressed against the tyre. With the lamp off the wheel spins freely; with the lamp on it gets harder to turn, and the faster you turn it the harder it resists. That resistance is the electricity being made.

A generator is a coil turned past magnets. By Faraday's law the voltage it makes is proportional to how fast it turns. Our generator's outer case, the stator, is fixed to the mother blade, and its turning part to the second blade, so it sees only the swing relative to the mother blade, $\dot\theta$. Its voltage $k_e\dot\theta$ drives the current $i = k_e\dot\theta/(R_c + R_L)$ through the coil's resistance $R_c$ and the load $R_L$, the sensor's circuit. The current, flowing in the magnets' field, makes a torque $k_t i$ that opposes the motion (Lenz's rule). So the generator's torque on the second blade is

$$-c\,\dot\theta, \qquad c = \frac{k_t k_e}{R_c + R_L}, \tag{2.4}$$

a brake proportional to speed, like a spoon pulled through honey. It takes the power $c\dot\theta^2$ from the swing, and that power is the electricity, less what the coil wastes as heat; chapter 5 follows it to the wires. The constant $c$ is set by the choice of generator and load. Chapter 3 finds its best value.

What to remember
  • In a frame that accelerates or turns, add apparent pulls. They are bookkeeping, not forces exerted by anything, and they turn hard problems into pendulums at rest.
  • At the tip the outward pull is $\Gamma$ = 3 to 9 times gravity, and for turning the second blade it acts as a uniform second gravity along the mother blade. The Coriolis force has no torque about the hinge; the Euler force acts only while the rotor changes speed.
  • The second blade hangs along the apparent gravity, which rocks by up to $\arcsin(1/\Gamma)$ each way once per turn: a bowl that rocks.
  • A push in step with the position does no net work; only its part in step with the velocity does. The generator, a brake $-c\dot\theta$, makes the second blade lag, and the lag lets gravity's push work.
Problems
  1. The car of section 2.1 brakes hard, slowing at 7 m/s². Which way does the string hang, and at what angle?
    AnswerForward: the car's frame now accelerates backwards, so the apparent pull points forward. By (2.1), $\tan\alpha = 7/9.81$, $\alpha$ = {{n:p1_brake}}°.
  2. Show that the Coriolis force on a piece of the second blade points along the second blade.
    AnswerRelative to the mother blade, the piece at $s$ moves with the velocity $s\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}$, where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the unit vector across the second blade, in the rotor's plane. $\boldsymbol\Omega = \Omega\hat{\mathbf z}$ is at right angles to that plane, and $\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf n} = -\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$. So $-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v = 2\Omega s\dot\theta\,\hat{\mathbf e}_\theta$: along the second blade, through the hinge.
  3. Look at the rotor from upwind. It turns anticlockwise, and the mother blade points level to the right, so its tip is moving up. Does gravity push a second blade there forward, in the direction the rotor turns, or backward? And half a turn later?
    AnswerThe tip moves up, so forward is up, and gravity, pulling down, pushes the second blade backward. Half a turn later the mother blade points left and its tip moves down: now gravity pushes forward. So gravity pushes the second blade back and forth once per turn.
