2 The best brake, and how much it gives

Equation (1.5) cannot be solved in closed form. For small swings it can, and the answer contains the whole design rule.

2.1 Small swings: a driven, braked oscillator

Hold a stiff spring vertically with a weight on it and move your hand slowly up and down, far below the spring's own bounce. The weight simply follows your hand, a little late. Put the weight in honey and it lags further. It never bounces, because you never push at its rhythm.

Let $\theta$ be small: $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta) \approx \cos\varphi - \theta\sin\varphi$. The last term changes the spring's stiffness slightly once per turn. Compared with the centrifugal stiffness $R\theta$ it is of relative size $G/R = 1/\Gamma$, and its effect on the harvested power is smaller still: it shifts the swing to frequencies 0 and 2, which carry no power at first order (chapter 3 checks this against the full equation). Drop it, and (1.5) becomes

$$\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\,\theta = -G\cos\varphi. \tag{2.1}$$

This is the textbook driven, damped oscillator: a spring of stiffness $R$ (natural frequency $\sqrt R$ per turn), a damper $\kappa$, and a push of amplitude $G$ at frequency 1 per turn.

2.2 The swing

After the start-up has died away the rod swings at the frequency of the push. Try $\theta = \Theta\cos(\varphi - \delta)$, or better, its complex form $\theta = \mathrm{Re}\,[A\,e^{i\varphi}]$ with $\theta' \to iA$ and $\theta'' \to -A$. Then (2.1) gives one line of algebra:

$$(R - 1 + i\kappa)A = -G \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Theta = |A| = \frac{G}{\sqrt{(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{\kappa}{R-1}. \tag{2.2}$$

With no brake ($\kappa = 0$) the rod leans exactly in step with the push and swings $G/(R-1)$; the brake reduces the swing and makes it lag.

2.3 The harvested power

The generator takes the power $c\dot\theta^2$. Averaged over a turn, $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle = \Omega^2\langle\theta'^2\rangle = \Omega^2\Theta^2/2$, so

$$\bar P = \tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2\Theta^2 = mL^2\Omega^3\,\frac{\kappa\,G^2}{2\left[(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2\right]}. \tag{2.3}$$

Read it as a contest. A weak brake ($\kappa \to 0$) lets the rod swing freely but takes nothing from it. A strong brake ($\kappa \to \infty$) locks the rod to the blade, so nothing moves and again nothing is taken. In between lies a best brake. Set $d\bar P/d\kappa = 0$:

$$(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2 - 2\kappa^2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \kappa^* = |R - 1|. \tag{2.4}$$

The best brake equals the net stiffness. At that setting $\tan\delta = 1$: the rod lags gravity's push by exactly 45°. Put $\kappa^*$ back into (2.3) and return to physical units with $G = g/(\Omega^2L)$ and $R = r_p/L$:

$$\boxed{P^* = \frac{m\,L\,g^2}{4\,\Omega\,|r_p - L|}.} \tag{2.5}$$ {{table:fig_brake}}
A loudspeaker gets the most power from an amplifier when its resistance matches the amplifier's: too low and the current is wasted in the amplifier, too high and too little current flows. The best brake is the same kind of match, between the generator's resistance to motion and the stiffness the rod already has.
Check
Units: kg · m · (m/s²)² / (s⁻¹ · m) = kg m²/s³ = W. Limits: no gravity ($g \to 0$) gives no power, as it must, since nothing then pushes the rod off the blade's line. At $r_p = L$ the formula diverges. That is resonance: the rod's own frequency $\sqrt R$ equals the push. Small-swing theory breaks down there, and the swing is limited by the nonlinear terms we dropped; no real mounting is near it. The derivation of (2.2) to (2.5) is checked symbolically in SymPy.

Two consequences are worth a second look. First, the generator's torque at the optimum has the amplitude $$c^*\Omega\,\Theta^* = mL^2\Omega^2(R-1)\cdot\frac{G}{\sqrt2\,(R-1)} \quad=\quad \frac{mgL}{\sqrt2},$$ the same {{n:torque_star}} N m at every mounting, whatever the rotor. That is the torque the generator must be built to resist. Second, on a large rotor ($r_p \gg L$) the power is almost independent of how fast the rotor turns. At a fixed fraction $f$ of the blade, $\Omega r_p = f\lambda_T v$, so

$$P^* \approx \frac{mLg^2}{4\,\Omega r_p} = \frac{mLg^2}{4 f\lambda_T v}. \tag{2.6}$$

2.5 Could we tune it to resonance?

The rod is never at resonance, because its own frequency $\sqrt R$ is always above the push. Resonance would give far more power, so it is fair to ask whether we can bring it down to 1. One way is to tilt the hinge: turn its axis from the rotor shaft towards the blade by an angle $\alpha$. The rod then swings partly out of the rotor plane, where the centrifugal pull holds it less firmly. Working through the same Lagrangian (checked in SymPy) gives, for small swings,

$$x'' + \left(R\cos\alpha - \sin^2\alpha - G\cos\alpha\sin\varphi\right)x = -G\cos\varphi. \tag{2.7}$$

Gravity's push is unchanged, and the stiffness $R\cos\alpha - \sin^2\alpha$ can be set to 1. On the fastest rotor that needs $\alpha = {{n:tilt_fast}}°$, and the stiffness changes by {{n:tilt_sens_fast}} per radian of tilt error: forgiving. On the slowest rotor it needs $\alpha = {{n:tilt_slow}}°$, with the rod almost parallel to the shaft, and the stiffness changes by {{n:tilt_sens_slow}} per radian: a tilt error of a tenth of a degree moves the stiffness by half its value, and the resonance is lost. The blade's pitch, which the turbine changes in strong wind, tilts the hinge too. So resonance is a real option on a small, fast rotor and an impractical one on a large turbine; these notes keep Sol's straight pendulum.
