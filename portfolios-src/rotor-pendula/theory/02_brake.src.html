2 The best brake, and how much it gives

Chapter 1 ended with equation (1.6). It cannot be solved in closed form, but for small swings it can, and the answer holds the whole design rule: how hard the generator should brake, and how much power it then gets. The answer has the same shape as a question about batteries, so start there.

Warm-up
A battery makes 12 V, but inside it has a resistance of 2 Ω, which its current must pass through as well as through whatever you connect. You connect a lamp of resistance $R_L$. Which lamp gets the most power: one of 1 Ω, 2 Ω or 4 Ω? And what do a bare wire (0 Ω) and no lamp at all (an open gap) get?
AnswerThe current is $12/(2 + R_L)$ amperes, and the lamp's power is the current squared times $R_L$: 16 W at 1 Ω, 18 W at 2 Ω, 16 W at 4 Ω. The bare wire carries a large current but has no resistance to turn it into heat: 0 W. The open gap carries no current: 0 W. The most goes to the lamp whose resistance matches the battery's own, 2 Ω. Keep the shape in mind: nothing at either extreme, and a best value in between, where the load matches the source.

2.1 Small swings: a driven, braked oscillator

Hang a weight on a stiff spring and move your hand slowly up and down, far slower than the spring's own bounce. The weight simply follows your hand. Put the weight in honey and it follows a little late. It never bounces, because you never push at its own rhythm.

Let $\theta$ be small, so $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta) \approx \cos\varphi - \theta\sin\varphi$. Keep the first piece, gravity's push $G\cos\varphi$. The second piece, $-G\theta\sin\varphi$, makes the spring's stiffness go up and down once per turn. It is not small because the swing is small: compared with the spring's own term $R\theta$ it has the size $G/R = 1/\Gamma$, up to {{n:invGam_max}} at 6 m/s. We may drop it because of when it acts. Multiplying a swing at once per turn by $\sin\varphi$ makes motion at zero and twice per turn, not at once per turn, and that motion acts back on the once-per-turn swing only by passing through the same term a second time. So its effect is of second order, and section 2.4 measures it, together with everything else the small-swing theory leaves out. Without it, (1.6) becomes

$$\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\,\theta = -G\cos\varphi. \tag{2.1}$$

You know this equation: the driven, damped oscillator. A spring of stiffness $R$ (natural frequency $\sqrt R$ per turn), a damper $\kappa$, and a push of amplitude $G$ at the frequency 1 per turn.

2.2 The swing

Once the start-up has died away the second blade swings at the frequency of the push. Write $\theta = \mathrm{Re}\,[A\,e^{i\varphi}]$, so that $\theta' \to iA$ and $\theta'' \to -A$. Then (2.1) is one line of algebra:

$$(R - 1 + i\kappa)A = -G \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi - \delta), \qquad \Theta = \frac{G}{\sqrt{(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{\kappa}{R-1}. \tag{2.2}$$

Why $R - 1$ and not $R$? The $-1$ is the second blade's own inertia: shaking any body back and forth at the frequency 1 takes a torque proportional to its angular acceleration, $-\theta''$, which here works against the spring. So $R - 1$ is the spring minus the inertia at the push's frequency, the net stiffness the push meets. With no brake ($\kappa = 0$) the second blade moves exactly in step with the push and swings by $G/(R-1)$; a brake makes the swing smaller and makes it lag behind the push by the angle $\delta$.

A picture of (2.2): draw each torque as an arrow that turns once per turn, its length the torque's size. Gravity's push is one arrow. The net stiffness's torque points along the swing. The brake's torque opposes the velocity, so it stands at right angles to the swing. The two together must balance gravity's push, closing a right-angled triangle whose hypotenuse is gravity's arrow.

2.3 The harvested power

The generator takes the power $c\dot\theta^2$. Averaged over a turn, $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle = \Omega^2\langle\theta'^2\rangle = \Omega^2\Theta^2/2$, so

$$\bar P = \tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2\Theta^2 = I\Omega^3\,\frac{\kappa\,G^2}{2\left[(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2\right]}. \tag{2.3}$$

Read it as the battery of the warm-up. A weak brake ($\kappa \to 0$) is the open gap: the second blade swings freely but nothing is taken. A strong brake ($\kappa \to \infty$) is the bare wire: it locks the second blade to the mother blade, nothing moves, and again nothing is taken. In between lies a best brake. Set $d\bar P/d\kappa = 0$:

$$(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2 - 2\kappa^2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \kappa^* = |R - 1|. \tag{2.4}$$

The best brake equals the net stiffness: the load matches the source. At that setting $\tan\delta = 1$, and the second blade lags gravity's push by exactly 45°; in the triangle of torques the brake's arrow and the net stiffness's arrow are equal. Put $\kappa^*$ back into (2.3) and return to physical units with $I\Omega^2(R-1) = \left(\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p - I\right)\Omega^2$ and $I\Omega^2G = \tfrac{ml}{2}g$:

$$\boxed{P^* = \frac{\left(\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g\right)^2}{4\,\Omega\left(\tfrac{ml}{2}\,r_p - I\right)} = \frac{m\,g^2\,l}{8\,\Omega\left(r_p - \tfrac23 l\right)}.} \tag{2.5}$$

How strong is this brake? A spring of stiffness $R$ with a damper $\kappa$ just stops ringing when nudged, the brake called critical damping, at $\kappa = 2\sqrt R$. The best brake is $\kappa^*/(2\sqrt R)$ = {{n:zeta_star}} times that. Nudge the second blade and it does not ring at all; it creeps back. It does not need to ring, because it is pushed far below its own rhythm: it creeps after the moving bottom of the bowl of section 1.5, the marble in thick honey.

The exact electrical twin: an alternating supply feeds a resistor through a coil. The coil opposes the alternating current with its reactance, $\omega L_{\rm coil}$, but takes no power. The resistor takes the most power when its resistance equals that reactance. The generator is the resistor, the net stiffness $R - 1$ is the coil's reactance, and gravity's push is the supply.

Three results follow from (2.5).

The generator's torque. At the best brake the generator's torque swings with the amplitude

$$\tau^* = c^*\Omega\,\Theta^* = I\Omega^2(R-1)\cdot\frac{G}{\sqrt2\,(R-1)} \quad=\quad \frac{\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g}{\sqrt2},$$

{{n:tau_star}} N m for our second blade, at every wind. The triangle of torques says why: at the best brake its two short sides are equal, so each is the hypotenuse, gravity's largest torque $\tfrac{ml}{2}g$, divided by $\sqrt2$. That is the torque the generator must be built to hold.

The best brake in physical units. $c^* = \kappa^*I\Omega = \left(\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p - I\right)\Omega$, and on a large rotor, where $r_p \gg l$,

$$c^* \approx \frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\Omega: \tag{2.6}$$

the first moment times the speed of the hinge. For our second blade that is {{n:cst_6}} to {{n:cst_10}} N m s from 6 to 10 m/s, a hard brake for a small machine, and chapter 7 finds that building it is the hardest part of the generator.

Guess first
The wind gets stronger and the rotor turns faster. Does the second blade, at its best brake, give more power or less?
AnswerLess, as the next equation shows.

The power on a large rotor.

$$P^* \approx \frac{\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g^2}{4\,\Omega r_p} = \frac{\tfrac{ml}{2}\,g^2}{4\,v_{\rm tip}}. \tag{2.7}$$

It depends on the second blade only through its first moment, and on the turbine only through the speed of the hinge, $v_{\rm tip} = \Omega r_p = \lambda^*v$. The faster the hinge moves, the less power: the outward pull grows as $\Omega^2$ and holds the second blade stiffer, so gravity, which does not change, moves it less. The second blade gives the most in light wind.

2.4 On our blade

Worked example
Take 8 m/s, with the {{n:mB}} kg, {{n:lB}} m second blade at the 63 m tip. The rotor turns at $\Omega$ = {{n:acc8_om}} rad/s, one turn every {{n:turn8_s}} s. The stiffness is $R = 3r_p/2l$ = {{n:R}} and gravity's number is $G = 3g/(2l\Omega^2)$ = {{n:G_8}}. The best brake is $\kappa^* = R - 1$ = {{n:Rm1}}, that is $c^* = \kappa^* I\Omega$ = {{n:cst_8}} N m s. The swing is $\Theta^* = G/(\sqrt2\,\kappa^*)$ = {{n:Th_8}} rad, {{n:Th_8_deg}}°: the free end of the second blade sways {{n:Th_8_cm}} cm to either side of the mother blade's line, once every {{n:turn8_s}} s, a slow, gentle sway. The generator holds a torque swinging by {{n:tau_star}} N m, and (2.5) gives $P^*$ = {{n:Pst_8}} mW.

Every neglected effect grows that way: the cubic term of $\sin\theta$ and the $\theta^2\cos\varphi$ term of gravity's push are of higher order in the swing, and the dropped term of section 2.1, of order $G^2$, grows as $\Theta^2$ too, because the swing $\Theta$ is itself proportional to $G$. So the comparison shows how large the neglected effects are together, not which one dominates.

The timing of the swing is right, and so is its size: the theory's {{n:cmp_amp6}} rad at 6 m/s against the full model's {{n:Th_max}} rad. The second blade does not swing about the mother blade's line but about a line slightly behind it. A linear theory cannot produce a constant offset from a push that averages to zero; it takes the second-order terms. Section 3.2 shows that this small trail is exactly what makes the rotor pay.

2.5 Resonance is out of reach

Any oscillator gives the most when it is pushed at its own rhythm, at resonance. Can the second blade get there? Its stiffness is $R = 3r_p/(2l)$, so the net stiffness $R - 1$ falls to zero only for $l = \tfrac32 r_p$, a second blade half as long again as the mother blade. For any second blade no longer than its mother blade, $R \ge \tfrac32$, and for ours $R - 1$ = {{n:Rm1}}. The centrifugal spring of a body hinged at the tip of a long blade is always far stiffer than its own inertia at the push's frequency. To reach resonance, extra inertia would have to cancel it, for example a heavy flywheel turned by the generator through a gearbox (chapter 7): it would have to add about {{n:Rm1_round}} times the second blade's own moment of inertia. These notes do not pursue that.

What to remember
  • For small swings the second blade is a driven, braked oscillator: stiffness $R$, push $G$ once per turn, brake $\kappa$.
  • The best brake equals the net stiffness, $\kappa^* = R - 1$: the load matches the source, as for a lamp on a battery. The swing then lags the push by 45°.
  • The best brake is about five times critical damping: the second blade does not ring, it creeps after the moving bottom of the bowl.
  • $P^* \approx \tfrac{ml}{2}g^2/(4v_{\rm tip})$: proportional to the first moment, and smaller in stronger wind. At 6, 8 and 10 m/s, {{n:Pst_6}}, {{n:Pst_8}} and {{n:Pst_10}} mW for 1 kg on 1 m.
  • The generator must hold $\tfrac{ml}{2}g/\sqrt2$ = {{n:tau_star}} N m at every wind.
Problems
  1. Show from (2.3) that a brake twice as strong as the best one, or half as strong, gives the same power. What fraction of $P^*$ is it?
    AnswerWith $x = \kappa/\kappa^*$, (2.3) and (2.5) give $\bar P/P^* = 2x/(1 + x^2)$, which is unchanged when $x \to 1/x$. At $x = 2$ or $\tfrac12$ it is 4/5, 80 % of $P^*$. The hump of Figure 6 is flat-topped: the brake need not be set exactly.
  2. At 10 m/s, how far does the free end of our second blade sway, and how much power does it give at the best brake?
    Answer$\Theta^* = G/(\sqrt2\,\kappa^*)$ with $G$ = {{n:G_10}}: {{n:Th_10}} rad, so the free end sways {{n:Th_10_cm}} cm either way. (2.5) gives {{n:Pst_10}} mW.
  3. The second blade's mass is doubled, at the same length. What happens to $P^*$, to the best brake $c^*$, and to the torque the generator must hold?
    AnswerAll three double. In (2.5) the first moment appears squared on top and once below; $c^*$ and $\tau^*$ are each proportional to the first moment.
  4. Why is the generator's torque at the best brake the same at every wind, although the power is not?
    AnswerGravity's largest torque, $\tfrac{ml}{2}g$, does not depend on the wind, and at the best brake the generator carries $1/\sqrt2$ of it. What changes with the wind is how fast the swing goes: power is torque times angular speed, and the swing's angular speed, $\Omega\Theta^*$, falls as the rotor speeds up because $\Theta^*$ falls as $1/\Omega^2$.
  5. In the warm-up, the lamp that gets the most power gets only half of what the battery makes; the other half heats the battery. Does the generator at its best brake waste half of the power in the same way?
    AnswerNo. The net stiffness, like the coil in the electrical twin, stores energy and gives it back each swing but takes none. All of $P^*$ goes into the generator. (Inside the generator, some of it heats the coil; chapter 7.)
