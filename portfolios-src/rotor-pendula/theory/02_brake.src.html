2 The best brake, and how much it gives

Equation (1.5) cannot be solved in closed form. For small swings it can, and the answer contains the whole design rule.

2.1 Small swings: a driven, braked oscillator

Hold a stiff spring vertically with a weight on it and move your hand slowly up and down, far below the spring's own bounce. The weight simply follows your hand, a little late. Put the weight in honey and it lags further. It never bounces, because you never push at its rhythm.

Let $\theta$ be small: $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta) \approx \cos\varphi - \theta\sin\varphi$. Keep the first piece, gravity's push $G\cos\varphi$. The second piece, $-G\theta\sin\varphi$, makes the spring's stiffness go up and down once per turn. It is not small everywhere: compared with the centrifugal stiffness $R\theta$ its size is $G/R = 1/\Gamma$, up to {{n:invG_max}} on the slowest mounting. We drop it anyway, for a reason that has nothing to do with its size: multiplying a swing at frequency 1 by $\sin\varphi$ produces motion at frequencies 0 and 2, not at frequency 1, so to first order it does not change the swing that the push drives. Its effect enters only at second order, and chapter 3 measures it against the full equation. Without it, (1.5) becomes

$$\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\,\theta = -G\cos\varphi. \tag{2.1}$$

This is the textbook driven, damped oscillator: a spring of stiffness $R$ (natural frequency $\sqrt R$ per turn), a damper $\kappa$, and a push of amplitude $G$ at frequency 1 per turn.

2.2 The swing

After the start-up has died away the rod swings at the frequency of the push. Use complex notation: $\theta = \mathrm{Re}\,[A\,e^{i\varphi}]$, so that $\theta' \to iA$ and $\theta'' \to -A$. Then (2.1) gives one line of algebra:

$$(R - 1 + i\kappa)A = -G \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi - \delta), \qquad \Theta = \frac{G}{\sqrt{(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{\kappa}{R-1}. \tag{2.2}$$

Why $R - 1$ and not $R$? The $-1$ is the bob's own inertia: driving any mass back and forth at frequency 1 costs a force proportional to its acceleration, $-\theta''$, which here works against the spring. So $R - 1$ is the spring minus the inertia at the push frequency, the net stiffness the push meets. With no brake ($\kappa = 0$) the rod follows the push exactly in step and swings $G/(R-1)$; the brake reduces the swing and makes it lag by $\delta$.

2.3 The harvested power

The generator takes the power $c\dot\theta^2$. Averaged over a turn, $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle = \Omega^2\langle\theta'^2\rangle = \Omega^2\Theta^2/2$, so

$$\bar P = \tfrac12 c\,\Omega^2\Theta^2 = mL^2\Omega^3\,\frac{\kappa\,G^2}{2\left[(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2\right]}. \tag{2.3}$$

Read it as a contest. A weak brake ($\kappa \to 0$) lets the rod swing freely but takes nothing from it. A strong brake ($\kappa \to \infty$) locks the rod to the blade, so nothing moves and again nothing is taken. In between lies a best brake. Set $d\bar P/d\kappa = 0$:

$$(R-1)^2 + \kappa^2 - 2\kappa^2 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \kappa^* = |R - 1|. \tag{2.4}$$

The best brake equals the net stiffness. At that setting $\tan\delta = 1$: the rod lags gravity's push by exactly 45°. Put $\kappa^*$ back into (2.3) and return to physical units with $G = g/(\Omega^2L)$ and $R = r_p/L$:

$$\boxed{P^* = \frac{m\,L\,g^2}{4\,\Omega\,|r_p - L|}.} \tag{2.5}$$
Connect a resistor to an AC supply through a coil. Too small a resistor and the current flows but little power is turned into heat in it; too large and hardly any current flows. The resistor takes the most power when its resistance equals the coil's opposition to the alternating current, its reactance $\omega L_{\rm coil}$. The generator is the resistor; the net stiffness of the pendulum, $R - 1$, plays the coil's reactance; gravity's push is the supply. The best brake is that match.

Two consequences are worth a second look. First, the generator's torque at the optimum has the amplitude

$$c^*\Omega\,\Theta^* = mL^2\Omega^2(R-1)\cdot\frac{G}{\sqrt2\,(R-1)} \quad=\quad \frac{mgL}{\sqrt2},$$

the same {{n:torque_star}} N m at every mounting, whatever the rotor. The reason: at the best brake the generator's torque and the torque of the net stiffness (spring minus inertia) are equal in size and 90° apart, and together they balance gravity's push, whose largest torque is $mgL$; each of the two therefore carries $1/\sqrt2$ of it. That is the torque the generator must be built to resist.

Second, on a large rotor ($r_p \gg L$)

$$P^* \approx \frac{mLg^2}{4\,\Omega r_p}. \tag{2.6}$$

The power depends only on the pivot's speed, $\Omega r_p$. For one rotor, turning faster gives less. Across the six rotors, though, the tip-speed ratio fixes the pivot's speed at a given fraction $f$ of the blade, $\Omega r_p = f\lambda_T v$, so a small fast rotor and a large slow one give the same power at the same fraction of the blade, as long as $r_p \gg L$. On the 400 rpm rotor the pivot at 30 % is only $2L$ from the hub, $r_p - L$ is half of $r_p$, and the power doubles (Table 1).

2.5 Tuning to resonance

The rod is never at resonance, because its own frequency $\sqrt R$ is always above the push. At resonance the small-swing power grows without limit as the brake weakens; in practice the swing is then limited by its own nonlinearity, and section 3.4 shows what that gives at $R = 1$: several hundred milliwatts, several times the best real mounting. So it is worth asking whether the stiffness can be brought down to 1.

One way is to tilt the hinge. Normally the hinge axis is parallel to the rotor's shaft, so the rod swings in the rotor plane. Turn the hinge axis by an angle $\alpha$ towards the blade's own axis (Figure 3): the rod then swings in a plane tilted out of the rotor plane, and at rest it no longer lies along the blade but leans out of the rotor plane by $\alpha$, where the centrifugal pull holds it less firmly. Two things soften the spring. Only the part $\cos\alpha$ of the outward pull now acts across the swing; and a leaning bob that swings sideways moves farther from the shaft's axis (for a rod in the rotor plane the sideways move and the shortening along the blade cancel exactly, for a leaning rod they no longer do), which the outward pull favours: that subtracts $\sin^2\alpha$. Let $\chi$ be the rod's swing angle about the tilted hinge. The Lagrangian of chapter 1, written now in three dimensions and worked through in SymPy, gives for small swings

$$\chi'' + \kappa\,\chi' + \left(R\cos\alpha - \sin^2\alpha - G\cos\alpha\sin\varphi\right)\chi = -G\cos\varphi. \tag{2.7}$$

Gravity's push is unchanged, and the stiffness $R\cos\alpha - \sin^2\alpha$ can be set to 1. On the fastest rotor (400 rpm, pivot at 30 %) that needs $\alpha = {{n:tilt_fast}}°$, and the stiffness changes by {{n:tilt_sens_fast}} per radian of tilt error: forgiving. On the slowest rotor (8 rpm, pivot at 90 %) it needs $\alpha = {{n:tilt_slow}}°$, with the rod almost parallel to the shaft, and the stiffness changes by {{n:tilt_sens_slow}} per radian: a tilt error of a tenth of a degree moves the stiffness by half its value, and the resonance is lost. The blade's pitch, which the turbine changes in strong wind, tilts the hinge too. So on paper resonance is an option on a small, fast rotor and an impractical one on a large turbine. Chapter 7 adds a second obstacle: at resonance a real generator's rotor is swung back and forth through its gearbox, and the gears lose most of what the resonance gains. These notes keep the straight pendulum.
