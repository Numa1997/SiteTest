3 Big swings, two rods, and chaos

Chapter 2 dropped every term that is not linear in $\theta$. This chapter solves the full equations, finds out who really pays and how, adds a second rod, and asks whether chaos can help.

3.1 The full equation, solved numerically

Push a spoon slowly through thick honey and it moves calmly; flick it and the honey stops it almost at once. A heavily braked pendulum has both kinds of motion at once: a slow swing, and a very fast relaxation of any motion the brake fights. That makes its equations "stiff".

Equation (1.5), with $\sin\theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta)$ kept whole, is integrated step by step on a computer. A simple step-by-step method that only looks at the present moment (an explicit method, such as the classical Runge–Kutta method) must take steps shorter than the fastest relaxation, or its errors grow without bound; for the braked double pendulum of section 3.3 that is tens of thousands of steps per turn. An implicit method instead solves, at each step, for the state at the end of the step, and stays stable with steps as long as the slow swing allows. These notes use one such method, a three-stage Radau IIA method of fifth order: halving the step shrinks the error some 30 times, the result agrees with an independent high-accuracy solver to six parts in $10^{13}$, and it reproduces all 36 of Sol's runs, single and double, to every digit he printed.

For the single pendulum, the closed form (2.5) is within {{n:lin_err_max}} % of the full model at every mounting. That needs an explanation, because the dropped terms are not all harmless. At second order in the swing three effects appear: the term $-\tfrac12G\theta^2\cos\varphi$ changes gravity's push itself, by about $\Theta^2/4$ of it (some 6 % on the slowest rotor); the cubic term of $\sin\theta$ softens the spring; and the mean trail of section 3.2 feeds back through $-G\bar\theta\sin\varphi$. These notes have not measured the three separately; the full model shows only their sum. Two reasons make a small sum plausible: they partly cancel, and what remains acts near the flat top of Figure 2, where a small change in the push or the stiffness moves the best brake but hardly the best power.

3.2 The rod trails, and that is the bill

One effect of the dropped terms is visible and easy to derive: on average, the rod is not straight out along the blade. Keep the gravity term to second order, $G\cos(\varphi+\theta) \approx G\cos\varphi - G\theta\sin\varphi$, and average (1.5) over a steady turn. The averages of $\theta''$, $\theta'$ and $\cos\varphi$ vanish, and what is left is

$$R\,\bar\theta = G\,\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle, \tag{3.1}$$

with $\theta$ on the right the swing (2.2), $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi-\delta)$. Its average with $\sin\varphi$ is $-\tfrac12\Theta\sin\delta$, and (2.2) gives $\sin\delta = \kappa\Theta/G$, so

$$\bar\theta = -\frac{\kappa\,\Theta^2}{2R}, \qquad\text{at the best brake}\quad \bar\theta = -\frac{G^2}{4R(R-1)}. \tag{3.2}$$

The minus sign means the rod trails behind the blade's line, against the direction of rotation, like a weight towed behind a boat.

Now compare (3.2) with the harvested power (2.3), $\bar P = mL^2\Omega^3\cdot\tfrac12\kappa\Theta^2$. The same combination $\kappa\Theta^2$ appears in both, and for every brake setting

$$\boxed{\bar P = -\,m\,r_p\,L\,\Omega^3\,\bar\theta.} \tag{3.3}$$

The trail is the bill. To see why, average the rotor's equation of section 1.4 over a turn: the part with a time derivative averages to zero, leaving $\bar\tau_{\rm rot} = mgL\langle\cos(\varphi+\theta)\rangle \approx -mgL\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle$. Gravity's torque about the hub on the bob would average to zero if the rod stayed on the blade's line; because the swing keeps in step with gravity's turning, it no longer does, and the rotor must push against it. The same average $\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle$ sets the trail in (3.1). Measure how far the rod trails, multiply by $mr_pL\Omega^3$, and you have the power the rotor is paying. There is a picture behind it: $mgL\langle\cos(\varphi+\theta)\rangle = mg\langle x_{\rm bob}\rangle$, since the pivot's own $x$ averages to zero. The trailing bob sits, on average, a little to the side of the hub where the blade is rising, so the rotor keeps lifting a weight that is never balanced, like a Ferris wheel loaded on one side.

On the slowest rotor (8 rpm, pivot at 30 %) (3.2) gives $\bar\theta = {{n:lean_theory}}$ rad, a trail of {{n:lean_deg}}°; the full model gives {{n:lean_run}} rad. The next largest trail, on the other large rotors, is {{n:lean_next_deg}}°; on the fast, small rotors it never exceeds {{n:lean_small_deg}}°.

3.3 Two rods

Swing a hammer held at the very end of its handle, then grip the same hammer halfway up. The second swing is easier and weaker: the same mass now sits closer to your hand, so it has less leverage. Spreading a pendulum's mass along two rods does the same thing to it.

Sol's double pendulum splits the same mass and reach in two: bobs of 0.05 kg on rods of 0.1 m, with a generator at the pivot and another at the middle hinge, both with the same constant $c$. Its brake parameter is $\kappa = c/(ML^2\Omega)$ with $M$ the total mass and $L$ the total reach, as for the single pendulum, and its best $c$ is the one value that maximises its small-swing power, confirmed in the full model (Sol's procedure). Its equations follow from the same Lagrangian with two angles, $q_1$ and $q_2$, each measured from the blade's outward direction; they were derived in SymPy and again from Newton's law in the turning frame, and agree with Sol's. At every mounting it gives {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}} times the single pendulum's power: always less.

The reason is simple once you lock the hinge. A locked double is a rigid body with mass $M$, moment of inertia $I_p$ about the pivot, and centre of mass at distance $l_c$ from it. Redo chapter 2 for a rigid body: the inertia $mL^2$ becomes $I_p$; the centrifugal stiffness $mr_pL\Omega^2$ becomes $Ml_c r_p\Omega^2$ (only the centre of mass matters, because the body's spread about its own pivot adds a constant to the centrifugal energy that does not change as the body turns); and the gravity torque $mgL$ becomes $Mgl_c$. The net stiffness, $mL^2\Omega^2(R - 1) = (mr_pL - mL^2)\Omega^2$ in physical units, becomes $(Ml_c r_p - I_p)\Omega^2$, so

$$P^*_{\rm rigid} = \frac{(M\,l_c\,g)^2}{4\,\Omega\,(M\,l_c\,r_p - I_p)}. \tag{3.4}$$

For the single pendulum, $Ml_c = mL$ and $I_p = mL^2$, and (3.4) is (2.5) again. For the locked double, the centre of mass sits at $l_c = {{n:lc_frac}}\,L$: half the mass at 0.1 m, half at 0.2 m. On a large rotor (3.4) is then simply proportional to $Ml_c$, and the double gives 3/4 of the single.

On our blade
The locked double gives {{n:lock_min}} to {{n:lock_max}} times the single, from the fastest rotor to the slowest. Letting the hinge move, with its own generator, costs only a further {{n:flex_cost_min}} to {{n:flex_cost_max}} %, giving Sol's {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}}. One rod beats two because the second rod brings its mass closer to the pivot, where gravity's push has less leverage.

3.4 Chaos and the harvest

A double pendulum can move chaotically: two copies started a hair apart drift apart exponentially, like two leaves dropped side by side into a stream. The rate of that drift is the largest Lyapunov exponent $\Lambda$: start two copies $10^{-8}$ apart, follow them, rescale their separation back every quarter turn of the rotor ($\pi/2$ of rotor angle), and divide the logarithm of the total growth by the time. A regular, damped motion gives $\Lambda < 0$; chaos gives $\Lambda > 0$. Here a run counts as chaotic when $\Lambda$ exceeds 0.01 per radian of rotor angle, which at the rotor speed below ({{n:chaos_Om}} rad/s) is {{n:chaos_thr}} s⁻¹. Because chaos depends on where the motion starts, every case is run from {{n:chaos_nic}} different starting positions, never from one.

Sol found chaos at $R = 1$: the pivot one rod length from the hub (0.2 m), a rotor turning at 4 rad/s, and gravity's push $G = 3.06$. $R = 1$ is exactly the resonance of section 2.3 for the single pendulum, and close to the double's: the locked double of (3.4) resonates where $Ml_c r_p = I_p$, at $R = {{n:R_res_double}}$. There the small-swing theory fails and the swing grows large; that is where chaos has room to appear. And note the brakes: near resonance the swing is large, the small-swing best brake no longer applies, and the best settings are far heavier than it.

The physical reason is the centrifugal spring: when it is much stiffer than gravity's push, both rods are held out along the blade and move together, and there is nothing irregular left for them to do.
