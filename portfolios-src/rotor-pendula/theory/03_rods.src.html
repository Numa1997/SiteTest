3 Big swings, two rods, and chaos

Chapter 2 dropped every term that is not linear in $\theta$. This chapter solves the full equations, adds a second rod, and asks whether chaos can help.

3.1 The full equation, solved numerically

Equation (1.5), with $\sin\theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta)$ kept whole, is integrated step by step on a computer. One numerical point matters, because it once misled the first version of this work. A heavily braked double pendulum (section 3.3) is stiff: its hinge relaxes thousands of times faster than it swings. An ordinary explicit method (such as the classical Runge–Kutta method) must then take tiny steps, or it goes unstable, and an approximate fix gave powers wrong by up to 2 %. These notes use a three-stage Radau IIA method, an implicit method of fifth order designed for stiff equations: each step solves for the end state, so the fast relaxation is damped correctly whatever the step. Halving the step shrinks the error 30 times (fifth order); the result agrees with an independent high-accuracy solver to six parts in $10^{13}$; and it reproduces all 36 of Sol's runs, single and double, to every digit he printed.

The result for the single pendulum: the closed form (2.5) is within {{n:lin_err_max}} % of the full model at every mounting. Why so close, even where the swing reaches {{n:Th_max}} rad? Each neglected term ($\theta^3$ in the sine, $\theta\sin\varphi$ and $\theta^2\cos\varphi$ in gravity's term) either moves the swing to frequency 0 or 2, which takes nothing at first order, or changes the stiffness by a few per cent, which moves the best brake but barely the best power, because $\bar P$ is flat at its maximum.

3.2 The rod trails a little

One effect of the dropped terms is visible and easy to derive: on average, the rod is not straight out along the blade. Keep the gravity term to second order, $G\cos(\varphi+\theta) \approx G\cos\varphi - G\theta\sin\varphi$, and take the average over a turn of (1.5) with the first-order swing $\theta_1 = -\Theta\cos(\varphi - \delta)$ inside it. The average of $\theta''$ and $\theta'$ over a steady turn is zero, so

$$R\,\bar\theta = G\,\langle\theta_1\sin\varphi\rangle = -\tfrac12 G\,\Theta\sin\delta \quad\Rightarrow\quad \bar\theta = -\frac{G^2}{4R(R-1)} \ \text{ at the best brake}, \tag{3.1}$$

using $\Theta^* = G/(\sqrt2(R-1))$ and $\sin\delta = 1/\sqrt2$ from (2.2) and (2.4). The minus sign means the rod trails behind the blade's line, against the direction of rotation.

On our blade
On the slowest rotor (8 rpm, pivot at 30 %) (3.1) gives $\bar\theta = {{n:lean_theory}}$ rad, a trail of {{n:lean_deg}}°; the full model gives {{n:lean_run}} rad. The next largest trail, on the other large rotors, is {{n:lean_next_deg}}°; on the fast, small rotors it never exceeds {{n:lean_small_deg}}°.

3.3 Two rods

Swing a stick held at one end, then swing a nunchaku (two sticks joined by a short chain) of the same total weight and length. The nunchaku's far half lags and flops; its weight, on average, sits closer to your hand.

Sol's double pendulum splits the same mass and reach in two: bobs of 0.05 kg on rods of 0.1 m, with a generator at the pivot and another at the middle hinge, both with the same constant $c$. Its equations follow from the same Lagrangian with two angles, $q_1$ and $q_2$, each measured from the blade's outward direction; they were derived in SymPy and again from Newton's law in the blade frame, and agree with Sol's. Run at its best brake at every mounting, it gives {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}} times the single pendulum's power: always less.

The reason is simple once you lock the hinge. A locked double is a rigid body with mass $M$, moment of inertia $I_p$ about the pivot, and centre of mass at distance $l_c$ from it. Redo section 2 for a rigid body: the inertia $mL^2$ becomes $I_p$, the centrifugal stiffness $mr_pL\Omega^2$ becomes $Ml_c r_p\Omega^2$ (it pulls on the centre of mass), and the gravity torque $mgL$ becomes $Mgl_c$. The "$R - 1$" of (2.4) was the spring minus the inertia at the push frequency; for the rigid body it becomes $(Ml_c r_p - I_p)\Omega^2$, so

$$P^*_{\rm rigid} = \frac{(M\,l_c\,g)^2}{4\,\Omega\,(M\,l_c\,r_p - I_p)}. \tag{3.2}$$

For the single pendulum, $Ml_c = mL$ and $I_p = mL^2$, and (3.2) is (2.5) again. For the locked double, the centre of mass sits at $l_c = {{n:lc_frac}}\,L$: half the mass at 0.1 m, half at 0.2 m. On a large rotor (3.2) is then simply proportional to $Ml_c$, and the double gives 3/4 of the single.

On our blade
The locked double gives {{n:lock_min}} to {{n:lock_max}} times the single, from the fastest rotor to the slowest. Letting the hinge move, with its own generator, costs a further {{n:flex_cost_min}} to {{n:flex_cost_max}} %, giving Sol's {{n:ratio_min}} to {{n:ratio_max}}. One rod beats two because the second rod brings its mass closer to the pivot, where gravity's push has less leverage.

3.4 Does chaos help?

A double pendulum can move chaotically: two copies started a hair apart drift apart exponentially, like two leaves dropped side by side into a stream. The rate of that drift is the largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$: start two copies $10^{-8}$ apart, follow them, rescale their separation back every quarter turn, and average the logarithm of how much it grew. A regular, damped motion gives $\lambda < 0$; chaos gives $\lambda > 0$. Because chaos depends on where the motion starts, every case below is run from {{n:chaos_nic}} different starting positions, never from one.

Sol found chaos at $R = 1$: the pivot only one rod length from the hub (0.2 m), a rotor turning at 4 rad/s, and gravity's push $G = 3.06$. Repeating his group with {{n:chaos_nic}} starts each, measured over {{n:chaos_turns}} turns:
