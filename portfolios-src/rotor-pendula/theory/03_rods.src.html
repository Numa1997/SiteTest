3 Big swings, the bill, and chaos

Chapter 2 kept only the terms of (1.6) that are linear in $\theta$. This chapter solves the full equation on a computer, finds out exactly how the rotor pays, asks where along the second blade its mass is best placed, and whether the motion can turn chaotic.

3.1 The full equation, solved numerically

Push a spoon slowly through thick honey and it moves calmly; flick it and the honey stops it almost at once. A heavily braked second blade has both kinds of motion at once: a slow swing, and a very fast death of any jerk the brake fights. An equation with two such different speeds in it is called stiff.

How different are the two speeds? A small disturbance of the braked spring behaves as $e^{\mu\varphi}$, with $\mu^2 + \kappa\mu + R = 0$. At the best brake, $\kappa^* = R - 1$, the two roots are far apart. The fast one, $\mu \approx -\kappa^*$, says that a jerk of the second blade against the generator dies in $1/(\kappa^*\Omega)$: {{n:relax_8}} s at 8 m/s. The slow one, $\mu \approx -R/\kappa^*$, says that a second blade pushed off its path creeps back to it in $\kappa^*/(R\Omega)$: {{n:relax_slow8}} s. One turn lasts {{n:turn_8}} s.

Equation (1.6), with $\sin\theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta)$ kept whole, is integrated step by step on a computer. A method that computes each step only from the present state (an explicit method, such as the classical Runge–Kutta method) must take steps shorter than the fast time, or its errors grow without bound. An implicit method instead solves, at each step, an equation for the state at the end of the step, and stays stable with steps as long as the slow swing allows. These notes use one, the three-stage Radau IIA method, which is of fifth order: halving the step should cut the error by $2^5$. On this equation its error falls on average by $2^{ {{n:radau_order}} }$ per halving. The torques it uses agree with a direct sum of Newton's law over the pieces of the second blade to {{n:newton_agree}}, and after the start-up it agrees with an independent high-accuracy solver to {{n:scipy_agree}} in the harvested energy. Started from rest, its first turns carry an error of {{n:startup_err}}, because a step, 1/240 of a turn, is longer than the fast time; the runs let 60 turns pass before they measure, and by then it has died away.

The closed form (2.5) is within {{n:lin_err_max}} % of the full model at every wind, although the second blade swings up to {{n:Th_max}} rad ({{n:Th_max_deg}}°). Why so close? At second order three effects appear, and the computer can switch each on alone, holding the brake at $\kappa^*$. At 6 m/s, where the swing is largest: the term section 2.1 dropped, $-G\theta\sin\varphi$, changes the power by {{n:trm_param_6}} %; the softer spring of $\sin\theta$ by {{n:trm_sin_6}} %; and gravity's push kept whole changes it by {{n:trm_grav_6}} %. That last run contains the dropped term too, so the push's own second-order weakening, $-\tfrac12G\theta^2\cos\varphi$, is worth about {{n:trm_th2_6}} %, the largest of the three. All together, the full equation: {{n:trm_full_6}} %. The effects pull both ways and nearly cancel. At 8 and 10 m/s each is three and eight times smaller, as the swing squared (the full equation: {{n:trm_full_8}} % and {{n:trm_full_10}} %).

3.2 The second blade trails, and that is the bill

Guess first
Averaged over a turn, does the second blade sit exactly on the mother blade's line, a little ahead of it, or a little behind?
AnswerA little behind, against the direction of rotation; and that small trail is exactly what makes the rotor pay.

Keep the term that section 2.1 dropped, $-G\theta\sin\varphi$. Because the swing $\theta$ is itself of order $G$, this term is of second order in $G$. Average (1.6) over a steady turn: the averages of $\theta''$, $\theta'$ and $\cos\varphi$ vanish, and what is left is

$$R\,\bar\theta = G\,\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle, \tag{3.1}$$

with the swing (2.2), $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi-\delta)$, on the right. Its average with $\sin\varphi$ is $-\tfrac12\Theta\sin\delta$, and (2.2) gives $\sin\delta = \kappa\Theta/G$, so

$$\bar\theta = -\frac{\kappa\,\Theta^2}{2R}, \qquad\text{at the best brake}\quad \bar\theta = -\frac{G^2}{4R(R-1)}. \tag{3.2}$$

The minus sign means the second blade trails behind the mother blade's line, against the rotation, like a weight towed behind a boat.

Now compare (3.2) with the harvested power (2.3), $\bar P = I\Omega^3\cdot\tfrac12\kappa\Theta^2$. The same combination $\kappa\Theta^2$ appears in both, and since $IR = \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p$, for every brake setting

$$\boxed{\bar P = -\,\frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\Omega^3\,\bar\theta.} \tag{3.3}$$

The trail is the bill. The exact version holds for any steady swing, large or small. Write out the rotor's torque on the second blade, $\tau_{\rm rot}$ of section 1.8, for a steadily turning rotor, and use (1.5) to remove the second blade's angular acceleration. SymPy and a page of algebra give

$$\tau_{\rm rot} = -\frac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta + mgr_p\cos\varphi + \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{ml}{2}r_p\,\frac{d\sin\theta}{dt} + ml\,r_p\Omega\cos\theta - c\,\theta\right].$$

The second term is the rotor carrying the second blade's weight round, which averages to zero over a turn. The bracket is a total time derivative of something that returns to its value every turn, so it averages to zero too; it holds the generator's torque, $-c\dot\theta = -d(c\theta)/dt$. Only the first term survives the average, so $\bar P = \langle\tau_{\rm rot}\rangle\Omega = -\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^3\langle\sin\theta\rangle$, exactly. (With the geared generator of chapter 7 the identity changes, because the gearbox's own torque enters the rotor's.)

Figure 8 shows the mechanism. The second blade, flung outwards, pulls on the hinge with the force $m\Omega^2$ times its centre of mass's distance from the hub. The pull acts at the hinge and points the way the centre of mass lies as seen from the hub. When the second blade trails, its centre of mass sits a little behind the mother blade's line, so that direction tilts backwards by a small angle, and a line through the hinge in that direction misses the hub by about $\tfrac l2\sin\theta$: a lever arm, and it pulls the rotor backwards with the torque $\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta$, negative while the second blade trails. That is the first term above with its sign reversed: the rotor's torque on the second blade balances the second blade's torque on the rotor. The rotor must push against that pull, all the time, and that steady push is the power it pays.

3.3 Where the mass sits

Swing a hammer held at the very end of its handle, then grip the same hammer halfway up. The second swing is easier and weaker: the same mass now sits nearer your hand, with less leverage. Spreading the second blade's mass along its length does the same to it.

Section 1.5 noted that (1.5) holds for any rigid body on the hinge, with its own moment of inertia $I_p$ and first moment $Ml_c$. Redo chapter 2 for such a body: $I$ becomes $I_p$, the first moment $ml/2$ becomes $Ml_c$, and (2.5) becomes

$$P^*_{\rm rigid} = \frac{(M\,l_c\,g)^2}{4\,\Omega\,(M\,l_c\,r_p - I_p)}. \tag{3.4}$$

On a large rotor this is simply proportional to the first moment $Ml_c$, as (2.7) said for the rod. So the same mass placed farther from the hinge gives more, in proportion to the distance of its centre of mass. A uniform blade has its centre of mass halfway out; the same mass as a lump at its end has it all the way out, and gives, by (3.4), {{n:rod_vs_point}} times as much as the uniform blade, almost exactly twice. A real blade-shaped second blade tapers towards its free end like the mother blade, which puts its centre of mass nearer the hinge than halfway: for harvesting, the wrong way round.

Check
For the lump on a light rod, $I_p = mL^2$ and $Ml_c = mL$, and (3.4) is $mLg^2/(4\Omega|r_p - L|)$ of section 2.3. For the uniform rod, $I_p = ml^2/3$ and $Ml_c = ml/2$, and (3.4) is (2.5).

3.4 Chaos and the harvest

{{table:chaos_text}}
What to remember
  • The braked second blade has two time scales: a jerk dies in about a hundredth of a second, a displaced swing creeps back in about a second. That makes its equation stiff, and an implicit solver handles it.
  • The full equation agrees with the small-swing power (2.5) to a third of a per cent.
  • On average the second blade trails slightly behind the mother blade. Its outward pull then holds the rotor back, and that torque times $\Omega$ is exactly the harvested power: the trail is the bill.
  • Mass far from the hinge is worth more: the same mass as a lump at the end gives twice what a uniform rod gives.
  • The motion is regular, never chaotic, at every wind and brake tried: the centrifugal spring holds the second blade too firmly.
Problems
  1. At 10 m/s, how long does a second blade pushed off its path take to creep back, at the best brake?
    Answer$\kappa^*/(R\Omega)$, with $\Omega$ = {{n:om10}} rad/s at 10 m/s: {{n:relax_slow10}} s. It is shorter than at 8 m/s because the rotor turns faster; counted in turns, it is the same.
  2. Use (3.3) and the full model's trail at 6 m/s, {{n:lean_run}} rad, to estimate the harvested power. Compare the full model's {{n:P6_full}} mW.
    Answer$\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^3|\bar\theta|$ = {{n:p3_bill}} mW, about one per cent above. The exact identity uses $\langle\sin\theta\rangle$ over the full swing rather than the small-swing relation (3.3), and with it the agreement is exact.
  3. Which way does the second blade's pull on the hinge turn the rotor when the second blade trails, and which way when it leads? Why can it, on average, only trail?
    AnswerTrailing, its pull holds the rotor back; leading, it would push the rotor on. A second blade that led on average would give the rotor energy while also feeding the generator, from nothing. So with a brake taking energy, energy conservation demands a trail.
  4. A 1 kg lump on a massless 1 m rod is compared with a uniform 1 kg rod of 1 m. Which needs the stronger generator, and by how much?
    AnswerThe lump: its first moment, $mL$ = 1 kg m, is twice the rod's, $ml/2$ = 0.5 kg m, and the generator's torque at the best brake, (first moment)$\cdot g/\sqrt2$, is proportional to it: twice as large.
