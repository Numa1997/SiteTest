3 Big swings, the bill, and chaos

Chapter 2 dropped every term that is not linear in $\theta$. This chapter solves the full equation, finds out who really pays and how, asks where along the second blade its mass is best placed, and whether chaos can help.

3.1 The full equation, solved numerically

Push a spoon slowly through thick honey and it moves calmly; flick it and the honey stops it almost at once. A heavily braked blade has both kinds of motion at once: a slow swing, and a very fast relaxation of any motion the brake fights. That makes its equation "stiff".

Equation (1.5), with $\sin\theta$ and $\cos(\varphi+\theta)$ kept whole, is integrated step by step on a computer. A simple step-by-step method that only looks at the present moment (an explicit method, such as the classical Runge–Kutta method) must take steps shorter than the fastest relaxation, or its errors grow without bound. Here that relaxation is fast indeed: at the best brake a disturbance of the swing dies in $1/(\kappa^*\Omega)$, {{n:relax_8}} s at 8 m/s, while one turn lasts {{n:turn_8}} s. An implicit method instead solves, at each step, for the state at the end of the step, and stays stable with steps as long as the slow swing allows. These notes use one such method, a three-stage Radau IIA method of fifth order. On this equation its error falls on average {{n:radau_order}} powers of two each time the step is halved, its torques agree with Newton's law summed over the second blade to {{n:newton_agree}}, and after the start-up it agrees with an independent high-accuracy solver to {{n:scipy_agree}} in the harvested energy. (From rest, the first turns carry a start-up error of {{n:startup_err}}: the brake's relaxation is shorter than one step of 240 per turn. It dies away in the 60 turns the runs let pass before they measure.)

The closed form (2.5) is within {{n:lin_err_max}} % of the full model at every wind, although the second blade swings up to {{n:Th_max}} rad ({{n:Th_max_deg}}°). Two reasons make the agreement plausible. At second order in the swing three effects appear: the term $-\tfrac12G\theta^2\cos\varphi$ changes gravity's push itself, by about $\Theta^2/4$ of it; the cubic term of $\sin\theta$ softens the spring; and the mean trail of section 3.2 feeds back through $-G\bar\theta\sin\varphi$. These notes have not measured the three separately; the full model shows only their sum. They partly cancel, and what remains acts near the flat top of Figure 5, where a small change in the push or the stiffness moves the best brake but hardly the best power.

3.2 The second blade trails, and that is the bill

One effect of the dropped terms is visible and easy to derive: on average, the second blade is not straight out along the mother blade. Keep the gravity term to second order, $G\cos(\varphi+\theta) \approx G\cos\varphi - G\theta\sin\varphi$, and average (1.5) over a steady turn. The averages of $\theta''$, $\theta'$ and $\cos\varphi$ vanish, and what is left is

$$R\,\bar\theta = G\,\langle\theta\sin\varphi\rangle, \tag{3.1}$$

with $\theta$ on the right the swing (2.2), $\theta = -\Theta\cos(\varphi-\delta)$. Its average with $\sin\varphi$ is $-\tfrac12\Theta\sin\delta$, and (2.2) gives $\sin\delta = \kappa\Theta/G$, so

$$\bar\theta = -\frac{\kappa\,\Theta^2}{2R}, \qquad\text{at the best brake}\quad \bar\theta = -\frac{G^2}{4R(R-1)}. \tag{3.2}$$

The minus sign means the second blade trails behind the mother blade's line, against the direction of rotation, like a weight towed behind a boat.

Now compare (3.2) with the harvested power (2.3), $\bar P = I\Omega^3\cdot\tfrac12\kappa\Theta^2$. The same combination $\kappa\Theta^2$ appears in both, and since $IR = \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p$, for every brake setting

$$\boxed{\bar P = -\,\frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\Omega^3\,\bar\theta.} \tag{3.3}$$

The trail is the bill. In fact the exact version, $\bar P = -\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^3\langle\sin\theta\rangle$, holds for any steady swing, large or small, with the ideal brake: the rotor's torque of section 1.4 is $-\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta + mgr_p\cos\varphi$ plus terms that are exact time derivatives, and over a steady turn those and $\cos\varphi$ average to zero. (With the geared generator of chapter 7 the identity changes, because the gearbox's own torque enters the rotor's.) There is a picture behind it. The second blade, flung outwards, pulls on the hinge, in the direction pointing from the hub to its own centre of mass, with the force $m\Omega^2$ times that centre's distance from the hub. When the second blade trails, its centre of mass sits a little behind the mother blade's line, so that pull, applied at the hinge, is no longer aimed straight away from the hub: it has a lever arm about the hub, $r_p$ times the sine of the small angle between the two lines, and it pulls the rotor backwards. Worked out, its torque is $\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta$, the term in the rotor's torque above. The rotor must push against it, all the time, and that steady push is the power it pays.

On our blade
At 6 m/s, where the swing is largest, (3.2) gives $\bar\theta = {{n:lean_theory}}$ rad, a trail of {{n:lean_deg}}°; the full model gives {{n:lean_run}} rad. In all nine runs of the full model (three winds, three brakes) the exact identity holds to {{n:bill_err}}.

3.3 Where the mass sits

Swing a hammer held at the very end of its handle, then grip the same hammer halfway up. The second swing is easier and weaker: the same mass now sits closer to your hand, so it has less leverage. Spreading the second blade's mass along its length does the same thing to it.

Section 1.3 noted that the equation holds for any rigid body on the hinge, with its own moment of inertia $I_p$ and first moment $Ml_c$. Redo chapter 2 for such a body: the inertia $I$ becomes $I_p$, the first moment $ml/2$ becomes $Ml_c$, and (2.5) becomes

$$P^*_{\rm rigid} = \frac{(M\,l_c\,g)^2}{4\,\Omega\,(M\,l_c\,r_p - I_p)}. \tag{3.4}$$

On a large rotor this is simply proportional to the first moment $Ml_c$, as (2.7) said for the rod. So the same mass placed farther from the hinge gives more, in proportion to the distance of its centre of mass. A uniform blade has its centre of mass halfway out; the same mass in a lump at its end would have it all the way out, and gives, from (3.4), {{n:rod_vs_point}} times as much as the uniform blade, almost exactly twice. A real second blade is neither: like the mother blade it tapers, so its centre of mass sits nearer the hinge than halfway, and it gives somewhat less than a uniform one. If the second blade's only job were to harvest, it should be heavy at its tip; a blade shaped for the air is the opposite.

Check
For the point bob, $I_p = mL^2$ and $Ml_c = mL$, and (3.4) is the bob's $mLg^2/(4\Omega|r_p - L|)$. For the uniform rod, $I_p = ml^2/3$ and $Ml_c = ml/2$, and (3.4) is (2.5).
