9 The answer

9.1 The ceiling

Lower a bag of shopping on a rope: the power you can take from it is its weight times the speed at which it falls, however it falls. The second blade is the same, with its weight acting at its centre of mass, $l/2$ from the hinge, and moving relative to the mother blade at the speed $\tfrac l2|\dot\theta|$.

Part I's mechanics meets in one inequality. Watch the second blade from the turning mother blade, the rotor steady and the mother blade rigid. There the centrifugal pull is a fixed field with a potential, giving back over a turn what it takes; the Coriolis force is at right angles to the motion and does no work; gravity, whose direction turns, is the only force that can feed the swing on average. Section 2.3 wrote the balance out: over a steady turn the generator's mean power equals the mean of gravity's torque times $\dot\theta$ (seen from the ground, the same energy comes from the rotor). Its torque, $-\tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, is never larger than $\tfrac{ml}{2}g$, so every watt the generator can take obeys

$$\bar P \le \frac{ml}{2}\,g\,\langle|\dot\theta|\rangle \tag{9.1}$$

for any motion, small or large, regular or chaotic, with any brake and gearbox. (A gearbox changes how the ground frame's bill is paid, section 4.2, but in the turning frame it only adds inertia on the hinge's axis, which feels neither pull, and losses, which take energy and never feed it.) To harvest more the second blade must move faster, and a stiff centrifugal spring does not let it. At the best brake, $\langle|\theta'|\rangle = 2\Theta^*/\pi$ and $P^* = \tfrac12\tau^*\Omega\Theta^*$ with $\tau^* = \tfrac{ml}{2}g/\sqrt2$, so

$$\frac{P^*}{\tfrac{ml}{2}g\,\Omega\cdot 2\Theta^*/\pi} = \frac{\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}}{2/\pi} = \frac{\pi}{4\sqrt2} = {{n:ceiling_frac}}:$$

the best brake already takes more than half of what gravity could ever give at that swing. The one lever that always works is the first moment $\tfrac{ml}{2}$, limited by the generator, whose best brake and torque grow with it (section 5.5). What the real wind adds lies outside (9.1), each with a feed of its own: the rotor's wandering speed and the blade's sag a few per cent (sections 6.5 and 8.1), the blade's vibration an amount resting on an unpublished level (section 8.2), the air on a lifting second blade a great deal (chapter 7).

9.2 What the second blade can and cannot do

The question was how much electricity a second blade hinged at a turbine blade's tip can make, and who pays for it.

  • A uniform second blade of {{n:mB}} kg and {{n:lB}} m takes {{n:P_high}} to {{n:P_low}} mW from its swing in steady winds of 6 to 10 m/s, with the rotor at its best tip-speed ratio and the generator at the best brake, $\kappa^* = R - 1$ (chapters 3 and 4). At 6 m/s the turbine's real control turns the rotor faster and the second blade's power changes by {{n:Preal_pct_6}} % (section 6.5). The power grows with the first moment $ml/2$ and falls with the speed of the hinge: it is largest in light wind.
  • The rotor pays for every joule, through a slight trail of the second blade behind the mother blade's line, a torque far too small for the rotor to notice (sections 1.3 and 4.2).
  • A real motor and gearbox from a catalogue deliver {{n:g4_min}} to {{n:g4_max}} mW to the load, more than {{n:g4_margin_round}} times what the minimal sensor of section 1.1 needs, and still hundreds of times if the gearbox proves twice as draggy and less efficient than its catalogue (chapter 5). Turning the slow alternating voltage into a steady supply is a problem of electronics left open.
  • Gusts change the power by a few per cent, through the rotor's wandering speed, and so does the mother blade's sag (sections 6.5 and 8.1). The blade's own vibration is the open question: little at a 5 mm tip vibration, more than gravity at 100 mm (section 8.2).
  • For a streamlined second blade, with no lift on average, the air changes little in steady wind and adds a few per cent in gusts. A lifting one gains about two fifths in steady wind and harvests the gusts far more; any shape other than a streamlined one must be designed with the air and the swing solved together (chapter 7).

Last, the double pendulum. The story began with a toy that never repeats itself, and ends with a machine that repeats itself every turn. The difference is the hand that holds the first arm. Free, the two arms trade energy, chaotically. Held by a rotor of forty million kilogram square metres, the first arm supplies whatever torque (1.5) asks and turns on. What is left is a pendulum in two gravities, swinging ten times per turn in a landscape that changes once: it keeps its action, and its motion is regular (section 1.5). Chaos survives only in a thin band where the swing nearly reaches a hilltop that rises and falls each turn, and near resonance; the brake empties the band, and a turbine never comes near resonance. The wind's randomness, which might have revived it, cannot feed more than a fixed share of the second blade's mass times the shaking's strength, chaotic or not. A fifth of a watt, regular, paid for by the rotor without its noticing: that is what a double pendulum gives when the wind holds its first arm.
