Part IIRandomness: shaking, gusts and a bending blade

4 A ceiling on random shaking

Wind is gusty, and a blade in gusty wind shakes. Could the second blade, perhaps moving chaotically, turn that randomness into extra power? Before any model of the wind, one exact result sets a limit. To keep it clean, this chapter treats a body (or a chain of bodies) hinged to a point that is shaken at random while it hangs in gravity, with no rotor; the last paragraph says what carries over to the turning blade.

4.1 Random kicks add energy through their square

Stand on a bus over a rough road with a cup of coffee. Every jolt kicks the coffee, sometimes with its slosh and sometimes against it. On average the "with" and "against" cancel, yet the coffee still sloshes harder the rougher the road: a kick adds kinetic energy even when it is equally likely to help or oppose the motion, because energy goes as the square of velocity.

Start with the simplest case, a small mass $m$ that can move only along a direction $\hat t$ (across its rod). Shake its pivot to and fro along a direction $\hat e$. In the pivot's frame each jolt is a sudden extra force on the mass; over a short time it changes its velocity along $\hat t$ by a small random amount $\Delta v$. The kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m v^2$ changes by $m v\,\Delta v + \tfrac12 m\,\Delta v^2$. The first term is as often negative as positive and averages to zero. The second is never negative. If the shaking's strength is $S$ (in m²/s³: the spread, the variance, of the velocity kicks it gives per second), the kicks along $\hat t$ have variance $S(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$ per second, and the shaking feeds in, on average, the power $\tfrac12 S\,m(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$. That is the whole idea. The next paragraphs say the same for any body or chain of bodies, in the language of Lagrangian mechanics; readers who skip them lose nothing but the proof, and can go on to the result (4.2) and its plain-words form after (4.4).

Shake the pivot along a fixed direction $\hat e$ with acceleration $a(t)$. In the pivot's frame every piece of mass $dm$ feels an extra force $-a(t)\,\hat e\,dm$. For a body (or a chain of bodies) with angles $q_k$, that force enters Lagrange's equations as a generalised force

$$Q_k = -a(t)\,b_k(q), \qquad b_k = \int \hat e\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf x}{\partial q_k}\,dm = \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k}\left(M\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf x_{\rm cm}\right), \tag{4.1}$$

the rate at which turning the angle $q_k$ moves the whole mass $M$ along the shaking, through its centre of mass $\mathbf x_{\rm cm}$. Let the shaking be white noise of strength $S$: kicks so quick and so uncorrelated that $\langle a(t)a(t')\rangle = S\,\delta(t - t')$ (the Dirac delta $\delta$ says that the shaking at two different instants is unrelated, however close the instants are). Over a short time $dt$ the momenta $p_k = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_k$ of the angles receive a random kick $\Delta p = -b\,\sqrt{S}\,dW$, where $dW$ is a random number of mean zero and variance $dt$. Why $\sqrt{dt}$? Within $dt$ there are many independent tiny kicks; like the steps of a random walk, their sum grows as the square root of their number, so as $\sqrt{dt}$, and its square is of order $dt$. That is why the square of the kick, which would be negligible for a smooth force, survives here. The kinetic energy is $K = \tfrac12 \dot q^{\mathsf T}A\,\dot q = \tfrac12 p^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}p$, with $A(q)$ the mass matrix (for one rigid body on its pivot, simply its moment of inertia). Expand to second order in the kick:

$$\Delta K = \dot q\cdot\Delta p + \tfrac12\,\Delta p^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}\Delta p.$$

The first term averages to zero: the kick is as likely to help as to oppose. The second does not: its average is $\tfrac12 S\,b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}b\,dt$. So on average the shaking feeds in the power

$$\boxed{P_{\rm in} = \frac S2\,\big\langle m_{\rm eff}\big\rangle, \qquad m_{\rm eff} = b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}b.} \tag{4.2}$$

Nothing else entered: not the stiffness, not the damping, not gravity, not whether the motion is regular or chaotic. In a steady state everything fed in is dissipated, and the generator is where it is dissipated. (One subtlety of random kicks is that the result can depend on whether the kick is evaluated at the start or in the middle of each step; that matters only when the kick's strength depends on the velocity it kicks. Here $b$ depends only on the angles, so the two readings agree. The algebra is checked in SymPy for a bob, for two bobs on two rods, and for the uniform rod.)

4.2 The effective mass never exceeds the real mass

$m_{\rm eff}$ measures how much of the body's mass the shaking can grab. For one body on its pivot it is $b^2/I_p$. For a small bob, $m_{\rm eff} = m(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$: the full mass when the bob can move along the shaking, none when its rod points along it. For our second blade, $b = \tfrac{ml}{2}\cos\psi$, where $\psi$ is the angle between the shaking and the direction the blade's pieces move, and

$$m_{\rm eff} = \frac{\left(\tfrac{ml}{2}\right)^2\cos^2\psi}{ml^2/3} = \tfrac34\,m\cos^2\psi \le \tfrac34\,m.$$

A uniform blade can give the shaking at most three quarters of its mass: its pieces near the hinge hardly move, and pay for their share of the kinetic energy without catching their share of the kicks. In general, the proof takes three lines. For any velocity $\dot q$, the pieces move with velocities $\mathbf v$, the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}A\dot q = \tfrac12\int|\mathbf v|^2dm$, and $b\cdot\dot q = \int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm$. Then

$$m_{\rm eff} = \max_{\dot q}\frac{(b\cdot\dot q)^2}{\dot q^{\mathsf T}A\dot q} = \max\frac{\left(\int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm\right)^2}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le \frac{\left(\int dm\right)\left(\int(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v)^2dm\right)}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le M_{\rm total}, \tag{4.3}$$

The first step is itself the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, in the product $u^{\mathsf T}Av$: $(b\cdot\dot q)^2 \le (b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}b)(\dot q^{\mathsf T}A\dot q)$, with equality at $\dot q = A^{-1}b$. The second is Cauchy–Schwarz again, and the third holds because $(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v)^2 \le |\mathbf v|^2$. Equality needs every piece to move along $\hat e$, all with the same velocity: the body must be able to carry all of its mass along the shaking at once, which a body turning on a hinge never can. So

$$P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\,M_{\rm total}, \qquad\text{and for the second blade}\quad P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\cdot\frac34\,m. \tag{4.4}$$

In plain words: random shaking cannot feed in more than $S/2$ times the body's mass, whatever the body does, regular or chaotic, and a rod on a hinge takes at most three quarters of that.

Check
A computer experiment on the general theorem, made for an earlier version of this study: a bob of 0.1 kg on 0.2 m and a double pendulum of the same mass and reach hang in gravity, braked with a damping ratio of 0.2, and their pivot is shaken sideways with white noise of strength $S = 0.028$ m²/s³ (gentle: swings of 0.05 rad) or $S = 48$ m²/s³ (violent: swings of more than a radian, the double's top rod tumbling right over about a hundred times in each 40-second window). For each case 1000 independent runs compare the generator's power with $\tfrac S2\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$: they agree within {{n:mc_dev_min}} to {{n:mc_dev_max}} %, at statistical errors of {{n:mc_se_min}} to {{n:mc_se_max}} %. In the violent runs $\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$ falls to {{n:meff_single_large}} of the total mass for the bob and {{n:meff_double_chaos}} for the tumbling double. The rod's value $\tfrac34 m\cos^2\psi$ is checked in SymPy.

On the turning blade the second blade is also driven by gravity's once-per-turn push, paid for by the rotor. The noise's own feed is still (4.2), whatever else drives the motion, because its first-order term averages to zero in any state. But the noise also changes the motion, and with it what gravity's push feeds in, which can rise or fall; (4.4) bounds the noise's own feed, not that indirect change. The mother blade's edgewise shaking, in the rotor plane and across the mother blade, is at right angles to a second blade lying along it, so the second blade starts with its full effective mass, $\tfrac34 m$; any wandering can only lower it. A real blade, though, does not shake as white noise: it shakes mostly near its own edgewise frequency, about 1.08 Hz on the 5 MW reference turbine of chapter 5 (1.0793 Hz in Jonkman et al. 2009, Table 9-1). Then what matters is how the second blade responds near that one frequency, and shaking at one slow frequency acts like a push, as gravity's does. Chapter 6 works it out.

5 Gusts and the rotor's speed

So far the rotor turned at a steady speed. In gusty wind it does not: a gust speeds it up over some seconds, a lull slows it down. To see what that does to the second blade we need the turbine's own dynamics and a real wind.

The NREL 5 MW turbine's rotor and generator together have a moment of inertia $J = 4.38\times10^7$ kg m², and the rotor's speed obeys $J\dot\Omega = Q_{\rm aero} - Q_{\rm gen}(\Omega)$: the wind's torque, $Q_{\rm aero} = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^3v^2C_p(\lambda)/\lambda$ with the tip-speed ratio $\lambda = \Omega r_{\rm rot}/v$ and the power coefficient $C_p(\lambda)$ from NREL's published table, against the generator's torque, which the controller sets as a function of $\Omega$. At a steady 8 m/s the two torques balance at {{n:rpm_ctrl_8}} rpm, {{n:ctrl_vs_lam}} % below the $\lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$ of section 0.3, because the controller's torque law holds the rotor a little off its best tip-speed ratio. The wind is the standard model of gusty wind used to design turbines (IEC 61400-1, edition 3, turbulence class A, as implemented in NREL's TurbSim): at 8 m/s its speed fluctuates with a standard deviation of 1.86 m/s, with the Kaimal spectrum, mostly over tens of seconds. The rotor feels the average over its disc: two points of the disc a distance apart fluctuate together only at low frequencies (the IEC coherence), so the disc average is smoother than the wind at the hub. In {{n:l2_records}} ten-minute records the rotor's speed wanders about its mean with a standard deviation of {{n:sigOm}} % of the mean.

A metronome on a train that slowly speeds up and slows down keeps its own beat, so a push timed to the train's wheels would drift in and out of step with it. Our second blade's beat, $\sqrt R$ per turn, and gravity's push, once per turn, both ride on the rotor's speed, so the two stay in step whatever the rotor does.

The gusts reach the second blade in two ways. They make the rotor speed up and slow down, which adds a small torque (the Euler terms of (1.3), with $\ddot\varphi$); and they move the rotor's speed away from its steady value, which changes the power through (2.3). The first is negligible: it changes the power by about {{n:l2_euler_parts}} parts in 10 000. The second has two parts. With the generator constant fixed, the power at speed $\Omega$ is, from (2.3) with $\kappa = \kappa^*$ at the steady speed $\Omega_0$, exactly $\bar P(\Omega)/\bar P(\Omega_0) = 2/(1 + \Omega^2/\Omega_0^2)$. Its slope at $\Omega_0$ is $-1$: a shift of the mean speed by a fraction $\epsilon$ changes the power by $-\epsilon$. And it curves upwards (its second derivative in $\Omega/\Omega_0$ is $+1$ there), so a speed that wanders about a fixed mean raises the average power, whichever way it wanders: by about $s^2/2$ for a relative spread $s$, {{n:jensen}} % here.

On our blade
Each of the {{n:l2_records}} records is run twice through the full equation of the second blade at the tip, once with the Euler terms and once without, and compared with steady rotation at the record's own mean speed and at the steady speed. With a pure square-law controller ($Q_{\rm gen} \propto \Omega^2$) the second blade gains {{n:l2_k2_tot}} % over steady operation at 8 m/s: the wandering speed gives {{n:l2_k2_wnd}} %, more than the {{n:jensen}} % the curvature alone predicts (these notes have not separated where the rest comes from), and the mean speed moves by {{n:l2_k2_dOm}} % and costs {{n:l2_k2_msh}} %, as the slope of $-1$ predicts. With the turbine's full controller the result changes sign, to {{n:l2_nrel_tot}} %: at 8 m/s the full controller works just above a kink in its torque law, at 8.98 rpm. Below the kink the generator's torque falls away steeply, so in a lull the rotor is braked less and slows less than it speeds up in a gust; the average speed rises by {{n:l2_nrel_dOm}} %, and the power falls by {{n:l2_nrel_msh}} %, again as the slope predicts, while the wander still adds {{n:l2_nrel_wnd}} %. That is a property of this turbine's controller, not of the wind. Either way, gusts change the second blade's power by a few per cent at most.

6 The blade bends and shakes

A real blade is not rigid. It bends under its own weight, once per turn, and it shakes in gusty wind near its own edgewise frequency. Both move the hinge, and at the tip they move it most.

6.1 The sag: a large push that cancels itself

Hold a long, springy ruler out sideways and turn your wrist slowly: the ruler sags first one way and then the other, following gravity, and its end sags most. A blade 61.5 m long does the same as it turns, in its own plane.

From the NREL blade's mass and stiffness tables, gravity bends it edgewise by {{n:sag_lo_mm}} to {{n:sag_hi_mm}} mm at the tip, depending on how the bend's shape is taken, once per turn and in step with gravity's push. The hinge therefore moves sideways by $w\cos\varphi$. Put a moving hinge into the Lagrangian of chapter 1 and linearise (done in SymPy): the hinge's motion adds $2\Omega^2w$ to gravity's push $g$, once from the hinge's own acceleration and once from the shift of the centrifugal pull as the hinge moves sideways, exactly as it would for a bob. On its own that would raise the power by {{n:sag_only_lo}} to {{n:sag_only_hi}} % (the power goes as the square of the push, (2.5)).

But a bending blade also turns: its cross-section rotates with the slope of the bend, and at the tip the slope is largest. The generator's stator, fixed in the mother blade, rotates with it. The generator brakes the second blade's motion relative to the stator, $-c(\dot\theta - \dot\beta)$, where $\beta$ is the section's angle; so the turning stator drags on the second blade with the extra torque $c\dot\beta$. That drag follows the rate of change of the bend's slope, a quarter turn out of step with the sag's push, while at the best brake the swing lags its push by 45° (section 2.3); so part of the drag works against the swing. The two effects largely cancel. What is left is a small difference of two large numbers, so it depends on the exact shape of the bend: taking the shape from the blade's first mode of vibration, or integrating the static bend directly from the same blade tables, gives a net change of {{n:sag_tot_lo}} to {{n:sag_tot_hi}} %. (For the first shape, linear theory and a full nonlinear run with the moving, turning hinge agree within {{n:sag_full_gap}} percentage points.) The lesson: a generator brakes motion relative to its own frame, so when that frame moves, the frame's motion counts too.

6.2 Random shaking

Gusts make the blade's first edgewise mode vibrate near 1.08 Hz. How strongly, no source we could open says for this blade, so the notes take a range: a tip vibration of 5, 20 or 100 mm rms. The model has two parts, coupled both ways. The blade's first edgewise mode is one oscillator: a modal mass of {{n:sh_mb}} kg on a spring tuned to 1.08 Hz, with a damping ratio $\zeta_b$ of 0.5 % or 3 % of critical, driven by a random force whose spectrum is flat near 1.08 Hz and scaled so that the blade alone would vibrate at the stated tip level. At the tip the hinge moves sideways with the tip itself, and the section turns by {{n:sh_dphi}} mrad per metre of tip motion. The second blade is (2.1) with its hinge shaken sideways and its stator turned by that much, and it pushes back on the blade's mode with the force and torque it needs. Gravity's push is left out of this part (it is in chapter 2 and section 6.1). Everything is linear, so the response is computed frequency by frequency, and the generator's mean power is the brake times the mean square relative speed. The full linear equations, with the second blade integrated along its length and the turning frame's centrifugal and Coriolis terms, were derived in SymPy. Since the model is linear, the swing from shaking and the swing from gravity add; but the brake that takes most from shaking is not the one that takes most from gravity ({{n:sh_c_one}} N m s against {{n:sh_cstar_one}} N m s), so the two best powers cannot simply be added. The model checks that the second blade never takes more than the gusts feed into the blade.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration the gusts feed {{n:sh_in_5}} W into the blade's vibration, and the {{n:lB}} m second blade, at its best brake for the shaking, takes {{n:sh_one_5}} mW of it, against {{n:grav_one}} mW from gravity. At 20 mm it takes {{n:sh_one_20}} mW; at 100 mm, the top of the range, {{n:sh_one_100}} mW, more than gravity gives. Chaos plays no part: at $R$ near {{n:R_round}} the second blade is held firmly along the mother blade.

The {{n:lB}} m second blade takes so little at small vibrations because its own frequency, $\sqrt R$ times the rotor's, is {{n:freq_ratio}} times the blade's: it is far from the blade's resonance. A second blade tuned to the blade, with its own frequency equal to 1.08 Hz at the mean rotor speed, needs $\Omega\sqrt{3r_p/(2l)} = \omega_e$, a length of {{n:tu_L}} m. With the same mass per metre it weighs {{n:tu_m}} kg. It is then a tuned mass damper, the device that calms swaying skyscrapers, and it draws on the blade's vibration directly.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration and a blade damping of 0.5 % the tuned second blade takes {{n:tu_P_lo}} mW, {{n:tu_share_lo}} % of what the gusts feed into the blade, and calms the blade's tip motion by {{n:tu_tipcut_lo}} %; at 3 % blade damping it takes {{n:tu_P_hi}} mW. Its swing is {{n:tu_q_lo}} rad rms. Three catches. First, the rotor's speed wanders by {{n:sigOm}} %, and the tuned blade's frequency wanders with it while the mother blade's does not; averaged over that wander the gain falls to {{n:tu_Pw_lo}} mW. Second, at 20 mm of tip vibration the linear model asks it to swing {{n:tu_q20_lo}} rad rms; at 100 mm, {{n:tu_q100_lo}} rad, where a linear model no longer describes it. Third, and worst, this model leaves gravity out of the stiffness. Gravity's term $-G\theta\sin\varphi$ (section 2.1) stiffens and softens the second blade once per turn, by $\pm G/R$ of its stiffness, so by $\pm G/(2R) = \pm1/(2\Gamma) = \pm{{n:tu_mod_pct}}$ % of its frequency at 8 m/s, whatever its length, while the blade's resonance is only 1 to 6 % wide (twice the damping ratio). So the tuned blade is detuned and retuned every turn, and its true gain may be somewhat below these numbers or several times below them. The tuned second blade is an idea these notes have not shown to work; only a calculation in time, with gravity kept whole, can say how much of it survives. For comparison, the longer blade also takes {{n:grav_tuned}} mW from gravity alone, simply because its first moment is larger.
