Part IIRandomness: shaking, gusts and a bending blade

4 A ceiling on random shaking

Wind is gusty, and a blade in gusty wind shakes. Could a pendulum, perhaps a chaotic one, turn that randomness into extra power? Before any model of the wind, one exact result sets a limit. To keep it clean, this chapter treats a pendulum (or a chain of pendula) whose pivot is shaken at random while it hangs in gravity, with no rotor; the last paragraph says what carries over to the blade.

4.1 Random kicks add energy through their square

Stand on a bus over a rough road with a cup of coffee. Every jolt kicks the coffee, sometimes with its slosh and sometimes against it. On average the "with" and "against" cancel, yet the coffee still sloshes harder the rougher the road: a kick adds kinetic energy even when it is equally likely to help or oppose the motion, because energy goes as the square of velocity.

Start with one pendulum, a bob of mass $m$ that can move only along a direction $\hat t$ (across its rod). Shake its pivot to and fro along a direction $\hat e$. In the pivot's frame each jolt is a sudden extra force on the bob; over a short time it changes the bob's velocity along $\hat t$ by a small random amount $\Delta v$. The kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m v^2$ changes by $m v\,\Delta v + \tfrac12 m\,\Delta v^2$. The first term is as often negative as positive and averages to zero. The second is never negative. If the shaking's strength is $S$ (in m²/s³: the spread, the variance, of the velocity kicks it gives per second), the kicks along $\hat t$ have variance $S(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$ per second, and the shaking feeds in, on average, the power $\tfrac12 S\,m(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$. That is the whole idea. The next paragraphs say the same for a chain of pendula, in the language of Lagrangian mechanics; readers who skip them lose nothing but the proof, and can go on to the result (4.2) and its plain-words form after (4.4).

Shake the pivot along a fixed direction $\hat e$ with acceleration $a(t)$. In the pivot's frame each mass $m_i$ feels an extra force $-m_i a(t)\hat e$. For a pendulum (or a chain of pendula) with angles $q_k$, that force enters Lagrange's equations as a generalised force

$$Q_k = -a(t)\,b_k(q), \qquad b_k = \sum_i m_i\,\hat e\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf x_i}{\partial q_k}. \tag{4.1}$$

Let the shaking be white noise of strength $S$: kicks so quick and so uncorrelated that $\langle a(t)a(t')\rangle = S\,\delta(t - t')$ (the Dirac delta $\delta$ says that the shaking at two different instants is unrelated, however close the instants are). Over a short time $dt$ the momenta $p_k = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_k$ of the angles receive a random kick $\Delta p = -b\,\sqrt{S}\,dW$, where $dW$ is a random number of mean zero and variance $dt$. Why $\sqrt{dt}$? Within $dt$ there are many independent tiny kicks; like the steps of a random walk, their sum grows as the square root of their number, so as $\sqrt{dt}$, and its square is of order $dt$. That is why the square of the kick, which would be negligible for a smooth force, survives here. The kinetic energy is $K = \tfrac12 \dot q^{\mathsf T}A\,\dot q = \tfrac12 p^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}p$, with $A(q)$ the chain's mass matrix (for one pendulum, simply $mL^2$). Expand to second order in the kick:

$$\Delta K = \dot q\cdot\Delta p + \tfrac12\,\Delta p^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}\Delta p.$$

The first term averages to zero: the kick is as likely to help as to oppose. The second does not: its average is $\tfrac12 S\,b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}b\,dt$. So on average the shaking feeds in the power

$$\boxed{P_{\rm in} = \frac S2\,\big\langle m_{\rm eff}\big\rangle, \qquad m_{\rm eff} = b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}b.} \tag{4.2}$$

Nothing else entered: not the stiffness, not the damping, not gravity, not whether the motion is regular or chaotic. In a steady state everything fed in is dissipated, and the generator is where it is dissipated. (One subtlety of random kicks is that the result can depend on whether the kick is evaluated at the start or in the middle of each step; that matters only when the kick's strength depends on the velocity it kicks. Here $b$ depends only on the angles, so the two readings agree. The algebra is checked in SymPy for the single and the double pendulum.)

4.2 The effective mass never exceeds the real mass

$m_{\rm eff}$ measures how much of the pendulum's mass the shaking can grab. For a single pendulum $m_{\rm eff} = m\,(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$, where $\hat t$ is the direction the bob can move: the full mass when the bob can move along the shaking, none when the rod points along it. In general, the proof takes three lines. For any velocity $\dot q$ of the chain, the masses move with velocities $\mathbf v_i$, its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}A\dot q = \tfrac12\sum m_i|\mathbf v_i|^2$, and $b\cdot\dot q = \sum m_i\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf v_i$. Then

$$m_{\rm eff} = \max_{\dot q}\frac{(b\cdot\dot q)^2}{\dot q^{\mathsf T}A\dot q} = \max\frac{\left(\sum_i m_i\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf v_i\right)^2}{\sum_i m_i|\mathbf v_i|^2} \le \frac{\left(\sum_i m_i\right)\left(\sum_i m_i(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v_i)^2\right)}{\sum_i m_i|\mathbf v_i|^2} \le M_{\rm total}, \tag{4.3}$$

The first step is itself the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, in the product $u^{\mathsf T}Av$: $(b\cdot\dot q)^2 \le (b^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}b)(\dot q^{\mathsf T}A\dot q)$, with equality at $\dot q = A^{-1}b$. The second is Cauchy–Schwarz again, and the third holds because $(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v_i)^2 \le |\mathbf v_i|^2$. Equality needs every mass to move along $\hat e$, all with the same velocity: the chain must be able to carry all of its mass along the shaking at once. So

$$P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\,M_{\rm total}. \tag{4.4}$$

In plain words: random shaking cannot feed in more than $S/2$ times the pendulum's total mass, whatever the pendulum does, regular or chaotic.

For a double pendulum the equality case happens whenever its top rod is perpendicular to the shaking: turning the top rod while the lower rod keeps its direction carries both bobs along together. On a turning blade, a pendulum at rest relative to the blade lies along the blade, and the blade's edgewise shaking (in the rotor plane, across the blade) is perpendicular to it; so a single and a double pendulum start with the same full effective mass. Any wandering, chaotic or not, can only lower it.

Check
A computer experiment: a single pendulum (0.1 kg on 0.2 m) and a double pendulum of the same mass and reach hang in gravity, braked with a damping ratio of 0.2 (a brake at a fifth of the strength that would just stop a free swing from overshooting), and their pivot is shaken sideways with white noise of strength $S = 0.028$ m²/s³ (gentle: swings of 0.05 rad) or $S = 48$ m²/s³ (violent: swings of more than a radian, the double's top rod tumbling right over about a hundred times in each 40-second window). For each case 1000 independent runs compare the generator's power with $\tfrac S2\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$: they agree within {{n:mc_dev_min}} to {{n:mc_dev_max}} %, at statistical errors of {{n:mc_se_min}} to {{n:mc_se_max}} %. In the violent runs $\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$ falls to {{n:meff_single_large}} of the total mass for the single and {{n:meff_double_chaos}} for the tumbling double.

On the blade the pendulum is also driven by gravity's once-per-turn push, paid for by the rotor. The noise's own feed is still (4.2), whatever else drives the motion, because its first-order term averages to zero in any state. But the noise also changes the motion, and with it what gravity's push feeds in, which can rise or fall; (4.4) bounds the noise's own feed, not that indirect change. These notes give no value of $S$ for a real blade, because a real blade does not shake as white noise. A real blade, though, does not shake as white noise: it shakes mostly near its own edgewise frequency, about 1.08 Hz on the 5 MW reference turbine of chapter 5 (1.0793 Hz in Jonkman et al. 2009, Table 9-1). Then what matters is how the pendulum responds near that one frequency, and the answer differs from white noise: white noise sees only $m_{\rm eff}$, so a single and a double pendulum take the same, but shaking at one slow frequency acts like a push, as gravity's does, and the double's mass closer to the pivot counts against it again. Chapter 6 works it out.

5 Gusts and the rotor's speed

So far the rotor turned at a steady speed. In gusty wind it does not: a gust speeds it up over some seconds, a lull slows it down. To see what that does to the pendulum we need a real turbine and a real wind.

The turbine is the NREL 5 MW reference turbine, a published design of a utility turbine with a 126 m rotor (Jonkman, Butterfield, Musial and Scott 2009, NREL/TP-500-38060), widely used as a common test case. Its rotor and generator together have a moment of inertia $J = 4.38\times10^7$ kg m², and the rotor's speed obeys $J\dot\Omega = Q_{\rm aero} - Q_{\rm gen}(\Omega)$: the wind's torque, $Q_{\rm aero} = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm tip}^3v^2C_p(\lambda)/\lambda$ with the tip-speed ratio $\lambda = \Omega r_{\rm tip}/v$ and the power coefficient $C_p(\lambda)$ from NREL's published table, against the generator's torque, which the controller holds proportional to $\Omega^2$ in normal operation. At a steady 8 m/s the two torques balance at 9.07 rpm. The wind is the standard model of gusty wind used to design turbines (IEC 61400-1, edition 3, turbulence class A, as implemented in NREL's TurbSim): at 8 m/s its speed fluctuates with a standard deviation of 1.86 m/s, with the Kaimal spectrum, mostly over tens of seconds. The rotor feels the average over its disc: two points of the disc a distance apart fluctuate together only at low frequencies (the IEC coherence), so the disc average is smoother than the wind at the hub. In {{n:l2_records}} ten-minute records the rotor's speed wanders about its mean with a standard deviation of {{n:sigOm}} % of the mean.

A metronome on a train that slowly speeds up and slows down keeps its own beat, so a push timed to the train's wheels would drift in and out of step with it. Our pendulum's beat, $\sqrt R$ per turn, and gravity's push, once per turn, both ride on the rotor's speed, so the two stay in step whatever the rotor does.

The gusts reach the pendulum in two ways. They make the rotor speed up and slow down, which adds a small force (the Euler terms of (1.3)); and they move the rotor's speed away from 9.07 rpm, which changes the power through (2.5). The first is negligible: it changes the power by about {{n:l2_euler_parts}} parts in 10 000. The second is a small, systematic gain. With the generator constant fixed, the power at speed $\Omega$ is $\bar P(\Omega)/\bar P(\Omega_0) = 2/(1 + \Omega^2/\Omega_0^2)$, from (2.3); this curve is convex near $\Omega_0$ (it curves upwards: its second derivative in $\Omega/\Omega_0$ is 1 there), so a speed that wanders about a fixed mean, with a relative standard deviation $s$, raises the average power by about $s^2/2 = {{n:jensen}}$ %, whichever way it wanders.

On our blade
With a pure square-law controller, the pendulum at 30, 60 and 90 % of the 63 m rotor radius gains {{n:l2_k2_tot_min}} to {{n:l2_k2_tot_max}} % over steady operation at 8 m/s: the wandering speed accounts for +{{n:l2_k2_wnd_min}} to +{{n:l2_k2_wnd_max}} %, as the curvature predicts, and the mean speed barely moves ({{n:l2_k2_msh_min}} to +{{n:l2_k2_msh_max}} %). With the turbine's full controller the result changes sign, to {{n:l2_nrel_tot_min}} %: at 8 m/s the full controller works just above a kink in its torque law, at 8.98 rpm. Below the kink the generator's torque falls away steeply, so in a lull the rotor is braked less and slows less than it speeds up in a gust; the average speed rises by {{n:l2_nrel_speedup}} %, and (2.5) falls as $1/\Omega$. That is a property of this turbine's controller, not of the wind. Either way, gusts change the pendulum's power by a few per cent at most.

6 The blade bends and shakes

A real blade is not rigid. It bends under its own weight, once per turn, and it shakes in gusty wind near its own edgewise frequency. Both move the pivot.

6.1 The sag: a large push that cancels itself

Hold a long, springy ruler out sideways and turn your wrist slowly: the ruler sags first one way and then the other, following gravity. A blade 61.5 m long does the same as it turns, in its own plane.

From the NREL blade's mass and stiffness tables, gravity bends it edgewise by {{n:sag_tip_cm}} cm at the tip (depending on how the bend's shape is taken), once per turn and in step with gravity's push. A pivot on the blade therefore moves sideways by $w\cos\varphi$, with $w$ from {{n:sag_w_30}} mm at 30 % of the blade to {{n:sag_w_90}} mm at 90 %. Put a moving pivot into the Lagrangian of chapter 1 and linearise (done in SymPy): the pivot's motion adds $2\Omega^2w$ to gravity's push $g$, once from the pivot's own acceleration and once from the shift of the centrifugal pull as the pivot moves sideways. On its own that would raise the power by {{n:sag_only_30}} %, {{n:sag_only_60}} % and {{n:sag_only_90}} % at the three pivots (the power goes as the square of the push, (2.5)).

But a bending blade also turns: its cross-section rotates with the slope of the bend, and the generator's stator, fixed in the blade, rotates with it. The generator brakes the rod's motion relative to the stator, $-c(\dot\theta - \dot\beta)$, where $\beta$ is the section's angle; so the turning stator drags on the rod with the extra torque $c\dot\beta$. That drag follows the rate of change of the bend's slope, a quarter turn out of step with the sag's push, while at the best brake the swing lags its push by 45° (section 2.3); so part of the drag works against the swing. The numbers say how much. The two effects almost cancel. What is left is a small difference of two large numbers, so it depends on the exact shape of the bend: taking the shape from the blade's first mode of vibration, or integrating the static bend directly from the same blade tables, gives a net change of {{n:sag_rng_30}} % at 30 %, {{n:sag_rng_60}} % at 60 % and {{n:sag_rng_90}} % at 90 %. (For one shape, linear theory and a full nonlinear run with the moving, turning pivot agree within 0.2 percentage points.) The lesson: a generator brakes motion relative to its own frame, so when that frame moves, the frame's motion counts too.

6.2 Random shaking

Gusts make the blade's first edgewise mode vibrate near 1.08 Hz. How strongly, no source we could open says for this blade, so the notes take a range: a tip vibration of 5, 20 or 100 mm rms. The model has two parts, coupled both ways. The blade's first edgewise mode is one oscillator: a modal mass of {{n:sh_mb}} kg on a spring tuned to 1.08 Hz, with a damping ratio $\zeta_b$ of 0.5 % or 3 % of critical, driven by a random force whose spectrum is flat near 1.08 Hz and scaled so that the blade alone would vibrate at the stated tip level. At the pivot, at 90 % of the rotor radius, the blade moves sideways by {{n:sh_phi}} times the tip's motion and its section turns by {{n:sh_dphi}} mrad per metre of tip motion. The pendulum is (2.1) with its pivot shaken sideways and its stator turned by that much, and it pushes back on the blade's mode with the force and torque it needs. Gravity's push is left out of this part (it is in chapter 2 and section 6.1). Everything is linear, so the response is computed frequency by frequency, and the generator's mean power is the brake times the mean square relative speed. The full linear equations, with the turning frame's centrifugal and Coriolis terms, were derived in SymPy. Since the model is linear, the swing from shaking and the swing from gravity add; but the brake that takes most from shaking is not the one that takes most from gravity (for the 20 cm pendulum {{n:sh_c_one}} N m s against {{n:sh_cstar_one}} N m s), so the two best powers below cannot simply be added. The model checks that the pendulum never takes more than the gusts feed into the blade.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration the gusts feed {{n:sh_in_5}} W into the blade's vibration, and the 20 cm pendulum, at its best brake for the shaking, takes {{n:sh_one_5}} mW of it, against {{n:grav_one90}} mW from gravity. At 20 mm it takes {{n:sh_one_20}} mW; at 100 mm, the top of the range, {{n:sh_one_100}} mW. The double pendulum of the same mass and reach takes {{n:sh_ratio}} times as much, for the same reason as in chapter 3: its mass sits closer to the pivot. Chaos plays no part: at $R$ near 280 the double pendulum is regular.

The 20 cm pendulum takes so little because its own frequency, $\sqrt R$ times the rotor's, is 2.4 times the blade's: it is far from the blade's resonance. A pendulum tuned to the blade, with its own frequency equal to 1.08 Hz at the mean rotor speed, needs a rod of {{n:tu_L}} m, {{n:tu_len_ratio}} times longer. It is then a tuned mass damper, the device that calms swaying skyscrapers, and it draws on the blade's vibration directly.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration and a blade damping of 0.5 % the tuned rod takes {{n:tu_P_lo}} mW, {{n:tu_share_lo}} % of what the gusts feed into the blade, and calms the blade's tip motion by {{n:tu_tipcut_lo}} %; at 3 % blade damping it takes {{n:tu_P_hi}} mW. Its swing is {{n:tu_q_lo}} rad rms, at the edge of what a linear model can describe for a device whose value lies in a narrow resonance. Three catches. First, the rotor's speed wanders by {{n:sigOm}} %, and the tuned rod's frequency wanders with it while the blade's does not; averaged over that wander the gain falls to {{n:tu_Pw_lo}} mW. Second, at 20 mm of tip vibration the linear model would ask the rod to swing {{n:tu_q20_lo}} rad rms, more than a metre each way inside a blade some 2.3 m wide: there the model, and the device, stop working. Third, and worst, this model leaves gravity out of the rod's stiffness. Gravity's term $-G\theta\sin\varphi$ (section 2.1) stiffens and softens the rod once per turn, by $\pm G/2R = \pm{{n:tu_mod_pct}}$ % of its frequency at 90 % of the blade, whatever its length, while the blade's resonance is only 1 to 6 % wide (twice the damping ratio). So the tuned rod is detuned and retuned every turn, and its true gain may be somewhat below these numbers or several times below them. The tuned rod is an idea these notes have not shown to work; only a calculation in time, with gravity kept whole, can say how much of it survives. For comparison, the longer rod also takes {{n:grav_tuned90}} mW from gravity alone, simply because it is longer.
