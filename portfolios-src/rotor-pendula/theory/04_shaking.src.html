Part IIRandomness: shaking, gusts and a bending blade

4 A ceiling on random shaking

So far the second blade was driven by one clean push, gravity once per turn. But wind is gusty, and a blade in gusty wind shakes. Physicists have proposed that oscillators which move irregularly, even chaotically, catch more of a random shaking than a calm one does (Cottone, Vocca and Gammaitoni 2009). Could our second blade, perhaps tumbling chaotically, turn the wind's randomness into extra power? Before any model of the wind, one exact result sets a ceiling. To keep it clean, this chapter takes a body, or a chain of bodies, hinged to a point that is shaken at random while it hangs in gravity, with no rotor; the last section says what carries over to the turning blade.

4.1 Random kicks add energy through their square

Stand in a bus on a rough road with a full cup of coffee. Every jolt kicks the coffee, sometimes with its slosh and sometimes against it. On average the "with" and the "against" cancel, and yet the coffee sloshes harder the rougher the road. A kick adds kinetic energy even when it is equally likely to help or oppose the motion, because kinetic energy goes as the square of the velocity.

Start with the simplest case: a small mass $m$ that can move only along one direction $\hat t$, across its rod. Shake its pivot to and fro along a direction $\hat e$. In the pivot's frame each jolt is a sudden extra force on the mass, which changes its velocity along $\hat t$ by a small random amount $\Delta v$. Its kinetic energy $\tfrac12mv^2$ changes by $mv\,\Delta v + \tfrac12m\,\Delta v^2$. The first term is as often negative as positive and averages to zero. The second is never negative. Call the shaking's strength $S$, in m²/s³: the spread (the variance) of the velocity kicks it gives per second. The kicks along $\hat t$ then have the variance $S(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$ per second, and the shaking feeds in, on average, the power $\tfrac12Sm(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$. That is the whole idea. The next paragraphs prove it for any body or chain of bodies, with Lagrange's equations; a reader who skips them loses only the proof, and can go on to the result (4.2) and its plain-words form after (4.4).

Shake the pivot along a fixed direction $\hat e$ with the acceleration $\ddot u(t)$. In the pivot's frame every piece of mass $dm$ feels the extra force $-\ddot u(t)\,\hat e\,dm$. For a body or chain with the angles $q_k$, that force enters Lagrange's equations as the generalised force

$$Q_k = -\ddot u(t)\,b_k(q), \qquad b_k = \int \hat e\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf x}{\partial q_k}\,dm = \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k}\left(M\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf x_{\rm cm}\right), \tag{4.1}$$

the rate at which turning the angle $q_k$ moves the whole mass $M$ along the shaking, through its centre of mass $\mathbf x_{\rm cm}$. Let the shaking be white noise of strength $S$: kicks so quick and so unrelated that $\langle\ddot u(t)\ddot u(t')\rangle = S\,\delta(t - t')$, where $\delta(\cdot)$ is the Dirac delta function (not the lag $\delta$ of chapter 2); it says that the shaking at two different instants is unrelated, however close the instants are. Over a short time $dt$ the momenta $p_k = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_k$ receive the random kick $\Delta p = -b\,\sqrt{S}\,dW$, where $dW$ is a random number of mean zero and variance $dt$. Why $\sqrt{dt}$? Within $dt$ there are many independent tiny kicks, and, like the steps of a random walk, their sum grows as the square root of their number, so as $\sqrt{dt}$, and its square is of order $dt$. That is why the square of the kick, which would be negligible for a smooth force, survives here. The kinetic energy is $K = \tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\dot q = \tfrac12p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}p$, with $\mathsf M(q)$ the mass matrix (for one rigid body on its pivot, simply its moment of inertia). To second order in the kick,

$$\Delta K = \dot q\cdot\Delta p + \tfrac12\,\Delta p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}\Delta p.$$

The first term averages to zero: the kick is as likely to help as to oppose. The second does not: its average is $\tfrac12S\,b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b\,dt$. So on average the shaking feeds in the power

$$\boxed{P_{\rm in} = \frac S2\,\big\langle m_{\rm eff}\big\rangle, \qquad m_{\rm eff} = b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b.} \tag{4.2}$$

Nothing else entered: not the stiffness, not the damping, not gravity, not whether the motion is regular or chaotic. In a steady state everything fed in is dissipated, and the generator is where it is dissipated. (Random kicks have one subtlety, known as the Itô–Stratonovich question: the result can depend on whether a kick's strength is evaluated at the start or in the middle of each step. That matters only when the kick's strength depends on the velocity it kicks; here $b$ depends only on the angles, so the two readings agree.)

4.2 The effective mass never exceeds the real mass

$m_{\rm eff}$ measures how much of the body's mass the shaking can grab. For one body on its pivot it is $b^2/I_p$. For a small bob it is $m(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$: the full mass when the bob can move along the shaking, none when its rod points along it. For our uniform second blade, $b = \tfrac{ml}{2}\cos\psi$, where $\psi$ is the angle between the shaking and the direction in which the blade's pieces move, and

$$m_{\rm eff} = \frac{\left(\tfrac{ml}{2}\right)^2\cos^2\psi}{ml^2/3} = \tfrac34\,m\cos^2\psi \le \tfrac34\,m.$$

The shaking can grab at most three quarters of a uniform rod's mass: the pieces near the hinge hardly move, so they take their share of the kinetic energy without catching their share of the kicks. The general proof takes three lines. For any velocities $\dot q$, the pieces move with velocities $\mathbf v$, the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot q = \tfrac12\int|\mathbf v|^2dm$, and $b\cdot\dot q = \int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm$. Then

$$m_{\rm eff} = \max_{\dot q}\frac{(b\cdot\dot q)^2}{\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot q} = \max\frac{\left(\int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm\right)^2}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le \frac{\left(\int dm\right)\left(\int(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v)^2dm\right)}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le M_{\rm total}. \tag{4.3}$$

The first step is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the product $u^{\mathsf T}\mathsf Mv$: $(b\cdot\dot q)^2 \le (b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b)(\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot q)$, with equality at $\dot q = \mathsf M^{-1}b$. The second is Cauchy–Schwarz again, and the third holds because $(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v)^2 \le |\mathbf v|^2$. Equality would need every piece to move along $\hat e$ with the same velocity, carrying all the mass along the shaking at once, which a body turning on a hinge never can. So

$$P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\,M_{\rm total}, \qquad\text{and for the second blade}\quad P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\cdot\frac34\,m. \tag{4.4}$$

In plain words: random shaking cannot feed in more than $S/2$ times the body's mass, whatever the body does, regular or chaotic, and a rod on a hinge takes at most three quarters of that.

How hard would it have to shake?
For the {{n:mB}} kg second blade to take as much from white-noise shaking as from gravity at 8 m/s, {{n:Pst_8}} mW, (4.4) needs $S$ = {{n:S_match}} m²/s³ at least. Spread evenly over every frequency from 0 to 10 Hz, that is a shaking of {{n:arms_match}} m/s² rms (the root of the mean square), about a third of gravity, all the time. The blade's own shaking is nothing like that, as chapter 6 shows.
Check
A computer test of the general theorem on two simpler bodies: a bob of 0.1 kg on 0.2 m, and a double pendulum of the same mass and reach, hang in gravity, braked with a damping ratio of 0.2 (a fifth of the brake that just stops ringing), while their pivot is shaken sideways with white noise of strength $S = 0.028$ m²/s³ (gentle: swings of 0.05 rad) or $S = 48$ m²/s³ (violent: swings of more than a radian, the double pendulum's top rod tumbling right over about a hundred times in each 40-second window). For each case 1000 independent runs compare the generator's power with $\tfrac S2\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$: they agree within {{n:mc_dev_min}} to {{n:mc_dev_max}} %, at statistical errors of {{n:mc_se_min}} to {{n:mc_se_max}} %. In the violent runs $\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$ falls to {{n:meff_single_large}} of the total mass for the bob and {{n:meff_double_chaos}} for the tumbling double pendulum: chaos took less, not more. The rod's value $\tfrac34m\cos^2\psi$ is checked in SymPy.

On the turning blade, gravity's once-per-turn push, paid for by the rotor, also drives the second blade. The noise's own feed is still (4.2), whatever else drives the motion, because its first-order term averages to zero in any state. But the noise also changes the motion, and with it what gravity's push feeds in; (4.4) bounds the noise's own feed, not that indirect change. A blade bends two ways. Flapwise it bends out of the rotor's plane, along the wind; that moves the hinge along its own axle and cannot turn the second blade at all. Edgewise it bends in the rotor's plane, across the blade; that moves the hinge across a second blade lying along the mother blade, so the second blade starts with its full effective mass, $\tfrac34m$, and any wandering can only lower it. A real blade, though, does not shake as white noise: it shakes mostly near its own edgewise frequency, about 1.08 Hz for the 5 MW turbine's blade (1.0793 Hz in Jonkman et al. 2009, Table 9-1). Then what matters is how the second blade responds near that one frequency, and shaking at one frequency acts like a push, as gravity's does. Chapter 6 works it out.

What to remember
  • A random kick adds energy through its square, even when it is as likely to help as to oppose.
  • White-noise shaking of strength $S$ feeds in $\tfrac S2$ times an effective mass, never more than the real mass, $\tfrac34m$ for a rod on a hinge, whatever the motion: chaos cannot beat the ceiling.
  • To match gravity's harvest, the blade tip would have to shake far harder than a blade does.

5 Gusts and the rotor's speed

So far the rotor turned at a steady speed. In gusty wind it does not: a gust speeds it up over some seconds, a lull slows it down. This chapter asks what that does to the second blade. It needs three things: how the rotor's speed answers the wind, a realistic gusty wind, and how the second blade's power depends on the rotor's speed.

A heavy flywheel turned by a hand that pushes unevenly turns almost evenly: the flywheel smooths the pushes. The turbine's rotor is such a flywheel. At 8 m/s it carries, as kinetic energy, about {{n:store_s}} s of its own output, so a gust lasting a second barely changes its speed; only changes that last tens of seconds do.

How the rotor answers. The 5 MW turbine's rotor and main generator together have a moment of inertia $J_{\rm rot} = 4.38\times10^7$ kg m², and the rotor's speed obeys Newton's law for turning, $J_{\rm rot}\dot\Omega = Q_{\rm aero} - Q_{\rm gen}(\Omega)$: the wind's torque against the main generator's. The wind's torque is its power over the angular speed, $Q_{\rm aero} = P/\Omega = \tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^2v^3C_p(\lambda)/\Omega$, and with $\Omega = \lambda v/r_{\rm rot}$ this is $\tfrac12\rho\pi r_{\rm rot}^3v^2C_p(\lambda)/\lambda$, with $C_p(\lambda)$ from NREL's published table. The main generator's torque is set by the control computer from the rotor's speed alone, by a rule called the torque law. Its main part is a square law, $Q_{\rm gen} = k\,\Omega^2$. Why a square? At the best tip-speed ratio the wind's torque is proportional to $v^2$, and so to $\Omega^2$; a square law with the right $k$ balances it at $\lambda^*$ in every wind, so the rotor settles at its best speed by itself. Below 8.98 rpm the law has a kink: there the torque ramps down in a straight line to zero at 6.9 rpm, so that the turbine starts and stops gently. At a steady 8 m/s the two torques balance at {{n:rpm_ctrl_8}} rpm, {{n:ctrl_vs_lam}} % below the $\lambda^*v/r_{\rm rot}$ of section 0.3, because that ramp holds the rotor a little off its best ratio.

A gusty wind. Turbines are designed against a standard description of gusty wind, set out in the international standard IEC 61400-1. Its strongest class of turbulence, class A, makes the wind's speed at 8 m/s fluctuate with a standard deviation of 1.86 m/s, 23 % of the mean. How that fluctuation spreads over slow and fast changes is given by the Kaimal spectrum, a formula fitted to measured winds: most of it lies in changes lasting tens of seconds to minutes, and less and less in quicker wiggles. Two points of the rotor's disc a few tens of metres apart wander together only in the slow changes, so the wind averaged over the disc, which is what turns the rotor, is smoother than the wind at any one point. NREL's program TurbSim generates such winds; these notes use {{n:l2_records}} generated ten-minute records at a mean of 8 m/s, not measurements. In them the rotor's speed wanders about its mean with a standard deviation of {{n:sigOm}} % of the mean. Only 8 m/s was simulated.

What the second blade feels. The gusts reach the second blade in two ways. While the rotor speeds up or slows down, the Euler torque of (1.5) acts; it changes the power by about {{n:l2_euler_parts}} parts in 10 000, nothing. More important, the rotor's speed itself changes. The second blade settles to a new speed in about a second (section 3.1), while the rotor's speed changes over tens of seconds, so at every moment the second blade swings as it would at that moment's speed in a steady wind. With the hinge generator's constant $c$ fixed, (2.3) at the speed $\Omega$, divided by its value at the speed $\Omega_0$ for which $c$ is the best brake, is

$$\frac{\bar P(\Omega)}{\bar P(\Omega_0)} = \frac{2}{1 + \Omega^2/\Omega_0^2}.$$

(In rotor turns, $R$ stays the same at every speed, but $\kappa = c/(I\Omega)$ and $G$ change with $\Omega$; this formula carries both.) Its slope at $\Omega_0$ is $-1$: a shift of the mean speed by a fraction $\epsilon$ changes the power by $-\epsilon$. And it curves upwards there (its second derivative in $\Omega/\Omega_0$ is $+1$), so a speed that wanders about a fixed mean raises the average power, whichever way it wanders: by about $s^2/2$ for a relative spread $s$, {{n:jensen}} % here.

On our blade
The full equation of the second blade was run through each of the {{n:l2_records}} records, with the rotor's speed taken from the turbine's own equation, and compared with steady rotation. Two main-generator rules were tried. With a pure square law ($Q_{\rm gen} \propto \Omega^2$, no kink) the second blade's power changes by {{n:l2_k2_tot}} % against steady operation at 8 m/s: the wandering speed adds {{n:l2_k2_wnd}} %, a little more than the {{n:jensen}} % the curvature alone predicts (we have not traced the rest), and the mean speed moves by {{n:l2_k2_dOm}} % and changes the power by {{n:l2_k2_msh}} %, as the slope of $-1$ predicts. With the turbine's real torque law, kink included, the total turns to {{n:l2_nrel_tot}} %. At 8 m/s the rotor runs just above the kink. In a lull it drops onto the ramp, where the main generator brakes much less, so it slows less than it speeds up in a gust; its mean speed rises by {{n:l2_nrel_dOm}} %, which changes the power by {{n:l2_nrel_msh}} %, again as the slope predicts, while the wander still adds {{n:l2_nrel_wnd}} %. That is a property of this turbine's control, not of the wind. Either way, gusts change the second blade's power by a few per cent.
What to remember
  • The rotor is a flywheel: gusts make its speed wander slowly, by about a tenth.
  • The second blade follows the rotor's speed from moment to moment. A wander about a fixed mean adds a little; a shift of the mean speed by a fraction $\epsilon$ costs $\epsilon$ of the power.
  • Net effect at 8 m/s: a few per cent, its sign set by the main generator's torque law.

6 The blade bends and shakes

A real blade is not rigid. It bends under its own weight, once per turn, and it shakes in gusty wind near its own edgewise frequency. Both move the hinge, and at the tip they move it most.

6.1 The sag: a large push that cancels itself

Hold a long, springy ruler out sideways and turn your wrist slowly: the ruler sags first one way and then the other, following gravity, and its end sags most. A blade 61.5 m long does the same as it turns, in its own plane.

The blade's mass and stiffness, metre by metre, are published (the OpenFAST blade file of the 5 MW turbine). From them, gravity bends the blade edgewise so that its tip moves to and fro by $w$ = {{n:sag_lo_mm}} to {{n:sag_hi_mm}} mm either side of its straight position, once per turn and in step with gravity's push; the range comes from two ways of taking the shape of the bend, from the blade's first mode of vibration or by integrating the bending directly. The hinge, at the tip, therefore moves across the mother blade by $w\cos\varphi$.

That motion adds to gravity's push in two ways, each worth $\Omega^2 w$ per kilogram. First, seen from the turning mother blade, the hinge accelerates to and fro by $-\Omega^2w\cos\varphi$, which in the hinge's frame is an apparent push of $+\Omega^2w\cos\varphi$, in step with gravity's. Second, a hinge moved sideways by $w\cos\varphi$ sits at a slightly different angle seen from the hub, $w\cos\varphi/r_p$, so the outward pull, which points away from the hub, turns by that angle, and its part across the mother blade grows by $\Omega^2r_p\cdot w\cos\varphi/r_p = \Omega^2w\cos\varphi$. Together, gravity's push $g$ becomes $g + 2\Omega^2w$. (Linearising the Lagrangian with a moving hinge, in SymPy, gives the same.) On its own that would raise the power, which goes as the push squared (2.5), by {{n:sag_only_lo}} to {{n:sag_only_hi}} % at 8 m/s.

But a bending blade also turns: each cross-section tilts with the slope of the bend, and at the tip the slope is largest. The generator's stator, fixed in the mother blade, tilts with it by an angle $\beta$. The generator brakes the second blade's motion relative to the stator, $-c(\dot\theta - \dot\beta)$, so the tilting stator drags the second blade with the extra torque $c\dot\beta$. That drag follows the rate of change of the slope, a quarter turn out of step with the sag's push, while at the best brake the swing lags its push by 45° (section 2.3); so part of the drag works against the swing. The two effects largely cancel. What is left is a small difference of two large numbers, so it depends on the exact shape of the bend: a net change of {{n:sag_tot_lo}} to {{n:sag_tot_hi}} % for the two shapes. (For the first shape, linear theory and a full nonlinear run with the moving, tilting hinge agree within {{n:sag_full_gap}} percentage points.) The lesson: a generator brakes motion relative to its own frame, so when that frame moves, the frame's motion counts too.

6.2 Random shaking

Gusts make the blade's first edgewise mode vibrate near 1.08 Hz. How far the tip of this blade vibrates edgewise in ordinary operation, we found no published value. So the notes take a range of tip vibrations, 5, 20 and 100 mm rms, and say what each would give; the answer rests on that unknown.

The model has two parts, coupled both ways. The blade's first edgewise mode is one oscillator: a modal mass, the mass that, sitting at the tip on a spring, would vibrate like the whole blade in that mode ({{n:sh_mb}} kg, computed from the blade's mass table and the mode's shape), on a spring tuned to 1.08 Hz, with a damping ratio $\zeta_b$ of 0.5 % or 3 % of critical, two assumed values. It is driven by a random force whose spectrum is flat near 1.08 Hz, scaled so that the blade alone would vibrate at the stated tip level. At the tip the hinge moves sideways with the tip, and the section tilts by {{n:sh_dphi}} mrad per metre of tip motion. The second blade is (2.1) with its hinge shaken sideways and its stator tilted by that much, and it pushes back on the blade's mode with the force and torque it needs. Gravity's push is left out of this part (it is in chapter 2 and section 6.1). Everything is linear, so the response is computed frequency by frequency, and the generator's mean power is the brake times the mean square of the relative speed. (The full linear equations, with the second blade integrated along its length and the turning frame's centrifugal and Coriolis terms, were derived in SymPy.) The model checks that the second blade never takes more than the gusts feed into the blade.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration the gusts feed {{n:sh_in_5}} W into the blade's vibration, and the {{n:lB}} m second blade, at its best brake for the shaking, {{n:sh_c_one}} N m s, takes {{n:sh_one_5}} mW of it. At 20 mm it takes {{n:sh_one_20}} mW; at 100 mm, {{n:sh_one_100}} mW, more than gravity gives. For comparison, gravity gives {{n:grav_one}} mW at its own best brake, {{n:sh_cstar_one}} N m s. Since the model is linear, the swings from shaking and from gravity add; but the two best brakes differ by a factor of fifteen, so the two best powers cannot simply be added. At the shaking's best brake the second blade's damping ratio is only {{n:zeta_sh}}: here, unlike under gravity's push, it really does ring. The motion is regular (section 3.4).

The {{n:lB}} m second blade takes so little at small vibrations because its own frequency, $\sqrt R$ times the rotor's, is {{n:freq_ratio}} times the blade's: it is far from the blade's resonance. A second blade tuned to the blade, its own frequency $\Omega\sqrt{3r_p/(2l)}$ equal to the blade's $\omega_e = 2\pi\times1.08$ Hz at the mean rotor speed, needs a length of {{n:tu_L}} m; with the same mass per metre it weighs {{n:tu_m}} kg. It would then be a tuned mass damper, the device that calms swaying skyscrapers, and it would draw on the blade's vibration directly: at 5 mm and 0.5 % blade damping, the linear model gives it {{n:tu_P_lo}} mW. But gravity's term $-G\theta\sin\varphi$ (section 2.1) stiffens and softens it once per turn, shifting its frequency by $\pm1/(2\Gamma) = \pm${{n:tu_mod_pct}} % at 8 m/s, while the blade's resonance is only 1 to 6 % wide; the rotor's wandering speed detunes it too. So it is detuned and retuned every turn, and whether any of that gain survives is an open question that only a calculation in time, with gravity kept whole, can answer.

What to remember
  • The blade's sag moves and tilts the hinge once per turn. The two effects largely cancel: a few per cent net.
  • A generator brakes motion relative to its own frame; when the frame moves, that counts.
  • The blade's random vibration adds power as the square of the tip's vibration: negligible at 5 mm, more than gravity at 100 mm. The real level for this blade is not known, so this stays open.
