Part IIRandomness: shaking, gusts and a bending blade

4 A ceiling on random shaking

Wind is gusty, and a blade in gusty wind shakes. Could a pendulum, perhaps a chaotic one, turn that randomness into extra power? Before any model of the wind, one exact result sets a limit. To keep it clean, this chapter treats a pendulum (or a chain of pendula) whose pivot is shaken at random while it hangs in gravity, with no rotor; the last paragraph says what carries over to the blade.

4.1 Random kicks add energy through their square

Stand on a bus over a rough road with a cup of coffee. Every jolt kicks the coffee, sometimes with its slosh and sometimes against it. On average the "with" and "against" cancel, yet the coffee still sloshes harder the rougher the road: a kick adds kinetic energy even when it is equally likely to help or oppose the motion, because energy goes as the square of velocity.

Shake the pivot along a fixed direction $\hat e$ with acceleration $a(t)$. In the pivot's frame each mass $m_i$ feels an extra force $-m_i a(t)\hat e$. For a pendulum (or a chain of pendula) with angles $q_k$, that force enters Lagrange's equations as a generalised force

$$Q_k = -a(t)\,b_k(q), \qquad b_k = \sum_i m_i\,\hat e\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf x_i}{\partial q_k}. \tag{4.1}$$

Let the shaking be white noise of strength $S$: kicks so quick and so uncorrelated that $\langle a(t)a(t')\rangle = S\,\delta(t - t')$. Over a short time $dt$ the momenta $p_k$ conjugate to the angles receive a random kick $\Delta p = -b\,\sqrt{S}\,dW$, where $dW$ is a random number of mean zero and variance $dt$. Why $\sqrt{dt}$? Within $dt$ there are many independent tiny kicks; like the steps of a random walk, their sum grows as the square root of their number, so as $\sqrt{dt}$, and its square is of order $dt$. That is why the square of the kick, which would be negligible for a smooth force, survives here. The kinetic energy is $K = \tfrac12 p^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}p$, with $M(q)$ the mass matrix of the chain. Expand to second order in the kick:

$$\Delta K = \dot q\cdot\Delta p + \tfrac12\,\Delta p^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}\Delta p.$$

The first term averages to zero: the kick is as likely to help as to oppose. The second does not: its average is $\tfrac12 S\,b^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}b\,dt$. So on average the shaking feeds in the power

$$\boxed{P_{\rm in} = \frac S2\,\big\langle m_{\rm eff}\big\rangle, \qquad m_{\rm eff} = b^{\mathsf T}M^{-1}b.} \tag{4.2}$$

Nothing else entered: not the stiffness, not the damping, not gravity, not whether the motion is regular or chaotic. In a steady state everything fed in is dissipated, and the generator is where it is dissipated. (One subtlety of random kicks is that the result can depend on whether the kick is evaluated at the start or in the middle of each step; that matters only when the kick's strength depends on the velocity it kicks. Here $b$ depends only on the angles, so the two readings agree. The algebra is checked in SymPy for the single and the double pendulum.)

4.2 The effective mass never exceeds the real mass

$m_{\rm eff}$ measures how much of the pendulum's mass the shaking can grab. For a single pendulum $m_{\rm eff} = m\,(\hat e\cdot\hat t)^2$, where $\hat t$ is the direction the bob can move: the full mass when the bob can move along the shaking, none when the rod points along it. In general, the proof takes three lines. For any velocity $\dot q$ of the chain, the masses move with velocities $\mathbf v_i$, its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}M\dot q = \tfrac12\sum m_i|\mathbf v_i|^2$, and $b\cdot\dot q = \sum m_i\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf v_i$. Then

$$m_{\rm eff} = \max_{\dot q}\frac{(b\cdot\dot q)^2}{\dot q^{\mathsf T}M\dot q} = \max\frac{\left(\sum_i m_i\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf v_i\right)^2}{\sum_i m_i|\mathbf v_i|^2} \le \frac{\left(\sum_i m_i\right)\left(\sum_i m_i(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v_i)^2\right)}{\sum_i m_i|\mathbf v_i|^2} \le M_{\rm total}, \tag{4.3}$$

by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Equality needs every mass to move with the same velocity along $\hat e$: the chain must be able to carry all of its mass along the shaking at once. So

$$P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\,M_{\rm total}. \tag{4.4}$$

For a double pendulum the equality case happens whenever its top rod is perpendicular to the shaking: turning the top rod while the lower rod keeps its direction carries both bobs along together. On a turning blade, a pendulum at rest relative to the blade lies along the blade, and the blade's edgewise shaking (in the rotor plane, across the blade) is perpendicular to it; so a single and a double pendulum start with the same full effective mass. Any wandering, chaotic or not, can only lower it.

Check
A computer experiment: a single pendulum (0.1 kg on 0.2 m) and a double pendulum of the same mass and reach hang in gravity, braked with a damping ratio of 0.2, and their pivot is shaken sideways with white noise of strength $S = 0.028$ m²/s³ (gentle: swings of 0.05 rad) or $S = 48$ m²/s³ (violent: swings of more than a radian, the double's top rod tumbling right over about a hundred times in each 40-second window). For each case 1000 independent runs compare the generator's power with $\tfrac S2\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$: they agree within {{n:mc_dev_min}} to {{n:mc_dev_max}} %, at statistical errors of 0.4 to 0.7 %. In the violent runs $\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$ falls to {{n:meff_single_large}} of the total mass for the single and {{n:meff_double_chaos}} for the tumbling double.

On the blade the pendulum is also driven by gravity's once-per-turn push, paid for by the rotor. The two inputs simply add: the noise's contribution to the average energy is (4.2) whatever else drives the motion, because its first-order term averages to zero in any state. So the ceiling (4.4) bounds the extra power from white-noise shaking, and chaos can only fall below it. A real blade, though, does not shake as white noise: it shakes mostly near its own edgewise frequency, about 1.08 Hz on the 5 MW reference turbine of chapter 5 (1.0793 Hz in Jonkman et al. 2009, Table 9-1). Then what matters is how the pendulum responds near that one frequency; chapter 6 works it out.

5 Gusts and the rotor's speed

So far the rotor turned at a steady speed. In gusty wind it does not: a gust speeds it up over some seconds, a lull slows it down. To see what that does to the pendulum we need a real turbine and a real wind.

The turbine is the NREL 5 MW reference turbine, a published design of a utility turbine with a 126 m rotor (Jonkman, Butterfield, Musial and Scott 2009, NREL/TP-500-38060), widely used as a common test case. Its rotor and generator together have a moment of inertia of $4.38\times10^7$ kg m², its controller holds the generator's torque proportional to the square of the speed in normal operation, and its power coefficient curve comes from NREL's own published table. At a steady 8 m/s it turns at 9.07 rpm. The wind is the standard model of gusty wind used to design turbines (IEC 61400-1, turbulence class A): at 8 m/s its speed fluctuates with a standard deviation of 1.86 m/s, mostly over tens of seconds, and the rotor feels the average over its disc. In {{n:l2_records}} ten-minute records the rotor's speed wanders by {{n:sigOm}} % about its mean.

A metronome on a train that slowly speeds up and slows down keeps ticking at its own rate, unaffected. Our pendulum is different and better: its own frequency, $\sqrt R$ per turn, and gravity's push, once per turn, both ride on the rotor's speed, so it stays in the same relation to its push whatever the rotor does.

The gusts reach the pendulum in two ways. They make the rotor speed up and slow down, which adds a small force (the Euler terms of (1.3)); and they move the rotor's speed away from 9.07 rpm, which changes the power through (2.5). The first is negligible: it changes the power by about 2 parts in 10 000. The second is a small, systematic gain. With the generator constant fixed, the power at speed $\Omega$ is $\bar P(\Omega)/\bar P(\Omega_0) = 2/(1 + \Omega^2/\Omega_0^2)$, from (2.3); this curve bends upwards near $\Omega_0$, so a speed that wanders by $\pm s$ raises the average by about $s^2/2 = {{n:jensen}}$ %, whichever way it wanders.

On our blade
With a pure square-law controller, the pendulum at 30, 60 and 90 % of the 63 m blade gains {{n:l2_k2_tot_min}} to {{n:l2_k2_tot_max}} % over steady operation at 8 m/s: the wandering speed accounts for +{{n:l2_k2_wnd_min}} to +{{n:l2_k2_wnd_max}} %, as the curvature predicts, and the mean speed barely moves ({{n:l2_k2_msh_min}} to +{{n:l2_k2_msh_max}} %). With the turbine's full controller the result changes sign, to {{n:l2_nrel_tot_min}} %: at 8 m/s the full controller works just above a kink in its torque law, so gusts push the average speed up by 2.5 %, and (2.5) falls as $1/\Omega$. That is a property of this turbine's controller, not of the wind. Either way, gusts change the pendulum's power by a few per cent at most.

6 The blade bends and shakes

A real blade is not rigid. It bends under its own weight, once per turn, and it shakes in gusty wind near its own edgewise frequency. Both move the pivot.

6.1 The sag: a large push that cancels itself

Hold a long, springy ruler out sideways and turn your wrist slowly: the ruler sags first one way and then the other, following gravity. A blade 61.5 m long does the same as it turns, in its own plane.

From the NREL blade's mass and stiffness tables, gravity bends it edgewise by about {{n:sag_tip_cm}} cm at the tip, once per turn and in step with gravity's push. A pivot on the blade therefore moves sideways by $w\cos\varphi$, with $w$ from {{n:sag_w_30}} mm at 30 % of the blade to {{n:sag_w_90}} mm at 90 %. Put a moving pivot into the Lagrangian of chapter 1 and linearise (done in SymPy): the pivot's motion adds $2\Omega^2w$ to gravity's push $g$, once from the pivot's own acceleration and once from the shift of the centrifugal pull as the pivot moves sideways. On its own that would raise the power by {{n:sag_only_30}} %, {{n:sag_only_60}} % and {{n:sag_only_90}} % at the three pivots.

But a bending blade also turns: its cross-section rotates with the slope of the bend, and the generator's stator, fixed in the blade, rotates with it. The generator brakes the rod's motion relative to the stator, so the rotation enters through the brake, a quarter turn out of step with gravity's push. The two effects almost cancel: the net change is {{n:sag_tot_30}} %, {{n:sag_tot_60}} % and {{n:sag_tot_90}} %. (Linear theory and a full nonlinear run with the moving, turning pivot agree within 0.2 percentage points.) A large effect that cancels itself is a lesson in where the generator's frame is.

6.2 Random shaking

Gusts make the blade's first edgewise mode vibrate near 1.08 Hz. How strongly, nobody has published for this blade, so the notes take a range: a tip vibration of 5, 20 or 100 mm rms. The model couples the blade's mode and the pendulum both ways, so that the pendulum cannot take more power than the gusts feed into the blade (checked in every case), and treats the pivot at 90 % of the blade, where the blade moves most.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration the gusts feed {{n:sh_in_5}} W into the blade's vibration, and Sol's pendulum, at its best brake for the shaking, takes {{n:sh_sol_5}} mW of it, against {{n:grav_sol90}} mW from gravity. At 20 mm it takes {{n:sh_sol_20}} mW; at 100 mm, the top of the range, {{n:sh_sol_100}} mW. The double pendulum of the same mass and reach takes {{n:sh_ratio}} times as much, for the same reason as in chapter 3: its mass sits closer to the pivot. Chaos plays no part: at $R$ near 280 the double pendulum is regular.

Sol's pendulum takes so little because its own frequency, $\sqrt R$ times the rotor's, is 2.4 times the blade's: it is far from the blade's resonance. A pendulum tuned to the blade, with its own frequency equal to 1.08 Hz at the mean rotor speed, needs a rod of {{n:tu_L}} m, {{n:tu_len_ratio}} times longer. It is then a tuned mass damper, the device that calms swaying skyscrapers, and it draws on the blade's vibration directly.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration and a blade damping of 0.5 % the tuned rod takes {{n:tu_P_lo}} mW, {{n:tu_share_lo}} % of what the gusts feed into the blade, and calms the blade's tip motion by {{n:tu_tipcut_lo}} %; at 3 % blade damping it takes {{n:tu_P_hi}} mW. Its swing is {{n:tu_q_lo}} rad rms, small enough for the linear model. Two catches. First, the rotor's speed wanders by {{n:sigOm}} %, and the tuned rod's frequency wanders with it while the blade's does not; averaged over that wander the gain falls to {{n:tu_Pw_lo}} mW. Second, at 20 mm of tip vibration the linear model would ask the rod to swing {{n:tu_q20_lo}} rad rms, more than a metre each way inside a blade some 2.3 m wide: there the model, and the device, stop working. For comparison, the longer rod also takes {{n:grav_tuned90}} mW from gravity alone, simply because it is longer.

7 What is still to come

One piece is still missing: from swing to watts. A real generator has a coil resistance, a gearbox and its own inertia, and at the best brake it must resist {{n:torque_star}} N m at a slow swing of a few radians per second, which only a geared generator can do. That chapter will turn the mechanical power of these notes into the electrical power the sensor actually receives, and check the sensor's budget again with real losses.
