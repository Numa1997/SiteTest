8 A blade that bends and shakes

One simplification of Part I remains: a rigid mother blade. A real blade sags under its own weight, once per turn, and shakes in gusty wind near its own frequency, and both move the hinge. This chapter adds the two motions, then proves a ceiling for any random shaking: the double pendulum's chaos makes its last appearance there.

8.1 The sag: a large push that cancels itself

Hold a long, springy ruler out sideways and turn your wrist slowly: the ruler sags first one way and then the other, following gravity, and its end sags most. A blade 61.5 m long does the same as it turns, in its own plane.

From the blade's published mass and stiffness, metre by metre (the OpenFAST blade file), gravity bends the blade in the rotor's plane so that its tip moves to and fro with the amplitude $w$ = {{n:sag_lo_mm}} to {{n:sag_hi_mm}} mm, once per turn, in step with gravity's push. (It follows gravity as a slow, static sag: once per turn is about a seventh of the blade's own frequency, 1.08 Hz, and a spring pushed well below its own frequency moves $1/(1 - f^2/f_e^2)$ times its static amount, here about two per cent more.) (The range comes from two ways of taking the bend's shape: the blade's first mode of vibration, or the bend integrated from the stiffness tables.) The hinge moves across the mother blade by $w\cos\varphi$, counted, like every term in the display below, in the direction of gravity's push (backwards, against the rotor's turning, when $\varphi = 0$). That adds to gravity's push twice, per kilogram:

$$\underbrace{g\cos\varphi}_{\text{gravity}} + \underbrace{\Omega^2w\cos\varphi}_{\text{the hinge accelerating, as in the car}} + \underbrace{\Omega^2r_p\cdot\frac{w\cos\varphi}{r_p}}_{\text{the outward pull turned by the hinge's shift}} = (g + 2\Omega^2w)\cos\varphi.$$

(The hinge's acceleration in the turning frame is $\frac{d^2}{dt^2}w\cos\Omega t = -\Omega^2w\cos\varphi$, felt as $+\Omega^2w\cos\varphi$; seen from the hub the shifted hinge sits at the angle $w\cos\varphi/r_p$, and the outward pull turns with it. The Lagrangian of section 8.2, with the moving hinge, gives the same two terms: there they are $-\tfrac{ml}{2}(\ddot y - \Omega^2y)$, and for $y = w\cos\varphi$ they add $\tfrac{ml}{2}\cdot2\Omega^2w\cos\varphi$.) The power goes as the push squared, so alone this would raise it by $(1 + 2\Omega^2w/g')^2 - 1$, about $4\Omega^2w/g'$ for a small sag, with $g' = g\cos5°$ the part of gravity in the rotor's plane: {{n:sag_only_lo}} to {{n:sag_only_hi}} % at 8 m/s (from the full square).

But a bending blade also turns. Each section tilts with the bend's slope; at the tip the slope is $w' = dw/dr$, the rate at which the displacement grows along the blade, so the tip section tilts by $w'\cos\varphi$ once per turn, the same way it sags (for the first of the two shapes, the blade's mode, the tilt is {{n:sh_dphi}} mrad per metre of tip motion, section 8.2) (Figure {{fig:sag}}). The generator's stator is fixed to that section and tilts with it, and so does the line from which $\theta$ is measured. That matters, because the generator brakes only motion relative to its stator.

Count angles positive in the direction the rotor turns. With the mother blade level and rising ($\varphi = 0$), gravity pushes the second blade back, $-G\cos\varphi < 0$, and sags the tip back by $w$; the tip section tilts back with it. So the tilt is $\beta = -w'\cos\varphi$, with $w$ and $w'$ the sizes of the sag and of the slope. Let $\chi = \theta + \beta$ be the second blade's angle from the unbent mother blade's line. The pulls act on $\chi$; they do not care how the stator sits. The brake acts on $\theta' = \chi' - \beta'$. Measuring the push $-G\cos\varphi$ of (3.1) with gravity's in-plane part $g'$ and collecting the sag's two terms above, the linear equation in turns reads

$$\chi'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\,\chi = -G\Big(1 + \frac{2\Omega^2w}{g'}\Big)\cos\varphi.$$

Put $\chi = \theta + \beta$. The tilt goes once per turn, so $\beta'' = -\beta$, and

$$\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\,\theta = -G\Big(1 + \frac{2\Omega^2w}{g'}\Big)\cos\varphi - (R - 1)\,\beta.$$

The tilt is a push of its own: $-(R-1)\beta = +(R-1)w'\cos\varphi$, against gravity's and the sag's. The stator turns back and forth under a second blade that the outward pull holds nearly still, and the net stiffness turns that relative swing into a push. The swing answers the total push exactly as in chapter 3, at any brake, so the power scales with its square:

$$\frac{\bar P}{\bar P_0} = \Big[\Big(1 + \frac{2\Omega^2w}{g'}\Big) - \frac{(R-1)\,w'}{G}\Big]^2:$$

two terms of nearly equal size that largely cancel, so the result depends on the bend's exact shape: a net {{n:sag_tot_lo}} to {{n:sag_tot_hi}} % for the two shapes (the formula reproduces the computed totals; for the first shape, linear theory and a full nonlinear run agree within {{n:sag_full_gap}} percentage points). A generator works in its own frame: when that frame moves and turns, its motion counts too.

8.2 The blade's own vibration

Gusts make the blade's first mode in the rotor's plane, the edgewise mode, ring near 1.08 Hz (1.0793 Hz, Jonkman et al. 2009, Table 9-1). How far this blade's tip vibrates that way in ordinary operation, we found no published value; so these notes take tip vibrations of 5, 20 and 100 mm rms (root mean square) and say what each would give. (Flapwise bending, along the wind, moves the hinge along its axle and cannot turn the second blade.)

The model has two parts coupled both ways. The edgewise mode is one oscillator: a modal mass ({{n:sh_mb}} kg, the mass that, at the tip on a spring, would vibrate like the whole blade in that mode) on a spring tuned to 1.08 Hz, damped at 0.5 % or 3 % of critical (assumed), driven by a random force flat near 1.08 Hz, scaled to the stated tip level. The hinge moves with the tip, and the section tilts by {{n:sh_dphi}} mrad per metre of tip motion. The second blade is (3.1) with its hinge shaken and its stator tilted, pushing back on the mode with the force and torque it needs; gravity's push is left to chapter 3 and section 8.1.

Written out: let $y$ be the tip's edgewise displacement, $\chi$ the second blade's angle from the unbent mother blade's line (as in section 8.1), $\beta = \Phi'y$ the tilt of the tip section ($\Phi'$ the mode's slope at the tip, {{n:sh_dphi}} mrad per metre) and $F(t)$ the gusts' force on the mode. The mode alone is a mass $m_b$ on a spring $k_b$ (the subscript $b$ for the blade's mode, to keep them apart from the generator's constants), with the damping $c_b = 2\zeta_b\sqrt{k_bm_b}$. In the turning frame the hinge sits at $r_p\hat{\mathbf e} + y\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf e}$ and a piece of the second blade $s$ beyond it; its velocity seen from the ground is the turning-frame velocity plus $\Omega\hat{\mathbf z}\times$ its position (section 2.2). Square, integrate along the rod as in (1.2), and keep every term of second order in the small quantities $y$, $\chi$ and their rates:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal L_2 = {}& \tfrac12(m_b + m)\,\dot y^2 + \tfrac12I\dot\chi^2 + \tfrac{ml}{2}\,\dot y\dot\chi - \tfrac12(k_b - m\Omega^2)\,y^2 - \tfrac{ml}{4}\,r_p\Omega^2\chi^2 + \tfrac{ml}{2}\,\Omega^2y\chi\\ &+ \Omega\Big[\Big(\tfrac{ml}{2} + mr_p\Big)\dot y + \Big(I + \tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Big)\dot\chi\Big]. \end{aligned}$$

The last line, the only place a single rate meets $\Omega$, has constant coefficients: it is the time derivative of something and drops out of Lagrange's equations. That is why no Coriolis term survives at this order. Lagrange's equations, with the generator's torque $-c(\dot\chi - \dot\beta)$ on the second blade and its reaction on the stator, which turns through $\beta = \Phi'y$ and so enters the mode with the factor $\Phi'$, give

$$\begin{aligned} (m_b + m)\,\ddot y + c_b\,\dot y + (k_b - m\Omega^2)\,y &= F(t) - \frac{ml}{2}\big(\ddot\chi - \Omega^2\chi\big) + \Phi'\,c\,(\dot\chi - \dot\beta),\\ I\,\ddot\chi + \frac{ml}{2}\,\Omega^2r_p\,\chi &= -\frac{ml}{2}\big(\ddot y - \Omega^2y\big) - c\,(\dot\chi - \dot\beta). \end{aligned}$$

Every term has a picture. In the second line, the left side is the centrifugal spring of chapter 1; on the right, $-\tfrac{ml}{2}\ddot y$ is the pendulum in the accelerating car of section 2.1, $+\tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2y$ the outward pull turned by the hinge's shift, the same two terms that made the sag's push in section 8.1, and $-c(\dot\chi - \dot\beta)$ the generator, braking motion relative to its tilting stator. The first line is the blade's mode carrying the second blade's mass at its tip (the $-m\Omega^2y$ is the outward pull on that mass, turned as the tip moves sideways) and feeling the second blade's reaction: the force of its swing at the hinge, and the generator's torque on the stator. Everything is linear, so each frequency can be treated alone. Put $y = \hat y\,e^{i\omega t}$, $\chi = \hat\chi\,e^{i\omega t}$, $F = \hat F e^{i\omega t}$; each time derivative becomes a factor $i\omega$, and the two equations become two linear equations for the two amplitudes:

$$\begin{pmatrix} k_b - m\Omega^2 - (m_b + m)\,\omega^2 + i\omega\,(c_b + \Phi'^2c) & -\tfrac{ml}{2}(\omega^2 + \Omega^2) - i\omega\,\Phi'c \\ -\tfrac{ml}{2}(\omega^2 + \Omega^2) - i\omega\,\Phi'c & \tfrac{ml}{2}\Omega^2r_p - I\omega^2 + i\omega\,c \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat y\\ \hat\chi\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\hat F\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The matrix is symmetric, as it must be for a system derived from one Lagrangian with one shared brake. The diagonal holds each part alone: the mode's spring, mass and damping, and the second blade's centrifugal spring, inertia and brake; the off-diagonal entries couple them, through the hinge's motion and the generator's torque on the stator. Solving by Cramer's rule gives $\hat y$ and $\hat\chi$ per unit force, and with them $Y(\omega) = i\omega(\hat\chi - \Phi'\hat y)/\hat F$, the relative speed the generator sees per unit force. With and $S_F(\omega)$ the force's spectrum, one-sided in $\omega$ (so that $\int_0^\infty S_F\,d\omega$ is the force's variance) and flat near the mode, its height set so that the blade without a second blade vibrates at the stated tip level, the generator takes

$$\bar P = c\int_0^\infty S_F(\omega)\,|Y(\omega)|^2\,d\omega,$$

the brake times the mean square relative speed, summed over the band. (The study's script L3_shake2.py derives the same equations in SymPy and evaluates the integral.)

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration the gusts feed {{n:sh_in_5}} W into the blade's vibration, and the {{n:lB}} m second blade, at its best brake for the shaking, {{n:sh_c_one}} N m s, takes {{n:sh_one_5}} mW of it. At 20 mm it takes {{n:sh_one_20}} mW; at 100 mm, {{n:sh_one_100}} mW, more than gravity gives. This model runs at the rotor speed of section 6.5, {{n:rpm_O0}} rpm, with the part of gravity in the rotor's plane, $g\cos 5°$ (the turbine's shaft is tilted up by 5°), so gravity's own best brake here is {{n:sh_cstar_one}} N m s and its best power {{n:grav_one}} mW. One generator has one brake. Since the model is linear and the two pushes are unrelated, the powers from shaking and from gravity add at any one brake. At the shaking's best brake, {{n:sh_x}} of gravity's, gravity gives only $2x/(1+x^2)$ of its best, {{n:grav_at_sh}} mW, so at 100 mm the second blade takes {{n:sh_tot_100}} mW in all. At the shaking's best brake the second blade's damping ratio is only {{n:zeta_sh}}: here, unlike under gravity's push, it really does ring.

It takes so little at small vibrations because its own frequency, $\sqrt R$ times the rotor's, is {{n:freq_ratio}} times the blade's: far from the blade's resonance.

A tuned mass damper
feel
Some tall towers hang a great weight on a spring near the top; when the tower sways, the weight swings the other way and the sway dies.
physics
A small oscillator tuned to a structure's frequency, and braked, draws energy out of the structure's vibration, far more than an untuned one.
measure
Its own frequency must sit within the structure's resonance width, twice the damping ratio times the frequency: here 1 to 6 %.
maths
Tuned when $\Omega\sqrt{3r_p/(2l)} = 2\pi f_{\rm blade}$, which fixes the length $l$.

Tuned at the mean rotor speed, the second blade would be {{n:tu_L}} m long, {{n:tu_m}} kg at the same mass per metre, and at 5 mm and 0.5 % blade damping the linear model gives it {{n:tu_P_lo}} mW. But gravity's term $-G\theta\sin\varphi$ (section 3.1) shifts its frequency by $\pm1/(2\Gamma) = \pm${{n:tu_mod_pct}} % once per turn at 8 m/s, against a resonance 1 to 6 % wide, and the rotor's wandering speed detunes it too. Whether any of that gain survives being detuned and retuned every turn is open; only a calculation in time with gravity kept whole can say.

8.3 A ceiling for random shaking, and chaos once more

Physicists have proposed that oscillators which move irregularly, even chaotically, catch more of a random shaking than calm ones (Cottone, Vocca and Gammaitoni 2009). If that were so, chaos might turn the wind's randomness into extra power. One exact result settles it, for shaking with no preferred rhythm.

White noise
feel
The hiss of a radio between stations: every pitch at once, equally loud, with nothing to recognise.
physics
Shaking with no memory: what it does at one instant tells nothing about the next, however close. Its spectrum is flat, the same power at every frequency.
measure
Record the shaking's acceleration and take its spectrum: flat at the height $S$, over the frequencies that matter. (This $S$ counts negative frequencies too; a one-sided spectrum, as in chapter 6, reads $2S$.)
maths
$\langle\ddot u(t)\,\ddot u(t')\rangle = S\,\delta(t - t')$. Over a time $dt$ it gives a velocity kick of mean zero and variance $S\,dt$, which grows like a random walk, as $\sqrt{dt}$.
Stand in a bus on a rough road with a full cup of coffee. Every jolt kicks the coffee, sometimes with its slosh and sometimes against it. On average the "with" and the "against" cancel, and yet the coffee sloshes harder the rougher the road. A kick adds kinetic energy even when it is as likely to help as to oppose the motion, because kinetic energy goes as the square of the velocity.

Take any body or chain of bodies hinged to a point shaken along $\hat e$ with the acceleration $\ddot u(t)$. In the pivot's frame every piece $dm$ feels $-\ddot u\,\hat e\,dm$ (section 2.1), which enters Lagrange's equations for the angles $q_k$ as

$$Q_k = -\ddot u(t)\,b_k(q), \qquad b_k = \int \hat e\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf x}{\partial q_k}\,dm = \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k}\left(M\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf x_{\rm cm}\right), \tag{8.1}$$

the rate at which turning $q_k$ moves the whole mass along the shaking. Over $dt$, white noise kicks the momenta $p_k = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_k$ by $\Delta p = -b\sqrt S\,dW$, $dW$ of mean zero and variance $dt$; its square is of order $dt$, so it survives. With $K = \tfrac12p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}p$ ($\mathsf M(q)$ the mass matrix), to second order

$$\Delta K = \dot q\cdot\Delta p + \tfrac12\,\Delta p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}\Delta p.$$

The first term averages to zero, the kick as likely to help as to oppose; the second averages $\tfrac12S\,b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b\,dt$. So the shaking feeds in, on average, the power below. In a steady state that power has only one place to go: the conservative forces (gravity, the outward pull, the springs) return over time what they take, so the brake takes what the shaking feeds in, and $P_{\rm in}$ is also the generator's power.

$$\boxed{P_{\rm in} = \frac S2\,\big\langle m_{\rm eff}\big\rangle, \qquad m_{\rm eff} = b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b.} \tag{8.2}$$

Nothing else entered: not the stiffness, the damping, gravity, or whether the motion is regular or chaotic. (Stochastic calculus asks whether a kick's strength is taken at the start or at the middle of a time step: the Itô and Stratonovich conventions. The choice matters only when the strength depends on the very quantity the kick changes. Here the kicks change the momenta, while $b$ depends on the angles alone, which move smoothly, by $\dot q\,dt$, during a step; so both conventions give the same answer.)

The effective mass
feel
Hold a stick loosely by one end between two fingers, so that it can pivot, and shake your hand sideways: its far end swings, its near end barely moves, and the shaking grips the stick far less than a stone held firmly in the hand.
physics
The part of a body's mass that a shaking along $\hat e$ can push on, given how the body is free to move.
measure
Shake it with known white noise, measure the mean power fed in: $m_{\rm eff} = 2P_{\rm in}/S$.
maths
$m_{\rm eff} = b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b$; for a uniform rod on a hinge, $\tfrac34m\cos^2\psi$.

It can never exceed the real mass. For any rates $\dot q$ the pieces move with velocities $\mathbf v$, the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\int|\mathbf v|^2dm$, and $b\cdot\dot q = \int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm$ (by (8.1), $b_k\dot q_k$ is the part of each piece's velocity along $\hat e$ that the rate $\dot q_k$ makes, summed over the mass). The first step below uses the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the inner product $\langle x, y\rangle = x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf My$ that the kinetic energy defines: $b\cdot\dot q = \langle\mathsf M^{-1}b, \dot q\rangle$, so $(b\cdot\dot q)^2 \le (b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b)(\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot q)$, with equality at $\dot q = \mathsf M^{-1}b$. The second step is the ordinary Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for integrals over the mass, and the last uses $(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v)^2 \le |\mathbf v|^2$:

$$m_{\rm eff} = \max_{\dot q}\frac{(b\cdot\dot q)^2}{\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot q} = \max\frac{\left(\int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm\right)^2}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le \frac{\left(\int dm\right)\left(\int(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v)^2dm\right)}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le M_{\rm total}. \tag{8.3}$$

Equality needs every piece moving along $\hat e$ at the same velocity. A rod on a hinge never can: its pieces near the hinge barely move. Point masses on massless rods can, when they hang straight across the shaking, all swinging along $\hat e$ together; that is why the gentle runs below come close to the full mass. For our rod, $b = \tfrac{ml}{2}\cos\psi$ ($\psi$ between the shaking and the direction its pieces move) and $m_{\rm eff} = (\tfrac{ml}{2})^2\cos^2\psi/(ml^2/3) = \tfrac34m\cos^2\psi$: the pieces near the hinge hardly move, so they take their share of the kinetic energy without catching their share of the kicks. So

$$P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\,M_{\rm total}, \qquad\text{and for the second blade}\quad P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\cdot\frac34\,m. \tag{8.4}$$

White noise cannot feed more than $S/2$ times the body's mass, regular or chaotic. (On the turning blade the noise also changes the motion and with it gravity's feed; (8.4) bounds the noise's own feed, not that indirect change.)

Check, and the double pendulum
A computer test of (8.2) on two bodies: a bob of 0.1 kg on 0.2 m, and a double pendulum of the same mass and reach, hanging in gravity, braked with a damping ratio of 0.2, while their pivot is shaken sideways with white noise of strength $S = 0.028$ m²/s³ (gentle: swings of 0.05 rad) or $S = 48$ m²/s³ (violent: swings of more than a radian, the double pendulum's upper rod tumbling right over about a hundred times in each 40-second window). For each case 1000 independent runs compare the generator's power with $\tfrac S2\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$; they agree within {{n:mc_dev_min}} to {{n:mc_dev_max}} %, at statistical errors of {{n:mc_se_min}} to {{n:mc_se_max}} %. In the gentle runs $\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$ is at least {{n:meff_gentle_min}} of the total mass; in the violent runs it falls to {{n:meff_single_large}} for the swinging bob and to {{n:meff_double_chaos}} for the tumbling double pendulum. Large motions, regular or chaotic, took less, not more: a body thrown about spends much of its time with its mass moving across the shaking, where the kicks cannot grab it. The tumbling double pendulum did a little better than the swinging bob ({{n:meff_double_chaos}} against {{n:meff_single_large}} of its mass), and both stayed far below the ceiling.
How hard it would have to shake
For the {{n:mB}} kg second blade to take as much from white noise as from gravity at 8 m/s, {{n:Pst_8}} mW, (8.4) needs $S$ = {{n:S_match}} m²/s³ at least. Spread evenly over every frequency from 0 to 10 Hz (variance $2S$ times 10 Hz), that is a shaking of {{n:arms_match}} m/s² rms, about a third of gravity, all the time. But the blade's real shaking is not white: it rings near one frequency, its own, and there the feed depends on how the second blade answers at that frequency, which section 8.2 computed directly. The ceiling settles the question of chaos for broadband shaking; for the blade's narrow-band vibration, section 8.2 is the answer.
What to remember
  • The blade's sag moves and tilts the hinge once per turn. The two effects largely cancel: a few per cent net. A generator brakes motion relative to its own frame; when the frame moves, that counts.
  • The blade's own vibration adds power as the square of the tip's vibration: negligible at 5 mm, more than gravity at 100 mm. The real level for this blade is not known, so this stays open.
  • White-noise shaking of strength $S$ feeds in $\tfrac S2$ times an effective mass, never more than the real mass, $\tfrac34m$ for a rod on a hinge, whatever the motion: chaos cannot beat the ceiling, and in the test it fell short of it.
