8 A blade that bends and shakes

One more simplification of Part I remains: the mother blade was rigid. A real blade bends under its own weight, once per turn, and it shakes in gusty wind near its own frequency. Both move the hinge, and at the tip they move it most. This chapter adds the two motions, and then proves a ceiling that holds for any random shaking whatever: the place where the double pendulum's chaos makes its last appearance.

8.1 The sag: a large push that cancels itself

Hold a long, springy ruler out sideways and turn your wrist slowly: the ruler sags first one way and then the other, following gravity, and its end sags most. A blade 61.5 m long does the same as it turns, in its own plane.

The blade's mass and stiffness, metre by metre, are published (the OpenFAST blade file of the 5 MW turbine). From them, gravity bends the blade within the rotor's plane so that its tip moves to and fro by $w$ = {{n:sag_lo_mm}} to {{n:sag_hi_mm}} mm either side of its straight position, once per turn and in step with gravity's push; the range comes from two ways of taking the shape of the bend, from the blade's first mode of vibration or by integrating the bending directly. The hinge therefore moves across the mother blade by $w\cos\varphi$.

That motion adds to gravity's push in two ways, each worth $\Omega^2 w$ per kilogram. Seen from the turning mother blade, the hinge accelerates to and fro by $-\Omega^2w\cos\varphi$, which in the hinge's frame is an apparent push of $+\Omega^2w\cos\varphi$, in step with gravity's: the car of section 2.1 once more. And a hinge moved sideways by $w\cos\varphi$ sits at a slightly different angle seen from the hub, $w\cos\varphi/r_p$, so the outward pull, which points away from the hub, turns by that angle, and its part across the mother blade grows by $\Omega^2r_p\cdot w\cos\varphi/r_p = \Omega^2w\cos\varphi$. Together, gravity's push $g$ becomes $g + 2\Omega^2w$. (Linearising the Lagrangian with a moving hinge, in SymPy, gives the same.) On its own that would raise the power, which goes as the push squared (3.5), by {{n:sag_only_lo}} to {{n:sag_only_hi}} % at 8 m/s.

But a bending blade also turns: each cross-section tilts with the slope of the bend, and at the tip the slope is largest. The generator's stator, fixed in the mother blade, tilts with it by an angle $\beta$. The generator brakes the second blade's motion relative to the stator, $-c(\dot\theta - \dot\beta)$, so the tilting stator drags the second blade with the extra torque $c\dot\beta$. That drag follows the rate of change of the slope, a quarter turn out of step with the sag's push, while at the best brake the swing lags its push by 45° (section 3.3); so part of the drag works against the swing. The two effects largely cancel. What is left is a small difference of two large numbers, so it depends on the exact shape of the bend: a net change of {{n:sag_tot_lo}} to {{n:sag_tot_hi}} % for the two shapes. (For the first shape, linear theory and a full nonlinear run with the moving, tilting hinge agree within {{n:sag_full_gap}} percentage points.) The lesson: a generator brakes motion relative to its own frame, so when that frame moves, the frame's motion counts too.

8.2 The blade's own vibration

Gusts make the blade's first mode of vibration within the rotor's plane, its edgewise mode, ring near 1.08 Hz (1.0793 Hz in Jonkman et al. 2009, Table 9-1). How far the tip of this blade vibrates that way in ordinary operation, we found no published value. So these notes take a range of tip vibrations, 5, 20 and 100 mm rms (root mean square), and say what each would give; the answer rests on that unknown. (The blade also bends out of the rotor's plane, flapwise, along the wind; that moves the hinge along its own axle and cannot turn the second blade.)

The model has two parts, coupled both ways. The blade's edgewise mode is one oscillator: a modal mass, the mass that, sitting at the tip on a spring, would vibrate like the whole blade in that mode ({{n:sh_mb}} kg, from the blade's mass table and the mode's shape), on a spring tuned to 1.08 Hz, with a damping ratio of 0.5 % or 3 % of critical, two assumed values. It is driven by a random force whose spectrum is flat near 1.08 Hz, scaled so that the blade alone would vibrate at the stated tip level. At the tip the hinge moves sideways with the tip, and the section tilts by {{n:sh_dphi}} mrad per metre of tip motion. The second blade is (3.1) with its hinge shaken sideways and its stator tilted by that much, and it pushes back on the blade's mode with the force and torque it needs. Gravity's push is left out of this part (it is in chapter 3 and section 8.1). Everything is linear, so the response is computed frequency by frequency, and the generator's mean power is the brake times the mean square of the relative speed. (The full linear equations, with the second blade integrated along its length and the turning frame's centrifugal and Coriolis terms, were derived in SymPy.) The model checks that the second blade never takes more than the gusts feed into the blade.

On our blade
At a 5 mm tip vibration the gusts feed {{n:sh_in_5}} W into the blade's vibration, and the {{n:lB}} m second blade, at its best brake for the shaking, {{n:sh_c_one}} N m s, takes {{n:sh_one_5}} mW of it. At 20 mm it takes {{n:sh_one_20}} mW; at 100 mm, {{n:sh_one_100}} mW, more than gravity gives. This model runs at the rotor speed of section 6.5, {{n:rpm_O0}} rpm, with the part of gravity in the rotor's plane, $g\cos 5°$ (the turbine's shaft is tilted up by 5°), so gravity's own best brake here is {{n:sh_cstar_one}} N m s and its best power {{n:grav_one}} mW. One generator has one brake. Since the model is linear and the two pushes are unrelated, the powers from shaking and from gravity add at any one brake. At the shaking's best brake, {{n:sh_x}} of gravity's, gravity gives only $2x/(1+x^2)$ of its best, {{n:grav_at_sh}} mW, so at 100 mm the second blade takes {{n:sh_tot_100}} mW in all. At the shaking's best brake the second blade's damping ratio is only {{n:zeta_sh}}: here, unlike under gravity's push, it really does ring.

The {{n:lB}} m second blade takes so little at small vibrations because its own frequency, $\sqrt R$ times the rotor's, is {{n:freq_ratio}} times the blade's: far from the blade's resonance. A second blade tuned to the blade, its own frequency $\Omega\sqrt{3r_p/(2l)}$ equal to the blade's at the mean rotor speed, needs a length of {{n:tu_L}} m; with the same mass per metre it weighs {{n:tu_m}} kg. It would then be a tuned mass damper, the device that calms swaying skyscrapers, and it would draw on the blade's vibration directly: at 5 mm and 0.5 % blade damping, the linear model gives it {{n:tu_P_lo}} mW. But gravity's term $-G\theta\sin\varphi$ (section 3.1) stiffens and softens it once per turn, shifting its frequency by $\pm1/(2\Gamma) = \pm${{n:tu_mod_pct}} % at 8 m/s, while the blade's resonance is only 1 to 6 % wide, and the rotor's wandering speed detunes it too. It is detuned and retuned every turn, and whether any of that gain survives is an open question that only a calculation in time, with gravity kept whole, can answer.

8.3 A ceiling for random shaking, and chaos once more

Physicists have proposed that oscillators which move irregularly, even chaotically, catch more of a random shaking than a calm one does (Cottone, Vocca and Gammaitoni 2009). Our second blade could in principle tumble, as the double pendulum of chapter 1 does when its first arm is free. Could chaos turn the wind's randomness into extra power? One exact result answers it, for the kind of shaking with no preferred rhythm.

Stand in a bus on a rough road with a full cup of coffee. Every jolt kicks the coffee, sometimes with its slosh and sometimes against it. On average the "with" and the "against" cancel, and yet the coffee sloshes harder the rougher the road. A kick adds kinetic energy even when it is as likely to help as to oppose the motion, because kinetic energy goes as the square of the velocity.

Take any body, or chain of bodies, hinged to a point that is shaken along a fixed direction $\hat e$ with the acceleration $\ddot u(t)$. In the pivot's frame every piece of mass $dm$ feels the extra force $-\ddot u(t)\,\hat e\,dm$. For a body with the angles $q_k$, that force enters Lagrange's equations as the generalised force

$$Q_k = -\ddot u(t)\,b_k(q), \qquad b_k = \int \hat e\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf x}{\partial q_k}\,dm = \frac{\partial}{\partial q_k}\left(M\,\hat e\cdot\mathbf x_{\rm cm}\right), \tag{8.1}$$

the rate at which turning the angle $q_k$ moves the whole mass along the shaking. Let the shaking be white noise of strength $S$: kicks so quick and so unrelated that $\langle\ddot u(t)\ddot u(t')\rangle = S\,\delta(t - t')$, the shaking at two different instants unrelated however close they are. Over a short time $dt$ the momenta $p_k = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_k$ receive the random kick $\Delta p = -b\sqrt{S}\,dW$, with $dW$ a random number of mean zero and variance $dt$. (Within $dt$ there are many independent tiny kicks, and like the steps of a random walk their sum grows as the square root of their number, so as $\sqrt{dt}$; its square is of order $dt$, which is why the square of the kick survives here.) With the kinetic energy $K = \tfrac12p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}p$, $\mathsf M(q)$ the mass matrix, to second order in the kick

$$\Delta K = \dot q\cdot\Delta p + \tfrac12\,\Delta p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}\Delta p.$$

The first term averages to zero: the kick is as likely to help as to oppose. The second has the average $\tfrac12S\,b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b\,dt$. So the shaking feeds in, on average, the power

$$\boxed{P_{\rm in} = \frac S2\,\big\langle m_{\rm eff}\big\rangle, \qquad m_{\rm eff} = b^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}b.} \tag{8.2}$$

Nothing else entered: not the stiffness, not the damping, not gravity, not whether the motion is regular or chaotic. (Random kicks have one subtlety, the Itô–Stratonovich question: the result can depend on whether a kick's strength is evaluated at the start or the middle of each step. That matters only when the strength depends on the velocity it kicks; here $b$ depends only on the angles.) The effective mass $m_{\rm eff}$ is how much of the body's mass the shaking can grab, and it can never exceed the real mass. For any rates $\dot q$ the pieces move with velocities $\mathbf v$, the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\int|\mathbf v|^2dm$, and $b\cdot\dot q = \int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm$, so by Cauchy–Schwarz twice

$$m_{\rm eff} = \max_{\dot q}\frac{(b\cdot\dot q)^2}{\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot q} = \max\frac{\left(\int\hat e\cdot\mathbf v\,dm\right)^2}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le \frac{\left(\int dm\right)\left(\int(\hat e\cdot\mathbf v)^2dm\right)}{\int|\mathbf v|^2dm} \le M_{\rm total}. \tag{8.3}$$

Equality would need every piece to move along $\hat e$ with the same velocity, which a body turning on a hinge never can. For our uniform second blade, $b = \tfrac{ml}{2}\cos\psi$, with $\psi$ the angle between the shaking and the direction in which its pieces move, and $m_{\rm eff} = (\tfrac{ml}{2})^2\cos^2\psi/(ml^2/3) = \tfrac34m\cos^2\psi$: the pieces near the hinge hardly move, so they take their share of the kinetic energy without catching their share of the kicks. So

$$P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\,M_{\rm total}, \qquad\text{and for the second blade}\quad P_{\rm in} \le \frac S2\cdot\frac34\,m. \tag{8.4}$$

White-noise shaking cannot feed more than $S/2$ times the body's mass, whatever the body does, regular or chaotic. On the turning blade the noise's own feed is still (8.2), since its first-order term averages to zero in any state, though the noise also changes the motion and with it what gravity's push feeds in; (8.4) bounds the noise's own feed, not that indirect change.

Check, and the double pendulum
A computer test of (8.2) on two bodies: a bob of 0.1 kg on 0.2 m, and a double pendulum of the same mass and reach, hanging in gravity, braked with a damping ratio of 0.2, while their pivot is shaken sideways with white noise of strength $S = 0.028$ m²/s³ (gentle: swings of 0.05 rad) or $S = 48$ m²/s³ (violent: swings of more than a radian, the double pendulum's upper rod tumbling right over about a hundred times in each 40-second window). For each case 1000 independent runs compare the generator's power with $\tfrac S2\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$; they agree within {{n:mc_dev_min}} to {{n:mc_dev_max}} %, at statistical errors of {{n:mc_se_min}} to {{n:mc_se_max}} %. In the gentle runs $\langle m_{\rm eff}\rangle$ is at least {{n:meff_gentle_min}} of the total mass; in the violent runs it falls to {{n:meff_single_large}} for the swinging bob and to {{n:meff_double_chaos}} for the tumbling double pendulum. Large motions, regular or chaotic, took less, not more: a body thrown about spends much of its time with its mass moving across the shaking, where the kicks cannot grab it, and the tumbling double pendulum gained nothing over the swinging bob.
How hard would it have to shake?
For the {{n:mB}} kg second blade to take as much from white noise as from gravity at 8 m/s, {{n:Pst_8}} mW, (8.4) needs $S$ = {{n:S_match}} m²/s³ at least. Spread evenly over every frequency from 0 to 10 Hz, that is a shaking of {{n:arms_match}} m/s² rms, about a third of gravity, all the time. But the blade's real shaking is not white: it rings near one frequency, its own, and there the feed depends on how the second blade answers at that frequency, which section 8.2 computed directly. The ceiling settles the question of chaos for broadband shaking; for the blade's narrow-band vibration, section 8.2 is the answer.
What to remember
  • The blade's sag moves and tilts the hinge once per turn. The two effects largely cancel: a few per cent net. A generator brakes motion relative to its own frame; when the frame moves, that counts.
  • The blade's own vibration adds power as the square of the tip's vibration: negligible at 5 mm, more than gravity at 100 mm. The real level for this blade is not known, so this stays open.
  • White-noise shaking of strength $S$ feeds in $\tfrac S2$ times an effective mass, never more than the real mass, $\tfrac34m$ for a rod on a hinge, whatever the motion: chaos cannot beat the ceiling, and in the test it fell short of it.
