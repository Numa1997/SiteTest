Part IA double pendulum held by the wind

1 A double pendulum whose first arm the wind turns

Hang a short rod from the end of a longer one, hang the longer one from a nail, and let go from high up. The lower rod whips over the top, the upper one jerks back, and the motion never repeats. Start it again from what looks like the same place and within seconds it does something else. The double pendulum is the standard example of chaos: two arms, nothing random in the laws, and no way to predict where the lower arm will be a minute from now.

These notes are about a double pendulum of a stranger kind. Its first arm is the blade of a wind turbine, 63 m long and weighing about eighteen tonnes, and the wind turns it round at a steady rate. Its second arm is a rod of one kilogram and one metre, hinged at the blade's tip, and a small generator in the hinge brakes its swing. The question is practical: how much electricity can the second arm make, and who pays for it? The answer comes out of one change to the double pendulum. Once the first arm is no longer free but held to a steady turning, the chaos goes, a steady swing takes its place, and its power can be written down in closed form. The second arm then gives about a fifth of a watt.

1.1 The machine

A large wind turbine is built to run for twenty years. Engineers would like to know, all that time, how each blade bends and shakes, because a crack or a loose bolt changes how a blade vibrates long before it fails. A small sensor inside the blade can measure that, but it needs electricity, and the far end of a blade is the worst place on the machine to bring it. A cable would have to pass through the turning hub, and a battery 60 m out along a blade cannot be changed without stopping the turbine and sending a technician up a rope.

How much electricity is needed? A minimal sensor reads an accelerometer all the time (the ADXL362 chip draws 1.8 µA at 2.0 V: 3.6 µW), sleeps (a radio chip, the CC2652R, draws 0.94 µA at 3.0 V: 2.8 µW), and once a second wakes for 1 ms of computing (3.39 mA at 3.0 V) and 1 ms of sending by radio (7.0 mA at 3.0 V): 10.2 µJ + 21.0 µJ = 31.2 µJ every second, 31.2 µW. That is about {{n:sensor_uW}} µW in all, from the typical currents in the two datasheets, leaving out the radio's start-up and the losses of a voltage converter. Over twenty years it adds up to {{n:sensor_20y_Wh}} Wh, and one D-size lithium cell holds about 61 Wh (Saft LS 33600: 17 Ah at 3.6 V). So a battery could carry this minimal sensor, if it survives twenty years of cold, heat and shaking inside a blade. The case for harvesting is the other end of the scale: the machine below gives more than a thousand times this, enough for a sensor that measures faster, computes more and sends more, with no battery to fail.

The turbine of these notes is a published design, the NREL 5 MW reference turbine (Jonkman et al. 2009), shown in Figure 1. A tower 90 m tall carries a housing, the nacelle; at its front a hub carries three blades, and hub and blades together are the rotor. Each blade is 61.5 m long and starts 1.5 m from the centre, so its tip runs on a circle of radius $r_{\rm rot} = 63$ m in a vertical plane that faces the wind. The rotor turns a shaft at the angular speed $\Omega$, and a generator in the nacelle, the main generator, turns the shaft's work into electricity. One fact about the rotor is all the first chapters need. Below its full power, at 11.4 m/s, the turbine's control holds the speed of the blade tips at a fixed multiple of the wind's speed $v$, the tip-speed ratio $\lambda^* = \Omega r_{\rm rot}/v = 7.55$, because there the blades take the largest share of the wind's power (chapter 6 explains why). So in a steady wind the rotor turns at

$$\Omega = \frac{\lambda^* v}{r_{\rm rot}}:$$

{{n:rpm_6}}, {{n:rpm_8}} and {{n:rpm_10}} turns a minute (rpm) at the three winds of these notes, 6, 8 and 10 m/s, a moderate to fresh breeze. The tips then move at {{n:vtip_6}}, {{n:vtip_8}} and {{n:vtip_10}} m/s, faster than a car on a motorway. (At 6 m/s the turbine's real control turns the rotor somewhat faster than this rule, at {{n:rpm_real_6}} rpm; section 6.5 shows why and what it costs.)

Now the idea. At the very tip of one blade, put a hinge whose axle points along the rotor's shaft, and hang on it a second, much lighter blade, free to swing in the plane in which the blades turn. Call the big blade the mother blade. A small generator in the hinge brakes the second blade's swing and turns it into electricity for the sensor; from here on, the generator means this small one, and the turbine's own is the main generator. Anything that sticks out at a blade tip meets the air at 45 to 76 m/s and would be built like a small blade, slim and smooth; the mathematics treats it as a thin uniform rod, of mass $m = {{n:mB}}$ kg and length $l = {{n:lB}}$ m. (The length is a choice; the mass is set in chapter 5 by matching the second blade to a real generator.) Figure 2 shows the machine.

Tie a small weight to a short string, tie the string to the end of a stick 1 m long, and whirl the stick in a vertical circle about once a second. The string stays stretched out along the stick: the turning flings the weight outwards, about four times harder than gravity pulls it down, much as the tip of the turbine's blade is flung. But gravity keeps pulling down. On the way up it pulls the weight back, on the way down it pulls it forward, so the string swings to and fro about the stick's line, once per turn. That once-per-turn push is what we harvest.

Seen this way, the machine is a double pendulum. The mother blade is the first arm, pivoted at the hub; the second blade is the second arm, pivoted at the first arm's end. Everything the turbine adds is one change of the rules: the first arm is not free to swing. The rest of this chapter writes down the double pendulum, makes that change, and watches what it does. Until chapter 7 we leave out the air that rushes past the second blade; that chapter puts it back.

1.2 The free double pendulum

Let the first arm be any rigid body turning about a fixed pivot, the hub. Two numbers describe how it moves under gravity: its moment of inertia $J_1$ about the pivot, the sum of every piece's mass times its distance from the pivot squared, and its first moment $M_1d_1$, its mass times the distance from the pivot to its centre of mass. The second arm is a thin uniform rod of mass $m$ and length $l$, hinged at the first arm's end, a distance $r_p$ from the hub. Its moment of inertia about its own hinge is $I = ml^2/3$ and its first moment about the hinge is $ml/2$.

Two angles fix the pair (Figure 3). Put the hub at the origin with $y$ pointing up, and let $\varphi$ be the first arm's angle from the horizontal, so that the hinge sits at $r_p(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$. Let $\theta$ be the second arm's angle measured from the first arm's outward direction, positive in the direction of increasing $\varphi$. The point of the second arm at the distance $s$ from the hinge, $0 \le s \le l$, is then at

$$x = r_p\cos\varphi + s\cos(\varphi+\theta), \qquad y = r_p\sin\varphi + s\sin(\varphi+\theta). \tag{1.1}$$

The kinetic energy is the first arm's, $\tfrac12J_1\dot\varphi^2$, plus the sum of $\tfrac12(m/l)(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2)\,ds$ along the rod. Differentiate (1.1) and square: each piece's speed squared has three parts, the hinge's own motion, $r_p^2\dot\varphi^2$, the piece's motion about the hinge, $s^2(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)^2$, and a cross term, $2r_ps\,\dot\varphi(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\cos\theta$. The integrals $\int_0^l s^k ds$ give $l$, $l^2/2$ and $l^3/3$, so

$$\begin{aligned} T = {}& \tfrac12 J_1\dot\varphi^2 + \tfrac12 I(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)^2 \\ &+ \tfrac12 m\,r_p^2\dot\varphi^2 + \frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\dot\varphi(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\cos\theta. \end{aligned} \tag{1.2}$$

The potential energy is the weight of each arm times the height of its centre of mass:

$$V = M_1 g\,d_1\sin\varphi + mg\left[r_p\sin\varphi + \tfrac l2\sin(\varphi+\theta)\right]. \tag{1.3}$$

The generator enters through Rayleigh's dissipation function $\mathcal F = \tfrac12c\dot\theta^2$: it resists the second arm's motion relative to the first with a torque $-c\dot\theta$ (chapter 2 shows why a generator does exactly this). Lagrange's equations for $\mathcal L = T - V$ follow, one per angle. For $\theta$,

$$\begin{aligned} &I(\ddot\varphi+\ddot\theta) + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\left(\ddot\varphi\cos\theta + \dot\varphi^2\sin\theta\right) \\ &\quad + \frac{ml}{2}\,g\cos(\varphi+\theta) = -c\,\dot\theta, \end{aligned} \tag{1.4}$$

and for $\varphi$, with $\tau$ any torque that something outside the pair applies to the first arm,

$$\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}\Big[(J_1 + I + m r_p^2 + m l r_p\cos\theta)\,\dot\varphi + \Big(I + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\cos\theta\Big)\dot\theta\Big] \\ &\quad + M_1gd_1\cos\varphi + mg\Big[r_p\cos\varphi + \frac l2\cos(\varphi+\theta)\Big] = \tau. \end{aligned} \tag{1.5}$$

(In (1.4) two terms in $\dot\varphi\dot\theta\sin\theta$, one from the time derivative and one from $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\theta$, cancel. The generator does not appear in (1.5): it acts between the arms, so turning both together does no work against it.)

Check
The double pendulum on this site (Fig. 2 of the simulations) has two point masses on massless rods, with angles measured from the downward vertical. Put $J_1 = m_1l_1^2$, $M_1d_1 = m_1l_1$, $r_p = l_1$, and shrink the second arm to a point mass $m_2$ at its end ($I \to m_2l_2^2$, $ml/2 \to m_2l_2$); with $\theta_1 = \varphi + 90°$ and $\theta_2 = \varphi + \theta + 90°$, (1.2) and (1.3) become that page's $T$ and $V$ term for term. A falling second arm, $\varphi$ held still and $c = 0$, gives from (1.4) $I\ddot\theta = -\tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, the textbook rod pendulum, which swings at $\omega^2 = (ml/2)g/I = 3g/(2l)$ about the downward vertical.

Left alone, with $\tau = 0$ and no generator, the pair keeps its energy $T + V$, and the two angles trade it back and forth. That is all the double pendulum is: two equations, coupled through the cross term of (1.2), with four numbers ($\varphi$, $\theta$ and their rates) to fix its state. The four numbers live in a four-dimensional space, energy confines them to a three-dimensional slice of it, and that is room enough for chaos.

1.3 Holding the first arm

Our first arm is not free. The wind pushes the rotor round, the main generator holds it back, and the turbine's control balances the two so that the rotor turns at the steady $\Omega$ of section 1.1. Could the second arm disturb that? Its weight is about 10 N on a lever of 63 m, a torque of at most $mgr_p$ = {{n:tq_lift}} N m to either side of the hub, against the {{n:tq_turb6}} MN m with which the wind turns the shaft at 6 m/s, and the rotor with its main generator has a moment of inertia of $4.4\times10^7$ kg m². The second arm cannot change the rotor's speed. So the first arm's angle is no longer an unknown: it is a given function of time, $\varphi(t) = \Omega t$, and in a gusty wind some other given function (section 6.5). Mechanics calls this a constraint that depends on time. Landau and Lifshitz set the textbook version of it as a problem (Mechanics, §5, problem 3): a pendulum whose point of support moves uniformly round a vertical circle. Our second blade is exactly that pendulum, made of a rod rather than a point, and braked.

Now look at what the constraint does to the two equations, term by term.

Equation (1.4) survives, and becomes the equation of the second blade alone. Each of its terms changes meaning. $I\ddot\theta$ stays: the second blade's own inertia. $I\ddot\varphi$ and $\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\ddot\varphi\cos\theta$ were the first arm's acceleration acting on the second; now $\ddot\varphi$ is given, and they become a driving torque that acts only while the rotor speeds up or slows down. The term $\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\dot\varphi^2\sin\theta$ was a coupling of the two arms through the first arm's speed; with $\dot\varphi = \Omega$ held, it becomes $\tfrac{ml}{2}r_p\Omega^2\sin\theta$, a torque that pulls the second blade back to the first arm's line, as a spring would. Gravity's term, $\tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, now contains the clock $\varphi = \Omega t$: a push that comes round once per turn. And the first arm's own properties, $J_1$ and $M_1d_1$, are nowhere in (1.4). They never were; in the free pendulum they acted on the second arm only through the first arm's motion, and that motion is now fixed. So the eighteen-tonne blade could be replaced by anything at all that moves the hinge round the same circle at the same rate. Rearranged, (1.4) is

$$I\ddot\theta = -\frac{ml}{2}\left[\Omega^2 r_p\sin\theta + g\cos(\varphi+\theta)\right] - \left(I + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\cos\theta\right)\ddot\varphi - c\,\dot\theta, \qquad \Omega = \dot\varphi. \tag{1.6}$$

Equation (1.5) stops being an equation of motion. In the free pendulum it decided how $\varphi$ moves. Now $\varphi$ is decided, so (1.5) decides something else: the torque $\tau(t)$ that the shaft must supply at every instant to keep the first arm on its schedule against the second arm's pull. The constraint has turned an unknown motion into a known one, and a zero torque into an unknown one. That torque is the bill for everything the second blade does. Its work follows from the energy. The Lagrangian contains time only through $\varphi$, and $T$ is quadratic in the rates, so the energy $E = T + V$ of the pair changes only through the work of the shaft's torque, at the rate $\tau\dot\varphi$, and the work of the generator:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \tau\,\dot\varphi - c\,\dot\theta^2. \tag{1.7}$$

In a steady swing $E$ returns to the same value every turn, so over a turn the two terms balance: the rotor pays for every joule the generator harvests. Gravity, which seems to push the second blade, pays nothing over a turn, because the second blade comes back to the same height every turn. Chapter 4 finds the mechanism of the payment, and chapter 2 shows why, seen from the turning blade, it looks as if gravity did the work.

On our blade
The torque on the shaft is mostly not about harvesting. Each turn the rotor carries the second blade's weight up 126 m and lets it down again, which takes a torque swinging by about $mgr_p$ = {{n:tq_lift}} N m to either side and averaging to zero. The steady part that pays for the harvest is the harvested power over $\Omega$: at 6 m/s, {{n:tq_harv6}} N m, against the {{n:tq_turb6}} MN m with which the wind turns the shaft. The rotor pays, and does not notice.

When the rotor turns steadily, $\ddot\varphi = 0$, the natural clock is the rotor's own angle, one turn being $2\pi$. Write $\theta' = d\theta/d\varphi = \dot\theta/\Omega$ and divide (1.6) by $I\Omega^2$:

$$\boxed{\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi+\theta) = 0,} \qquad R = \frac{(ml/2)\,r_p}{I} = \frac{3r_p}{2l},\quad G = \frac{(ml/2)\,g}{I\,\Omega^2} = \frac{3g}{2l\Omega^2},\quad \kappa = \frac{c}{I\Omega}. \tag{1.8}$$

Three numbers now say everything. $R$ is the stiffness of the spring that the turning makes, the centrifugal spring: a free small swing about the first arm's line makes about $\sqrt R$ swings per turn of the rotor. $G$ is the strength of gravity's once-per-turn push. $\kappa$ is the generator's brake. Two second blades with the same three numbers do the same thing, turn for turn, whatever their size. $R$ does not depend on the wind: a faster rotor stiffens the spring in proportion to $\Omega^2$, so the free swing speeds up exactly as fast as the turns come. $G$ falls as the rotor speeds up, because gravity does not grow with it.

On our blade
For the {{n:lB}} m second blade at the 63 m tip, $R$ = {{n:R}} at every wind, so a free second blade swings $\sqrt R$ = {{n:sqrtR}} times per turn of the rotor, far quicker than gravity's push. $G$ is {{n:G_6}}, {{n:G_8}} and {{n:G_10}} at 6, 8 and 10 m/s. Their ratio, $R/G = \Omega^2r_p/g$, is the outward pull at the hinge in units of gravity, {{n:Gam_6}}, {{n:Gam_8}} and {{n:Gam_10}}: chapter 2 calls it $\Gamma$.

1.4 What holding the arm takes away

Holding the first arm removed one of the two angles, but not the possibility of chaos. A pendulum driven once per turn is itself a classic chaotic system: its state is three numbers, $\theta$, $\theta'$ and where the rotor is in its turn, and that is enough. Whether chaos happens depends on the three numbers of (1.8). We can test it on the computer, with the double pendulum first free and then held.

The test is the largest Lyapunov exponent $\Lambda$, the rate at which two copies of the motion started a hair apart drift apart: their separation grows as $e^{\Lambda t}$. Follow the copies, scale their separation back to a hair at regular intervals, and divide the logarithm of the total growth by the time. A regular motion gives $\Lambda \le 0$; chaos gives $\Lambda > 0$.

Free. Run the site's own double pendulum (the page of Fig. 2, its code copied out of the page and run unchanged: two point masses of 1 kg on rods of 1 m, the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with steps of 1/600 s) from its starting angles of 120° and 150°. Over {{n:dp_free_T}} s it keeps its energy to {{n:dp_free_drift}} J, so the integration is sound, and its exponent is $\Lambda$ = {{n:dp_free_lyap}} per second: any error grows by a factor $e$ every {{n:dp_free_efold}} s. Two copies started $10^{-9}$ rad apart are a metre apart within {{n:dp_free_1m}} s (Figure 4, left).

Held. Now hold the first arm of the same pendulum to a steady turning at the rate $\Omega$, and choose $\Omega$ through $\Gamma = \Omega^2l_1/g$, the outward pull at its end in units of gravity. For a point bob, (1.8) holds with $R = l_1/l_2$ and $G = g/(l_2\Omega^2)$, so $G = R/\Gamma$. Equal arms give $R = 1$, and that is a special value. The term $-1$ that appears beside $R$ in chapter 3 is the second arm's own inertia at the push's frequency; with $R = 1$ the centrifugal spring exactly cancels it, which is resonance: the push comes at the second arm's own rhythm. Of nine starts, spread over angle and rate, from one to six end chaotic at every $\Gamma$ from 0.1 to 30 (Figure 4, right).

Our second blade has $R$ = {{n:R}}. Its spring is about ninety-five times stiffer than its own inertia at the frequency of the push: it would swing nearly ten times per turn, and gravity pushes once. And the push is weak against the spring, $G/R = 1/\Gamma$ between {{n:invGam_min}} and {{n:invGam_max}}. Run the same test with these numbers and no brake. Every start inside the bowl that the spring makes about the mother blade's line ends regular, its exponent within the noise of a finite run, below {{n:dp_rod_reg_max}} per turn. Only the starts near the top of the spring's hill stay chaotic: the second blade pointing back at the hub, balanced against the outward pull, where a nudge can send it either way. Then switch the brake on, at its best value of chapter 3, and even those settle. Every exponent is negative, {{n:dp_braked_lo}} to {{n:dp_braked_hi}} per turn at the three winds, and every start ends on the same swing.

So the turbine trades a chaotic toy for a steady machine. What it takes away is the double pendulum's freedom: the first arm no longer exchanges energy with the second; it gives whatever is asked and takes back whatever is given. What it gives is a second arm that swings the same way every turn, with a power that chapter 3 writes in closed form. The price is the torque of (1.5), which the rotor pays.

Check of the reduction
Hold the equal-armed pendulum's first arm at $\Gamma = 4$, integrate (1.6) for the second arm, compute along the way the torque $\tau$ that (1.5) asks of the drive, and integrate its work, $\tau\dot\varphi$. With no brake, (1.7) says that work equals the change of the pair's energy. Over 20 turns the two agree to {{n:dp_red_gap}} J, on an energy scale of 20 J.
What to remember
  • The machine is a double pendulum: the mother blade is the first arm, the second blade the second.
  • The turbine holds the first arm to a steady turning. Its equation of motion then becomes the formula for the torque the rotor must supply, and the second arm's equation is a pendulum driven once per turn, (1.6).
  • Counted in rotor turns, $\theta'' + \kappa\theta' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi+\theta) = 0$, with $R = 3r_p/2l$ the centrifugal spring, the same at every wind, and $G$ gravity's push, weaker in stronger wind.
  • The free double pendulum is chaotic. Held, with $R$ = {{n:R}} and a brake, the second blade's motion is regular from every start: the spring is far too stiff for the push to drive it at its own rhythm.
  • The rotor pays for every joule; gravity, over a turn, pays nothing.
Problems
  1. Show that if the first arm turns at a steady rate, the first arm's mass and shape cannot affect the second arm's motion, and say which of the two Lagrange equations shows it.
    AnswerThe second arm obeys (1.4), whose only reference to the first arm is through $\varphi$, $\dot\varphi$ and $\ddot\varphi$. $J_1$ and $M_1d_1$ appear only in (1.5). Once $\varphi(t)$ is prescribed, (1.5) no longer feeds back into (1.4); it only gives the torque the drive must supply.
  2. At what wind speed would the outward pull at the tip only just equal gravity, $\Gamma = 1$, if the rotor kept to $\lambda^*$?
    Answer$\Omega^2 r_p = g$ gives $\Omega = \sqrt{g/r_p}$, and $v = \Omega r_p/\lambda^*$ = {{n:p1_v_gam1}} m/s. The turbine starts turning at 3 m/s, so in almost every wind in which it turns, the outward pull wins.
  3. How many times a second does a free second blade swing at 8 m/s? Compare a 1 m rod swinging from one end on the ground.
    Answer$\sqrt R$ swings per turn, at {{n:turn8_s}} s per turn: $\Omega\sqrt R/2\pi$ = {{n:p1_f8}} Hz. On the ground, $\sqrt{3g/2l}/2\pi$ = {{n:p1_fground}} Hz. The ratio is $\sqrt\Gamma$ = {{n:p1_sqrtGam8}}: the outward pull acts as a gravity $\Gamma$ times stronger, and a pendulum's frequency grows as the square root of its gravity. (This leaves out gravity's own share of the stiffness, which makes the rate wander by about $\pm 1/(2\Gamma)$ of itself during a turn.)
