Part IA double pendulum held by the wind

1 A double pendulum whose first arm the wind turns

Hang a short rod from the end of a longer one, hang the longer one from a nail, and let go from high up. The lower rod whips over the top, the upper one jerks back, and the motion never repeats. Start it again from what looks like the same place, and within seconds it does something else. Nothing in the laws is random, yet nobody can say where the lower rod will be a minute from now: chaos.

These notes are about a stranger double pendulum. Its first arm is a wind-turbine blade, 63 m long and about eighteen tonnes, turned by the wind at a steady rate; its second arm is a rod of one kilogram and one metre, hinged at the blade's tip and braked by a small generator. Holding the first arm to a steady turning takes the chaos away and leaves a steady swing whose power is a formula: about a fifth of a watt.

1.1 The machine

A blade cracks long before it breaks, and a crack changes how it vibrates. A sensor at the tip could hear it, if it had electricity; but a cable would have to cross the turning hub, and a battery 60 m out cannot be changed without stopping the turbine. A minimal sensor (typical currents of the ADXL362 and CC2652R datasheets; once a second, 1 ms of computing and 1 ms of sending) needs

$$P_{\rm sensor} = \underbrace{1.8\,\mu{\rm A}\cdot 2.0\,{\rm V}}_{\text{accelerometer}} + \underbrace{0.94\,\mu{\rm A}\cdot 3.0\,{\rm V}}_{\text{radio asleep}} + \underbrace{\frac{(3.39 + 7.0)\,{\rm mA}\cdot 3.0\,{\rm V}\cdot 1\,{\rm ms}}{1\,{\rm s}}}_{\text{compute and send}} = 3.6 + 2.8 + 31.2\ \mu{\rm W},$$

about {{n:sensor_uW}} µW, without the radio's start-up or a converter's losses. Twenty years of it is {{n:sensor_20y_Wh}} Wh; one D-size lithium cell (Saft LS 33600) holds about 61 Wh, if it survives twenty years inside a blade. The machine below gives thousands of times more, with nothing to replace.

The turbine is the NREL 5 MW reference turbine (Jonkman et al. 2009), Figure {{fig:turbine}}: a 90 m tower, the nacelle on top with the main generator, and in front the rotor, a hub with three blades 61.5 m long starting 1.5 m from the centre, so the tips run on a circle of radius $r_{\rm rot} = 63$ m. Below full power (11.4 m/s) the control holds the tip-speed ratio $\lambda^* = \Omega r_{\rm rot}/v = 7.55$, where the blades take the largest share of the wind's power (chapter 6). So the rotor turns at

$$\Omega = \frac{\lambda^* v}{r_{\rm rot}}.$$

At the three winds of these notes, 6, 8 and 10 m/s, that is {{n:rpm_6}}, {{n:rpm_8}} and {{n:rpm_10}} turns a minute, with the tips moving at {{n:vtip_6}}, {{n:vtip_8}} and {{n:vtip_10}} m/s. (At 6 m/s the real control turns somewhat faster, {{n:rpm_real_6}} rpm; section 6.5.)

{{table:fig_turbine}}

Now the idea. At the tip of one blade, the mother blade, a hinge with its axle parallel to the shaft carries a second, much lighter blade, free to swing in the plane of the rotor; a small generator in the hinge, the generator, brakes the swing and makes electricity. The air there rushes past at 45 to 76 m/s, so the second blade would be slim and smooth; the mathematics treats it as a thin uniform rod, $m = {{n:mB}}$ kg, $l = {{n:lB}}$ m (chapter 5 sets the mass by matching a real generator). Figure {{fig:machine}}.

Tie a weight on a short string to the end of a 1 m stick and whirl the stick in a vertical circle once a second. The string stays stretched along the stick: the turning flings the weight outwards four times harder than gravity pulls it down. But gravity still pulls: on the way up it drags the weight back, on the way down it pulls it ahead, and the string swings to and fro about the stick's line once per turn. That swing is what we harvest.

The machine is a double pendulum: the mother blade is the first arm, pivoted at the hub, the second blade the second arm. The turbine changes one rule: the first arm is not free. Until chapter 7 we leave out the air.

1.2 The free double pendulum

Let the first arm be any rigid body turning about the hub, with moment of inertia $J_1$ about the hub and first moment $M_1d_1$, its mass times the distance from the hub to its centre of mass. The second arm is a thin uniform rod of mass $m$ and length $l$, hinged a distance $r_p$ from the hub; about its hinge its moment of inertia is $I = ml^2/3$ and its first moment $ml/2$. With the hub at the origin and $y$ up, $\varphi$ is the first arm's angle from the horizontal and $\theta$ the second arm's angle from the first arm's outward line (Figure {{fig:arms}}). The piece of the second arm a distance $s$ from the hinge is at

$$x = r_p\cos\varphi + s\cos(\varphi+\theta), \qquad y = r_p\sin\varphi + s\sin(\varphi+\theta). \tag{1.1}$$
The state
feel
Glance at a swinging pendulum for an instant: you see where the bob is, not where it goes next. A second glance a moment later shows how fast it moves, and now you can say what it will do.
physics
Newton's law fixes accelerations, so positions and velocities now fix the whole future. Together they are the state; all possible states make up the phase space.
measure
An angle sensor on each pivot gives $\varphi$ and $\theta$; two readings a millisecond apart give $\dot\varphi$ and $\dot\theta$.
maths
The state is the point $(\varphi, \theta, \dot\varphi, \dot\theta)$, four numbers; a motion is a curve through phase space, and two such curves never cross.

Kinetic energy. Differentiate (1.1) and square:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot x &= -r_p\dot\varphi\sin\varphi - s(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\sin(\varphi+\theta), \qquad \dot y = r_p\dot\varphi\cos\varphi + s(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\cos(\varphi+\theta),\\ \dot x^2 + \dot y^2 &= r_p^2\dot\varphi^2 + s^2(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)^2 + 2r_ps\,\dot\varphi(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\cos\theta, \end{aligned}$$

since $\sin\varphi\sin(\varphi+\theta) + \cos\varphi\cos(\varphi+\theta)$ $= \cos\theta$. Add $\tfrac12(m/l)(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)\,ds$ along the rod, with $\int_0^l ds = l$, $\int_0^l s\,ds = l^2/2$, $\int_0^l s^2 ds = l^3/3$, and add the first arm's $\tfrac12J_1\dot\varphi^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} T = {}& \tfrac12 J_1\dot\varphi^2 + \tfrac12 I(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)^2 \\ &+ \tfrac12 m\,r_p^2\dot\varphi^2 + \frac{ml}{2}\,r_p\,\dot\varphi(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\cos\theta. \end{aligned} \tag{1.2}$$

Potential energy, each arm's weight times the height of its centre of mass:

$$V = M_1 g\,d_1\sin\varphi + mg\left[r_p\sin\varphi + \tfrac l2\sin(\varphi+\theta)\right]. \tag{1.3}$$

The brake. The generator resists the second arm's motion relative to the first with the torque $-c\dot\theta$ on the second arm (chapter 2 shows why), and, by Newton's third law, $+c\dot\theta$ on the first. A force that has no potential enters Lagrange's equations through its generalized force: turn each angle by a small $\delta q$, add up the work the force does, and write it as $\sum_q Q_q\,\delta q$. Turning $\theta$ alone by $\delta\theta$, the brake does the work $-c\dot\theta\,\delta\theta$; turning $\varphi$ alone turns both arms together, and the two torques of the pair do $+c\dot\theta\,\delta\varphi - c\dot\theta\,\delta\varphi = 0$. So the brake's generalized forces are $-c\dot\theta$ for $\theta$ and zero for $\varphi$. Both are minus the rate-derivatives of Rayleigh's dissipation function $\mathcal F = \tfrac12 c\dot\theta^2$, a short way to write them. Lagrange's equations for $\mathcal L = T - V$ then read $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q} = -\frac{\partial\mathcal F}{\partial\dot q} + Q_q$, with $Q_q$ the generalized force of any other outside torque. For $\theta$:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta} &= I(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta) + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\,\dot\varphi\cos\theta,\\ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta} &= I(\ddot\varphi+\ddot\theta) + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\left(\ddot\varphi\cos\theta - \dot\varphi\dot\theta\sin\theta\right),\\ \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\theta} &= -\frac{ml}{2}r_p\,\dot\varphi(\dot\varphi+\dot\theta)\sin\theta - \frac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi+\theta). \end{aligned}$$

Subtract the third line from the second; the two terms in $\dot\varphi\dot\theta\sin\theta$ cancel:

$$\begin{aligned} &I(\ddot\varphi+\ddot\theta) + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\left(\ddot\varphi\cos\theta + \dot\varphi^2\sin\theta\right) \\ &\quad + \frac{ml}{2}\,g\cos(\varphi+\theta) = -c\,\dot\theta. \end{aligned} \tag{1.4}$$

For $\varphi$, with $\tau$ the torque that something outside the pair applies to the first arm, and $p_\varphi = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\varphi$:

$$\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}\Big[\underbrace{(J_1 + I + m r_p^2 + m l r_p\cos\theta)\,\dot\varphi + \Big(I + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\cos\theta\Big)\dot\theta}_{p_\varphi}\Big] \\ &\quad + M_1gd_1\cos\varphi + mg\Big[r_p\cos\varphi + \frac l2\cos(\varphi+\theta)\Big] = \tau. \end{aligned} \tag{1.5}$$

The generator is absent from (1.5): $\mathcal F$ does not contain $\dot\varphi$, because turning both arms together does no work against a brake between them. The torque $\tau$ on the first arm does the work $\tau\,\delta\varphi$, so it is $Q_\varphi$ itself.

Check
The site's own double pendulum (Fig. 2 of the simulations) has point masses on massless rods, angles from the downward vertical. Put $J_1 = m_1l_1^2$, $M_1d_1 = m_1l_1$, $r_p = l_1$, shrink the second arm to a point mass $m_2$ at its end ($I \to m_2l_2^2$, $ml/2 \to m_2l_2$), and set $\theta_1 = \varphi + 90°$, $\theta_2 = \varphi + \theta + 90°$: (1.2) and (1.3) become that page's $T$ and $V$ term for term. Hold $\varphi$ still with $c = 0$: (1.4) is $I\ddot\theta = -\tfrac{ml}{2}g\cos(\varphi+\theta)$, the rod pendulum, $\omega^2 = 3g/(2l)$.

Left alone ($\tau = 0$, $c = 0$), the pair keeps $T + V$ and the two angles trade it back and forth through the cross term of (1.2). Four numbers, one conserved energy: the motion runs on a three-dimensional surface in phase space, and that is room enough for chaos.

1.3 Holding the first arm

A constraint that moves
feel
Whirl a bucket of water over your head in a vertical circle. Your arm decides the bucket's path; the water cannot change it, but you feel its tug change round the circle.
physics
The rotor is the arm, the second blade the water. The wind and the turbine's control decide the rotor's motion, whatever the second blade does; its angle stops being an unknown and becomes a given function of time.
measure
The second blade's weight, 10 N on a 63 m lever, twists the shaft by about $mgr_p$ = {{n:tq_lift}} N m; the wind turns it with {{n:tq_turb6}} MN m at 6 m/s, against a rotor and drivetrain of $4.4\times10^7$ kg m². The second blade cannot change the rotor's speed.
maths
$\varphi(t) = \Omega t$ is imposed: a constraint that depends on time. The equation (1.4) of the remaining angle keeps its form; the equation (1.5) of the imposed angle no longer decides $\varphi$, and gives instead the torque $\tau(t)$ that imposes it.

Equation (1.4) survives as the equation of the second blade alone. Solved for $\ddot\theta$, every term has a new meaning:

$$\begin{aligned} I\ddot\theta = &-\underbrace{\frac{ml}{2}\,\Omega^2 r_p\sin\theta}_{\text{centrifugal spring}} - \underbrace{\frac{ml}{2}\,g\cos(\varphi+\theta)}_{\text{gravity, once per turn}} \\ &- \underbrace{\Big(I + \frac{ml}{2}r_p\cos\theta\Big)\ddot\varphi}_{\text{only while the rotor speeds up}} - \underbrace{c\,\dot\theta}_{\text{brake}}, \qquad \Omega = \dot\varphi. \end{aligned} \tag{1.6}$$

The term that coupled the arms through $\dot\varphi^2$ is now a spring. Gravity's term now carries a clock, $\varphi = \Omega t$. And $J_1$ and $M_1d_1$ are gone: the eighteen-tonne blade could be anything that moves the hinge round the same circle at the same rate. This is Landau and Lifshitz's pendulum whose support moves uniformly round a vertical circle (Mechanics, §5, problem 3), made of a rod, and braked.

Equation (1.5) becomes the bill: the torque $\tau(t)$ the shaft must supply to keep the first arm on schedule. Its work follows from the energy $E = T + V$. Every term of $T$ in (1.2) is a product of two rates, so doubling all rates multiplies $T$ by four, and Euler's theorem for such functions gives $\sum_q\dot q\,\partial T/\partial\dot q = 2T$; $V$ contains no rates. Hence $\sum_q\dot q\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q - \mathcal L = 2T - (T - V) = E$. Differentiate this with respect to time. $\mathcal L$ contains no explicit time, so $d\mathcal L/dt = \sum_q(\dot q\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial q + \ddot q\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q)$, and the terms in $\ddot q$ cancel against those of $\frac{d}{dt}\sum_q\dot q\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q$. What is left is each rate times its Lagrange expression, which (1.4) and (1.5) set equal to $-c\dot\theta$ and $\tau$:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \sum_q \dot q\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q}\Big) = \tau\,\dot\varphi - c\,\dot\theta^2. \tag{1.7}$$

In a steady swing $E$ returns every turn, so $\oint\tau\dot\varphi\,dt = \oint c\dot\theta^2\,dt$: the rotor pays for every joule the generator harvests. Gravity pays nothing over a turn; the second blade comes back to the same height. Chapter 4 finds how the payment is made, and chapter 2 why it looks, from the turning blade, as if gravity did the work.

On our blade
Most of $\tau$ lifts the second blade's weight up 126 m and lets it down again, $\pm mgr_p$, averaging to zero. The steady part, harvested power over $\Omega$, is {{n:tq_harv6}} N m at 6 m/s, against the wind's {{n:tq_turb6}} MN m. The rotor pays, and does not notice.

Counted in turns. With the rotor steady, $\ddot\varphi = 0$, the natural clock is the rotor's own angle. Write $\theta' = d\theta/d\varphi$, so that $d/dt = \Omega\,d/d\varphi$, and divide (1.6) by $I\Omega^2$:

$$I\Omega^2\theta'' = -\frac{ml}{2}\left[\Omega^2 r_p\sin\theta + g\cos(\varphi+\theta)\right] - c\,\Omega\,\theta' \qquad\Big|\ \div\, I\Omega^2$$ $$\boxed{\theta'' + \kappa\,\theta' + R\sin\theta + G\cos(\varphi+\theta) = 0,} \qquad R = \frac{(ml/2)\,r_p}{I} = \frac{3r_p}{2l},\quad G = \frac{(ml/2)\,g}{I\,\Omega^2} = \frac{3g}{2l\Omega^2},\quad \kappa = \frac{c}{I\Omega}. \tag{1.8}$$

Three numbers say everything. $R$ is the centrifugal spring: flicked, the second blade rings $\sqrt R$ times per turn, at every wind. $G$ is gravity's once-per-turn push, falling as $1/\Omega^2$. $\kappa$ is the brake. $R/G = \Omega^2r_p/g$ is the outward pull at the hinge in units of gravity, $\Gamma$ (chapter 2).

On our blade
$R$ = {{n:R}} at every wind: flicked, the second blade rings $\sqrt R$ = {{n:sqrtR}} times per turn, {{n:p1_f8}} times a second at 8 m/s. $G$ = {{n:G_6}}, {{n:G_8}} and {{n:G_10}} at 6, 8 and 10 m/s; $\Gamma$ = {{n:Gam_6}}, {{n:Gam_8}} and {{n:Gam_10}}.

1.4 Chaos, measured

Holding the first arm removed one angle, not the possibility of chaos: a pendulum pushed once per turn has a state of three numbers, $\theta$, $\theta'$ and $\varphi$, and three are enough. Chaos needs a measure.

The Lyapunov exponent
feel
Knead dough with two poppy seeds pressed side by side. Each fold stretches the dough and roughly doubles their distance; after ten folds one may sit at each end of the loaf.
physics
Chaos is stretching and folding of phase space. Two states a hair apart drift apart exponentially, until their distance is as large as the motion itself.
measure
Run the motion twice from starts a hair apart and plot the logarithm of their distance against time: the slope of the straight part is $\Lambda$ (Figure {{fig:double}}, left). (In practice: scale the distance back to a hair at regular intervals and add up the logarithms of the growth.)
maths
$|\delta(t)| \approx |\delta_0|\,e^{\Lambda t}$; $\Lambda > 0$ is chaos, $\Lambda \le 0$ regular motion.
$$\Lambda = \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac1t\ln\frac{|\delta(t)|}{|\delta_0|}, \qquad t_D = \frac1\Lambda\ln\frac{D}{\delta_0}. \tag{1.9}$$

The second formula is the time for an error $\delta_0$ to grow to the size $D$. The logarithm carries a lesson: a start measured a thousand times better buys only $\ln(1000)/\Lambda$ more time.

Free. The site's own double pendulum (Fig. 2 of the simulations, its code run unchanged: 1 kg bobs on 1 m rods, fourth-order Runge–Kutta, steps of 1/600 s), from 120° and 150°, keeps its energy to {{n:dp_free_drift}} J over {{n:dp_free_T}} s, and has $\Lambda$ = {{n:dp_free_lyap}} per second (over the last half of the run the estimate moves by less than {{n:dp_free_settle}}): an error doubles every $\ln 2/\Lambda$ = {{n:dp_free_double}} s. By (1.9), an error of $10^{-9}$ rad in the angles, which puts the lower bob about $10^{-9}$ m off on its 1 m rod, grows to a metre, the size of the whole motion, in {{n:dp_horizon}} s; the twin runs of Figure {{fig:double}} take {{n:dp_free_1m}} s, since the first seconds are not yet exponential. A thousand times better start buys {{n:dp_gain_1000}} s.

Held. Now hold the same pendulum's first arm to a steady turning, with the outward pull at its end $\Gamma = \Omega^2l_1/g$ times gravity. For a point bob, (1.8) still holds, with $R = l_1/l_2$ and $G = R/\Gamma$. With equal arms, $R = 1$, the spring exactly cancels the second arm's inertia at the push's rhythm: a swing $\theta \propto \cos\varphi$ has $\theta'' = -\theta$, so shaking it once per turn costs the inertia exactly one unit of stiffness, and the push meets the net stiffness $R - 1$ (chapter 3). That is resonance, and from one to six of nine starts end chaotic at every $\Gamma$ from 0.1 to 30 (Figure {{fig:double}}, right). Our second blade, $R$ = {{n:R}}, is ninety-five times stiffer and only gently pushed, $G/R$ = {{n:invGam_min}} to {{n:invGam_max}}. Picture its centrifugal spring as a landscape (section 1.5 draws it): a bowl around the mother blade's outward line, where the outward pull draws the second blade back, and a hilltop opposite, where the second blade points back at the hub and the pull drives it away. Unbraked, every start in the bowl ends regular (exponents below {{n:dp_rod_reg_max}} per turn, the noise of a finite run); only starts near the hilltop, the second blade pointing back at the hub, stay chaotic. With the best brake, $\kappa^* = R - 1$ (chapter 3 derives it), every exponent is negative, {{n:dp_braked_lo}} to {{n:dp_braked_hi}} per turn. A negative exponent is the rate at which the motion forgets its start. A light brake forgets faster than the best one (Table 1), which looks odd until section 3.3: the best brake is about five times critical damping, so a nudged second blade creeps back slowly, at the slow root of the braked spring, about $-R/\kappa^*$.

1.5 Why the held blade is calm: a slow push and a moving hilltop

The held blade is regular in its bowl and chaotic only near the hilltop, and a landscape shows why. Unbraked, the torques of (1.8) are minus the slope of a landscape, $\theta'' = -\partial u/\partial\theta$, with $u = -R\cos\theta + G\sin(\varphi+\theta)$. Expand the sine and collect:

$$\begin{aligned} u(\theta,\varphi) &= -(R - G\sin\varphi)\cos\theta + G\cos\varphi\,\sin\theta \\ &= -A\cos(\theta - \theta_b), \qquad A = \sqrt{R^2 - 2RG\sin\varphi + G^2}, \quad \tan\theta_b = \frac{-G\cos\varphi}{R - G\sin\varphi}. \end{aligned} \tag{1.10}$$

The landscape is a bowl and a hill. The bowl's bottom, at $\theta_b$, is where the second blade points along the apparent gravity, the outward pull and gravity added as arrows (chapter 2 draws them). The hill is opposite, where the second blade points back at the hub. The depth is set by $A$: $A\,I\Omega^2$ is $\tfrac{ml}{2}$ times the apparent gravity at the hinge, $|g_{\rm eff}|$. That is weakest, $\Omega^2r_p - g$, when the mother blade points up, and strongest, $\Omega^2r_p + g$, when it points down. So the hill's height above the bowl, $2A\,I\Omega^2 = ml\,|g_{\rm eff}|$, rises and falls once per turn (Figure {{fig:hill}}).

On our blade
At 6 m/s the apparent gravity at the hinge swings between {{n:ps_geff_lo}} and {{n:ps_geff_hi}} m/s². To tip the second blade over the hill from rest at the bottom takes {{n:ps_hill_lo}} J when the mother blade points up and {{n:ps_hill_hi}} J when it points down. In the bowl it rings $\sqrt A$ = {{n:ps_w_lo}} to {{n:ps_w_hi}} times per turn.

Two clocks: the swing ticks about ten times per turn, the landscape changes once. To the swing, the landscape moves slowly, and slow change has a law.

The adiabatic invariant
feel
Swing a weight on a string, and slowly pull the string up through your fingers. The swings quicken and grow livelier, in step: twice the frequency, twice the energy. Jerk the string, and the step is lost.
physics
When the conditions change slowly compared with one swing, the swing keeps one quantity: its action, the area its path encloses in the plane of angle and rate, divided by $2\pi$. For a small swing the action is energy over frequency.
measure
Time the swings and measure their height before and after a slow change: energy over frequency comes out the same.
maths
$J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\theta'\,d\theta$. A small swing of amplitude $a$ in the landscape (1.10), whose stiffness at the bottom is $A$, has $\theta' = \sqrt A\,a\cos\psi$ and $J = \tfrac12\sqrt A\,a^2$ = (energy above the bottom)/$\sqrt A$. While the change goes on, $J$ wobbles by a fraction of the order of the ratio of the two clocks, but it does not drift; once a smooth change is over, it has changed by an amount exponentially small in that ratio (Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §49 and §51).

★ Why the action is kept. Let the swing obey a Hamiltonian $H(\theta, p; \lambda)$, $p = \theta'$, whose landscape depends on a parameter $\lambda$ that changes slowly with the clock $\varphi$ (here $\lambda$ is the rotor angle itself, as it enters $A$ and $\theta_b$ in (1.10); the change is slow in the sense that the landscape moves little during one swing, a tenth of a turn). The energy then changes only through the explicit dependence, $dH/d\varphi = (\partial H/\partial\lambda)\,\lambda'$. Average this over one swing, holding $\lambda$ fixed because it hardly moves in that time, and write the time element as $d\varphi = d\theta/\theta' = d\theta/(\partial H/\partial p)$:

$$\Big\langle\frac{dE}{d\varphi}\Big\rangle = \frac{\lambda'}{T}\oint\frac{\partial H/\partial\lambda}{\partial H/\partial p}\,d\theta, \qquad T = \oint\frac{d\theta}{\partial H/\partial p} = \oint\frac{\partial p}{\partial E}\,d\theta.$$

On the orbit, $H(\theta, p(\theta, E, \lambda), \lambda) = E$; differentiating at fixed $E$ and $\theta$ gives $\partial H/\partial\lambda + (\partial H/\partial p)(\partial p/\partial\lambda) = 0$, so the ratio in the integral is $-\partial p/\partial\lambda$, and

$$\oint\Big(\frac{\partial p}{\partial E}\,\Big\langle\frac{dE}{d\varphi}\Big\rangle + \frac{\partial p}{\partial\lambda}\,\lambda'\Big)\,d\theta = 0, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \frac{d}{d\varphi}\oint p\,d\theta = 0$$

on average: the area $2\pi J$ enclosed by the orbit does not drift. The step "hold $\lambda$ fixed over one swing" is where slowness enters; it fails when one swing lasts as long as the change itself, which is what happens near the hilltop below.

So each swing in the bowl keeps its action while the bowl rocks and breathes, and its motion stays regular. The law fails near the hilltop. A pencil balanced on its point hesitates longer the closer it starts to upright, without bound. Let $x$ be the second blade's angle from the top, $\theta = \theta_b + \pi - x$. Near the top $u \approx A(1 - x^2/2)$, a hill instead of a bowl, and the equation of motion pushes away from it:

$$x'' = A\,x \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = x_{\min}\cosh\big(\sqrt A\,(\varphi - \varphi_0)\big), \qquad \text{rotor angle spent within } |x| < 1 \approx \frac{2}{\sqrt A}\,\ln\frac{2}{x_{\min}},$$

with $x_{\min}$ the closest approach to the top, reached at $\varphi_0$. (Solve $x_{\min}\cosh u = 1$: $u = \operatorname{arccosh}(1/x_{\min}) \approx \ln(2/x_{\min})$, once on the way up and once on the way down; divide by $2\pi$ for turns.) A small swing's whole period is $2\pi/\sqrt A$ of rotor angle, about a tenth of a turn; a swing reaching 3 rad from the bottom, its time stretched by the hesitation, already has a period of {{n:ps_period_3}} turns. Near the top the two clocks become comparable, and the hill's height changes during the hesitation: arrive while it is low and the second blade goes over, while it is high and it falls back. The outcome hangs on the exact moment of arrival: stretching, and chaos.

The swing that just reaches the top has, from $\tfrac12\theta'^2 - A\cos(\theta-\theta_b) = A$, the rate $\theta' = 2\sqrt A\cos\tfrac{\theta-\theta_b}{2}$, and the action

$$J_s(A) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot 2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 2\sqrt A\cos\frac u2\,du = \frac{8\sqrt A}{\pi}. \tag{1.11}$$

A tumbling motion, the second blade going right round, does not close on itself in the plane of angle and rate. Its action counts one pass round, $\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\theta'\,d\theta$, and just at the top that is $J_s/2$. Over a turn $A$ runs from $R - G$ to $R + G$. A swing whose action stays below the lowest hill's $J_s$ never reaches the top. A tumble whose action stays above the highest hill's $J_s/2$ always clears it. Both are regular. Chaos is left to the band between them, the motions with

$$J_{\rm swing} > \frac{8\sqrt{R-G}}{\pi} \quad\text{or}\quad J_{\rm tumble} < \frac{4\sqrt{R+G}}{\pi}. \tag{1.12}$$

To see the band, look once per turn.

The Poincaré section
feel
In a dark hall a strobe lamp flashes once per beat on a dancer. If her steps repeat with the music she seems to stand still; if they drift slowly against it she creeps along a path; if she ignores the beat she is somewhere new at every flash.
physics
Looking only when the rotor passes the same angle turns the continuous motion into a sequence of points in the plane of $\theta$ and $\theta'$. A steady swing is one point; a regular motion lies on a closed curve; chaos scatters over an area.
measure

The map $(\theta_n, \theta'_n) \mapsto (\theta_{n+1}, \theta'_{n+1})$. Without a brake it keeps areas (Liouville's theorem), so a regular orbit cannot spiral in: it circles on its ring for ever. A brake $\kappa$ shrinks every area by $e^{-2\pi\kappa}$ per turn.

Estimate against computer
At 6 m/s, (1.12) says: chaotic are swings with action above {{n:ps_Jsep_lo}} and tumbles below {{n:ps_Jout}}. Turning an action into an amplitude takes the pendulum's exact orbit: a swing of amplitude $a$ in the landscape (1.10) has $J = \frac{8\sqrt A}{\pi}\big[E(k) - (1-k^2)K(k)\big]$ with $k = \sin\frac a2$, where $K$ and $E$ are the complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind; for small $a$ this is the $\tfrac12\sqrt A\,a^2$ above, and at $a = \pi$ it is $J_s$. In the snapshot that is a swing reaching more than {{n:ps_amp_pred}}° from the bowl's bottom; scanning starts with the computer, chaos begins at {{n:ps_amp_meas_lo}}° to {{n:ps_amp_meas_hi}}°. Passing the bowl's bottom, the band runs from $\theta'$ = {{n:ps_win_pred}} to {{n:ps_wout_pred}} radians of swing per radian of rotor turning ({{n:ps_win_rads}} to {{n:ps_wout_rads}} rad/s); the computer finds {{n:ps_win_meas}} to {{n:ps_wout_meas_lo}} forwards and to {{n:ps_wout_meas_hi}} backwards. (In these scans a start counts as chaotic when its exponent over 400 turns exceeds {{n:ps_thr}} per turn; Figure {{fig:double}} used a finer test, 0.02.) A two-line argument, with nothing fitted, puts the edge of chaos within a few per cent.

Switch on the best brake, $\kappa^* = R - 1$ (chapter 3), and every area shrinks by $e^{-2\pi\kappa^*}$ per turn: the rings collapse within a turn onto one point, the steady swing, and the chaotic band empties with them. The exponents of Table 1 are that collapse, each the slower decay of a nudged, braked spring, $2\pi\max\mathrm{Re}\,\mu$ with $\mu^2 + \kappa\mu + R = 0$. The turbine trades a chaotic toy for a steady machine, whose power chapter 3 writes in closed form; the price is the torque of (1.5), which the rotor pays.

Check of the reduction
Hold the equal-armed pendulum's first arm at $\Gamma = 4$, integrate (1.6) for the second arm, compute along the way the torque $\tau$ that (1.5) asks of the drive, and integrate its work, $\tau\dot\varphi$. With no brake, (1.7) says that work equals the change of the pair's energy. Over 20 turns the two agree to {{n:dp_red_gap}} J, on an energy scale of 20 J.
What to remember
  • The machine is a double pendulum: the mother blade is the first arm, the second blade the second.
  • Held, the first arm's equation becomes the torque the rotor supplies, (1.5); the second arm's is a pendulum pushed once per turn, (1.6).
  • In turns: $\theta'' + \kappa\theta' + R\sin\theta$ $+ G\cos(\varphi+\theta) = 0$. $R = 3r_p/2l$ is the centrifugal spring, the same at every wind; $G$ is gravity's push, weaker in stronger wind.
  • Chaos is measured by $\Lambda$, the rate at which nearby states separate; a better start buys only logarithmically more time, (1.9).
  • The held blade swings ten times per turn in a landscape that changes once: it keeps its action and moves regularly. Chaos lives only in the band (1.12), near a hilltop that rises and falls each turn; the brake empties even that.
  • The rotor pays for every joule; gravity, over a turn, pays nothing.
Problems
  1. Show that if the first arm turns at a steady rate, the first arm's mass and shape cannot affect the second arm's motion, and say which of the two Lagrange equations shows it.
    AnswerThe second arm obeys (1.4), whose only reference to the first arm is through $\varphi$, $\dot\varphi$ and $\ddot\varphi$. $J_1$ and $M_1d_1$ appear only in (1.5). Once $\varphi(t)$ is prescribed, (1.5) no longer feeds back into (1.4); it only gives the torque the drive must supply.
  2. At what wind speed would the outward pull at the tip only just equal gravity, $\Gamma = 1$, if the rotor kept to $\lambda^*$?
    Answer$\Omega^2 r_p = g$ gives $\Omega = \sqrt{g/r_p}$, and $v = \Omega r_p/\lambda^*$ = {{n:p1_v_gam1}} m/s. The turbine starts turning at 3 m/s, so in almost every wind in which it turns, the outward pull wins.
  3. How many times a second does a free second blade swing at 8 m/s? Compare a 1 m rod swinging from one end on the ground.
    Answer$\sqrt R$ swings per turn, at {{n:turn8_s}} s per turn: $\Omega\sqrt R/2\pi$ = {{n:p1_f8}} Hz. On the ground, $\sqrt{3g/2l}/2\pi$ = {{n:p1_fground}} Hz. The ratio is $\sqrt\Gamma$ = {{n:p1_sqrtGam8}}: the outward pull acts as a gravity $\Gamma$ times stronger, and a pendulum's frequency grows as the square root of its gravity. (Gravity's own share of the stiffness makes the rate breathe between $\sqrt{R-G}$ and $\sqrt{R+G}$ per turn, (1.10).)
  4. Show from (1.12) that the chaotic band shrinks as the wind rises, and say why in one sentence.
    AnswerThe hilltop's action $J_s = 8\sqrt A/\pi$ runs over a turn from $8\sqrt{R-G}/\pi$ to $8\sqrt{R+G}/\pi$. For $G \ll R$, $\sqrt{R \pm G} \approx \sqrt R\,(1 \pm G/2R)$, so the spread is $(8\sqrt R/\pi)\,G/R$, proportional to $1/\Gamma$, which falls as $1/\Omega^2$. A faster rotor makes the outward pull dominate gravity, so the hill hardly rises and falls.
