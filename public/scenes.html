Behind the pages · twelve small pictures

Living scenes.

Behind and between the pages of this site, small pictures move: a forest, roots, pendulums, iron powder, a harmonica. Each is a little physics story. Here every one runs on its own, with what it shows, what drives it, and its numbers to change. They are pictures drawn for the eye, not the site's measured simulations; the pendulums are the exception, played from a run computed once on Kaggle.

You asked your system for reduced motion, so the scenes do not move here. The text of each stays below.

S1 The forest

Home page, the land under the title.

What you see. A few trees throw seeds; a seed that finds room grows into a tree in about one spring and summer, and over several years of two minutes each the land fills. Leaves fall in autumn, rot in the soil and are taken up by the roots of the young trees.

What drives it. The leaves breathe vapour that rises into the open sky and gathers into clouds. Each cloud is built in 3D, round puffs on a flat base 1000 m up, and drawn as seen by someone standing on the ground: near clouds high and large, far ones small by the horizon, their flat undersides seen from below, and the same wind carrying near clouds across faster than far ones. A cloud's droplets grow by condensation until collisions merge them into raindrops heavy enough to beat the updraft (their speed grows as r², Stokes' law); the rain feeds the seedlings, and in winter the clouds snow. On the home page the sky is open only in the two top corners, beside the title and beside the poem, so that is where the clouds gather.

S2 Roots

Home page, the left margin, below the forest.

What you see. Roots grow down from the surface, branching like leaf veins or river beds; dots of sap climb back up.

What drives it. Space colonisation: water lies scattered in the soil, every drop pulls the nearest root tip within reach towards it, and a reached drop is used up. A root is as thick as the cube root of the number of tips it feeds (Murray's law: the cube of a parent's radius is the sum of its children's cubes), the rule that also sets the widths of blood vessels.

S3 Two pendulums and a spring

Home page, the right margin, a pair on every beam.

What you see. Two pendulums, 1.5 m long with 10 kg steel bobs, joined by a weak spring. The left one starts swinging; within about 13.5 s its swing has drained completely into the right one, and after 27 s it is back. The curves under them are each pendulum's energy over one loop; the line marks the moment on show.

What drives it. Swinging together, the spring idles and both keep the pendulum's own frequency ω₁ = √(g/L); swinging against each other, the spring works and they go a little faster, ω₂ = √(ω₁² + 2ka²/(mL²)). Starting one pendulum starts both motions at once, and their beat carries the swing across and back with period 2π/(ω₂ − ω₁). The motion is not computed here: tools/sims/coupled.py integrated the full equations once on Kaggle, with the energy kept to a few parts in 10¹³, and this page plays that stored run.

S4 Spinners locking into step

Home page, the band before the simulations.

What you see. Little three-armed spinners turn at their own random speeds. A rotating field wanders over them, and those it passes turn in step with it, brighter while they are locked.

What drives it. Each spinner's angle obeys θ' = ω + K sin(φ − θ) + (pull of its neighbours), the Kuramoto and Adler picture of phase locking: when the field's pull K is stronger than the spinner's own mistuning, the spinner is captured.

S5 The shores of light

Home page, the band above the footer.

What you see. Motes rise out of a slow dark tide and brighten as they cross its surface into the light.

What drives it. A picture of Lucretius (I 170, 179): things come into "the shores of light" from what was unseen. The surface is two waves added; brightness grows with the height above it.

S6 Iron powder

Simulations page, beside the title.

What you see. Iron filings on a sheet line up along the field of two slowly turning bar magnets; a wire through the sheet switches its current on and off, and the filings around it curl into rings.

What drives it. Each grain turns along the local field and drifts sideways towards the nearest field line, as real filings do when the sheet is tapped (the tap is the shiver every 3.5 s).

S7 A harmonica

Simulations page, the band above the footer.

What you see. A ten-hole harmonica plays; each note travels out through the air as rings that push the dots of air back and forth, and a dot far away moves only when the ring reaches it.

What drives it. Each hole's reed sends a wave of its own wavelength; the displacement at distance r is the reed's motion from the time r/c ago, so sound takes time to arrive (drawn here at a speed far below the real 343 m/s).

S8 A chip

Portfolios page, beside the title.

What you see. The blocks of a chip are traced, wires are routed between them one by one, and the finished die is stamped again and again across a round wafer.

What drives it. Not physics but the order of chip making: design, routing, then photolithography repeats one mask over the wafer.

S9 Mixing

Portfolios page, the band above the footer.

What you see. Three gases, hot triangles, warm rings and cold squares, wait in three chambers; the walls lift, the gases mix, and the curves show the mixing (solid) rise and the difference of temperature (dashed) fall.

What drives it. Elastic collisions between equal discs: each collision swaps the velocity components along the line between them, so energy spreads until every species moves alike, the second law in a box.

S10 Dust in a sunbeam

Lucretius page, beside the title.

What you see. Dust trembles in a slanting beam of light, lit only inside the beam.

What drives it. Lucretius' own picture (II 112–141) of Brownian motion: each mote is slowed by drag and kicked at random by molecules nobody sees, dv = −γ v dt + σ dW, small motes trembling more.

S11 The swerve

Lucretius page, the side margins.

What you see. Atoms fall side by side and never meet, until one tilts a little; it strikes a neighbour, and a cascade of collisions follows.

What drives it. Lucretius' clinamen (II 216–293): without the swerve the atoms would fall forever in parallel and nothing would form.

S12 Seeds

Lucretius page, the band above the footer.

What you see. Seeds fall and grow into a rose, an ear of wheat or a bunch of grapes, each from its own seed, then wither.

What drives it. Lucretius I 174–175: every thing comes from its own seed, at its own time.
