Lecture note · Fig. 12 computed on Kaggle · fluid dynamics · data

A swing that should ring, and stored data that says it does not

Numa Koudsie · for physics undergraduates; ★ marks the heavier mathematics, whose results are restated without it

Abstract. A pint of stout rides a one-wheel cart over a gentle road. Each bump sets the stout rocking front to back, and a real stout in a smooth glass keeps rocking for many seconds. The rides on this page were computed ahead of time on Kaggle’s graphics card and are only played back here. We first work out what the stout should do: the frequency of its first sloshing mode, 3.421 Hz, from the linear theory of a liquid in an upright cylinder, and its damping, a damping ratio of 0.0036 to 0.0053, from the boundary layer at the wall. We then draw the swing in phase space, show how to read the mode and its rate from stored surfaces without bias, and measure the stored rides. Their swing has the right pace but dies twelve to forty times too fast, and the lean read at single wall points drifts by several millimetres while the surface as a whole stays level. Last, we explain how the page plays stored data: no blending between frames, speeds that ease rather than jump, and a live preview, clearly labelled, where Kaggle has not computed.

01A pint that keeps rocking

Set a full pint down on a table a little too hard and watch the surface. It tips toward you, back, toward you again, a few times a second, and it goes on doing so long after your hand has left the glass.

That slow fading is the subject of this note. The stout’s rocking is a swing in the same sense as a playground swing: a restoring force, here gravity pulling the raised side of the surface down, and an inertia, the stout that has to be set moving. What stops a swing is friction. For a liquid in a smooth glass the friction is weak: it acts only in a thin layer of stout next to the wall, and the rest of the liquid slides past almost freely. So a real pint, once disturbed, rings for many seconds.

The figure above does not compute the stout. It plays rides that were computed ahead of time on a graphics card rented through Kaggle, a million particles of stout in the measured Guinness glass, and stored on this site. A page that only plays stored data can still be checked. We can work out from first principles what the stout should do, then read the stored data and see whether it does. It turns out that the stored stout swings at the right pace but stops far too soon, and the rest of this note explains how we know.

02What Kaggle computed

The stout is computed in the frame of the glass, as a passenger on the cart would see it, because the walls are then at rest.

Let \(\mathbf u\) be the velocity of the stout relative to the glass, \(p\) its pressure, \(\rho\) its density and \(\mathbf a_g(t)\) the acceleration of the glass. A frame that accelerates with \(\mathbf a_g\) adds the force \(-\rho\,\mathbf a_g\) per unit volume, so the stout feels an effective gravity \(\mathbf g - \mathbf a_g\). Without viscosity the equations Kaggle solved are Euler’s equations for an incompressible liquid,

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = -\frac{\nabla p}{\rho} + \mathbf g - \mathbf a_g , \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0 , \tag{1} \]

with the stout unable to cross the glass, \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n = 0\) on the wall, and the air’s pressure on the free surface. The cart is the one-wheel cart of Fig. 12: a body of 1.5 kg on a spring of 600 N/m and a damper of 8 N s/m over one wheel, driven by a motor whose speed loop holds the set speed. The stout and the cart push on each other: the glass’s acceleration enters (1), and the stout pushes back on the glass with the force \(-\mathrm d\mathbf P/\mathrm dt\), where \(\mathbf P\) is the stout’s momentum in the glass’s frame.

On Kaggle’s Tesla T4 the stout is 1,002,808 particles, eight in each cell of a grid of 1.5 mm with 370,386 cells (the APIC method [3], in the Taichi language). Each step of 2 ms took 123 ms, so a ride of 20 s, 10,000 steps, took 20 to 22 minutes. The road is a long hump 10 mm high at 1.5 m, a dip 8 mm deep at 3.5 m, four ripples 5 mm high from 5.5 to 7.5 m and a short bump 10 mm high at 9 m, then flat road; it was ridden at 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 m/s. Thirty times a second the notebook stored the cart’s position, speed and heights, the lean of the surface, the spilled mass, and the surface itself on a polar grid of 17 rings and 64 angles, each height rounded to steps of 0.2 mm. These files, generated on 12 November 2026, are what the page plays; their date, notebook version and checksums are in index.json.

Two things are left out of (1) on purpose: the stout’s viscosity and the friction of the glass wall. The creamy head is drawn on the surface, not computed.

03The first sloshing mode ★

For small swings the stout’s rocking is a sum of standing waves, the modes of the glass. The slowest is the one that tips the surface as a whole, and its frequency we can calculate.

Near the surface the Guinness tulip is almost a cylinder: within the top 20 mm, where the swing lives, its radius changes by less than 1 mm. We therefore take an upright cylinder of the radius at the surface, \(R = R(h) = 39.1\) mm, filled to the depth \(h = 125\) mm. For small motions started from rest the flow has no vorticity, \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), and incompressibility makes the potential harmonic, \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\). In cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\), with \(z\) up from the floor, we look for a standing wave that tips the surface once around the glass:

\[ \phi = \cos\omega t\;\cos\theta\;J_1(kr)\,\cosh kz . \tag{2} \]

Here \(J_1\) is the Bessel function of order one; it solves the radial part of Laplace’s equation for a \(\cos\theta\) dependence, and \(\cosh kz\) the vertical part, with the same \(k\). The cosine in \(z\) makes \(\partial\phi/\partial z = 0\) at the floor, \(z = 0\): no flow through the bottom. The wall allows no flow through it either, \(\partial\phi/\partial r = 0\) at \(r = R\), so

\[ J_1'(kR) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad kR = \xi_{11} = 1.8412 , \tag{3} \]

the first zero of \(J_1'\). At the free surface two conditions hold. The surface moves with the liquid, \(\partial\eta/\partial t = \partial\phi/\partial z\), and the pressure there is the air’s, which for small motions is Bernoulli’s law \(\partial\phi/\partial t + g\eta = 0\). Differentiating the second in time and using the first eliminates \(\eta\):

\[ \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2} + g\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z} = 0 \quad\text{at } z = h . \tag{4} \]

Putting (2) into (4) gives \(-\omega^2\cosh kh + gk\sinh kh = 0\), that is,

\[ \omega_{11}^2 = \frac{g\,\xi_{11}}{R}\,\tanh\frac{\xi_{11}h}{R} . \tag{5} \]

The same result is in Abramson’s review of sloshing in cylindrical tanks [2, §2.3].

Key result · the first sloshing frequency

An upright cylinder of radius \(R\) filled to depth \(h\) rocks its surface at \(\omega_{11}^2 = (g\xi_{11}/R)\tanh(\xi_{11}h/R)\), \(\xi_{11} = 1.8412\). For a glass deeper than its radius, \(\tanh \approx 1\) and the frequency depends on the width alone.

Example 3.1 · the pint

With \(R = 0.0391\) m and \(h = 0.125\) m, \(\xi_{11}h/R = 5.886\) and \(\tanh 5.886 = 0.99998\): the depth plays no part. Then \(\omega_{11}^2 = 9.81 \times 1.8412/0.0391 = 461.9\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), \(\omega_{11} = 21.49\ \mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(f_{11} = \omega_{11}/2\pi = 3.421\ \mathrm{Hz}\), one swing every 0.292 s. Check of units: \(g/R\) is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}/m = s^{-2}}\), as \(\omega^2\) must be.

The surface of this mode is \(\eta \propto J_1(kr)\cos\theta\): it rises at the front wall (\(\theta = 0\)), falls by as much at the back wall (\(\theta = \pi\)) and stays at rest along the line across the glass. We call its height at the wall \(q(t)\), so that at the wall \(\eta = q\cos\theta\). A steady push \(a\) along the road tips the surface; in the first mode it gives the wall height \(q = -\tfrac{2R}{\xi_{11}^2 - 1}\,\tfrac{a}{g} = -0.837\,R\,\tfrac{a}{g}\) (Fig. 12 note, eq. (17); the higher modes carry the rest of the exact tilt \(-Ra/g\)). We shall need this in section 7.

04How fast a real swing dies

Viscosity matters only in a thin skin of stout along the glass, and that skin sets how long the pint rings.

A liquid oscillating at \(\omega\) beside a wall at rest is brought to rest only within a layer of thickness \(\delta_s = \sqrt{2\nu/\omega}\), the Stokes layer, where \(\nu\) is the kinematic viscosity. With water’s \(\nu = 1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), which the site uses for the stout, and \(\omega_{11} = 21.49\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), the layer is 0.31 mm thick. All the friction of the swing happens there. The energy it takes per swing, compared with the energy of the swing, gives the logarithmic decrement \(\delta\), the natural logarithm of the ratio of two successive swing heights. For the first mode of a deep cylinder, laminar boundary-layer theory gives [1, eq. (3)]

\[ \delta = K\,\nu^{1/2}\,R^{-3/4}\,g^{-1/4} , \qquad K = 3.52 , \tag{6} \]

and experiments on cylinders of many sizes and liquids found the same law with \(K = 5.23\) [1, eq. (13)], half as much again, which the authors attribute to effects the laminar theory leaves out. The damping ratio is \(\zeta = \delta/2\pi\).

Example 4.1 · how long the pint rings

\(\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4} = 10^{-3} \times 11.37 \times 0.565 = 0.00643\) (dimensionless: \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1/2}\cdot m^{-3/4}\cdot m^{-1/4}s^{1/2}}\)). So \(\delta = 0.0226\) and \(\zeta = 0.0036\) by theory, \(\delta = 0.0336\) and \(\zeta = 0.0053\) by experiment. In section 5 we show that the swing’s height falls by the factor \(e\) in the time \(\tau = 1/(\zeta\omega_{11})\): 12.9 s by theory and 8.7 s by experiment, that is 44 or 30 swings. This is a clean liquid in a smooth glass; a real head of foam damps more (the Fig. 12 note estimates four to seven times more), but still over seconds, not a fraction of one.

05One swing in phase space

Two numbers tell where a swing is: how far it is out, and how fast it is moving. Plotted against each other they turn the swing into a path.

Once the road is flat again the first mode swings freely, and with a small damping it obeys the equation of a damped oscillator,

\[ \ddot q + 2\zeta\omega_{11}\,\dot q + \omega_{11}^2\,q = 0 . \tag{7} \]

We draw its state as the point \((q, \dot q/\omega_{11})\), the phase space of the swing. Dividing the rate by \(\omega_{11}\) gives both axes the unit of a length, and it makes an undamped swing a circle: with \(\zeta = 0\), \(q = A\cos\omega_{11}t\) and \(\dot q/\omega_{11} = -A\sin\omega_{11}t\), so \(q^2 + (\dot q/\omega_{11})^2 = A^2\). The radius of the circle is the swing’s amplitude, and the point goes round once per swing.

With damping, multiply (7) by \(\dot q\). The first and third terms are the time derivative of \(\tfrac12(\dot q^2 + \omega_{11}^2q^2)\), which is proportional to the swing’s energy \(E\):

\[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\tfrac12\big(\dot q^2 + \omega_{11}^2 q^2\big) = -2\zeta\omega_{11}\,\dot q^2 . \tag{8} \]

The right side is never positive: the energy only drains. Call the bracket on the left \(E = \tfrac12(\dot q^2 + \omega_{11}^2q^2)\). Over one swing of a nearly free oscillator the two parts of \(E\) are equal on average, so \(\dot q^2\) averages \(E\), and (8) becomes \(\mathrm dE/\mathrm dt = -2\zeta\omega_{11}E\) on average: \(E \propto e^{-2\zeta\omega_{11}t}\). The amplitude, the square root of the energy, falls as \(e^{-\zeta\omega_{11}t}\). The exact solution of (7) says the same: \(q = A e^{-\zeta\omega_{11}t}\cos(\omega_d t + \varphi)\) with \(\omega_d = \omega_{11}\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\).

Key result · the spiral and its time

In the plane \((q, \dot q/\omega_{11})\) a free swing is a circle when undamped and an inward spiral when damped. Its radius, the amplitude, falls by the factor \(e\) in the time \(\tau = 1/(\zeta\omega_{11})\); from one swing to the next it falls by \(e^{-2\pi\zeta}\) (for small \(\zeta\)).

The second figure of the page is this plane for the stored ride. A real pint after a bump would draw a tight spiral of some thirty turns. Reading \(\tau\) off the stored data gives \(\zeta = 1/(\omega_{11}\tau)\), and that is how section 7 measures the stored rides.

06Reading the mode from stored surfaces

The file holds heights at 64 points around the wall, 30 times a second, rounded to 0.2 mm. From these we want \(q\) and \(\dot q\) without adding errors of our own.

The height \(q\). Write the stored height around the outer ring as a Fourier series in the angle, \(\eta(\theta) = c_0 + \sum_m (a_m\cos m\theta + b_m\sin m\theta)\). The first mode is the term \(a_1\cos\theta\), so \(q = a_1\). On the 64 equally spaced angles \(\theta_j = 2\pi j/64\) the cosines are orthogonal: \(\sum_j\cos\theta_j\cos m\theta_j = 32\) for \(m = 1\) and 0 for every other \(m\) the grid can hold. Therefore

\[ q = \frac{2}{64}\sum_{j=0}^{63}\eta(R, \theta_j)\cos\theta_j \tag{9} \]

picks out \(a_1\) exactly. A lift of the whole surface, \(c_0\), drops out because \(\sum_j\cos\theta_j = 0\), and so do the sideways swing \(b_1\) and every higher term. The lean the Readings show, front height minus back height, is \(\Delta\eta = \eta(0) - \eta(\pi) = 2(a_1 + a_3 + a_5 + \dots)\): equal to \(2q\) for a pure first mode, but also moved by every odd harmonic, and by any error at just those two points.

The rounding. Each stored height is the true one rounded to the nearest 0.2 mm, an error spread evenly between \(-0.1\) and \(+0.1\) mm, with a root mean square of \(0.2/\sqrt{12} = 0.058\) mm. The lean, a difference of two rounded heights, can be off by up to 0.2 mm; over all 1,803 stored frames the stored lean, taken by Kaggle before rounding, and the lean of the rounded grid differ by at most 0.19 mm. In (9) the 64 errors are averaged: if they are independent, the error of \(q\) is \((2/64)\sqrt{32}\times0.058 = 0.010\) mm.

The rate \(\dot q\) ★. The file has no rates, only frames \(h = 1/30\) s apart. The usual estimate, the central difference \((q_{n+1} - q_{n-1})/2h\), is poor here: with fewer than nine frames per swing it reads the rate of a swing at \(\omega_{11}\) too low by the factor \(\sin(\omega_{11}h)/(\omega_{11}h)\). To see why, take a pure swing, \(q_n = A\cos(\omega_{11}nh + \varphi)\). The identity \(\cos(x + y) - \cos(x - y) = -2\sin x\sin y\) gives

\[ q_{n+1} - q_{n-1} = -2A\sin(\omega_{11}nh + \varphi)\,\sin\omega_{11}h = \frac{2\sin\omega_{11}h}{\omega_{11}}\;\dot q(t_n) . \tag{10} \]

So the page divides by \(2\sin\omega_{11}h\) instead of \(2\omega_{11}h\):

\[ p_n = \frac{q_{n+1} - q_{n-1}}{2\sin\omega_{11}h} , \tag{11} \]

which equals \(\dot q/\omega_{11}\) exactly for a swing at \(\omega_{11}\), so a free undamped swing would draw a true circle. With \(\omega_{11}h = 0.717\) the plain central difference would have been 8 % low (\(\sin 0.717/0.717 = 0.917\)). A swing at another frequency is read with an error: the second mode, at 5.82 Hz, comes out 16 % short in \(\dot q/\omega_{11}\), the third, at 7.37 Hz, 29 % short. Only the first mode is drawn true.

Key result · reading the swing from stored frames

The first mode’s height is the cos θ part of the outer ring, (9), which ignores lifts of the whole surface and errors at single points. Its scaled rate is (11). The stored frames, 30 a second, are well above twice the first three mode frequencies (3.42, 5.82 and 7.37 Hz), so no mode is mistaken for a slower one.

07What the stored rides show

After the last bump, at 9 m, the road is flat and the cart rolls at a steady speed. The stout should now ring freely for several seconds. We measure what the stored rides do instead.

The measurement (tools/fig12/kaggle_decay.py, run on the stored file) takes \(q\) from (9) for every frame, keeps only its part between 2.5 and 4.5 Hz, finds its envelope, and fits a straight line to the logarithm of the envelope over 1.5 s from its peak after the last bump. By section 5 the slope of that line is \(-1/\tau\).

The pace is right. The strongest swing in the band is at 3.433 Hz in the 1.0 m/s ride and 3.393 Hz in the 1.5 m/s ride, within 1 % of the 3.421 Hz of (5). (The 0.5 m/s ride swings only 0.4 mm, too little for a clean peak.)

The swing dies far too fast. The fitted times are 0.76, 0.41 and 0.32 s at 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 m/s.

Example 7.1 · the damping of the stored stout

At 1.5 m/s, \(\tau = 0.32\) s, so \(\zeta = 1/(\omega_{11}\tau) = 1/(21.49 \times 0.32) = 0.145\). The other two rides give 0.061 and 0.114. Against the 0.0053 of experiment (section 4) this is 12 to 27 times too much; against the 0.0036 of theory, 17 to 40 times. The stored stout loses most of its swing within one or two swings; a real pint would take about thirty.

Nothing physical in (1) removes energy: it has no viscosity and no wall friction. The loss must therefore come from the numerical method, the transfers between particles and grid or the pressure solution, or from how the surface is built from the particles. APIC was designed to avoid the strong dissipation of plain particle-in-cell transfers [3, abstract], so a damping this large points at a fault to be found in the notebook rather than at the method itself; that is an inference, to be tested by the run proposed in section 10.

The lean at the wall drifts. Over the last 5 s of the 1.5 m/s ride, at a steady speed on a flat road, the stored lean averages 6.36 mm while \(q\) from (9) averages 0.89 mm, so \(2q = 1.8\) mm.

Example 7.2 · what push a 6 mm lean would need

A pure first mode with \(\Delta\eta = 2q = 6.36\) mm has \(q = 3.18\) mm. By section 3 a steady push \(a\) gives \(|q| = 0.837\,R\,a/g\), so \(a = 3.18\times10^{-3} \times 9.81/(0.837 \times 0.0391) = 0.95\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). The stored cart speed stays within 0.03 m/s of 1.5 m/s over the whole ride, so its mean acceleration over those 5 s is at most a few hundredths of that. The drift is not the stout answering a push.

Two further facts point at the reconstruction of the surface near the wall. At rest, at the start of every ride, the stored outer ring sits about 7 mm above the axis (133 mm against 126 mm in the 0.5 m/s ride), although a liquid at rest is level. And the notebook builds the surface within about 5 mm of the wall not from particles but by extending the heights of columns further in, straight out along the radius, because the columns cut by the wall hold too few particles. Both are inferences about the cause; the particles that would settle it were not stored.

08Playing stored data back

A page that plays computed data has three rules: show only what was computed, never jump, and say plainly when it shows something else.

Only stored frames. At ride time \(t\) the page shows frame \(n = \mathrm{round}(t f_s)\), \(f_s = 30\) per second, and never a blend of two frames: a blend of two stored surfaces is not a surface anybody computed. The speed control likewise stops only at speeds that exist: 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 m/s are computed on Kaggle (filled dots), 2.0 and 2.5 m/s are not (hollow dots).

No jumps. The playback rate \(r\), ride seconds per second of the clock, changes only smoothly. A governor \(g\) follows its goal with a first-order lag of time constant \(\tau_g = 0.45\) s, \(\dot g = (r_\text{goal} - g)/\tau_g\), which the page steps exactly from frame to frame, \(g \to g + (r_\text{goal} - g)(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau_g})\). The goal is \(1 - 0.8c\), where \(c\) rises smoothly from 0 to 1 as the speed control comes near the edge of the computed speeds. Near the end of the computed time a second law caps the rate:

\[ r \le \sqrt{\ell/\tau_e} , \qquad \tau_e = 3\ \mathrm s , \tag{12} \]

where \(\ell\) is the ride time left. While (12) holds with equality, \(\mathrm d\ell/\mathrm dt_\text{clock} = -\sqrt{\ell/\tau_e}\). Separating the variables, \(\mathrm d\ell/\sqrt\ell = -\mathrm dt_\text{clock}/\sqrt{\tau_e}\), and integrating from \(\ell_0 = \tau_e\) gives \(\sqrt\ell = \sqrt{\tau_e} - t_\text{clock}/(2\sqrt{\tau_e})\), so

\[ r = \sqrt{\ell/\tau_e} = 1 - \frac{t_\text{clock}}{2\tau_e} . \tag{13} \]
Key result · stopping exactly at the end

Under (12) the picture slows down at a constant rate, from real time to rest in \(2\tau_e = 6\) s of the clock while it plays the last 3 s of the ride, and it reaches rest exactly at the last stored frame. A law with a softer approach, such as \(r \propto \ell\), would never arrive: the time left would only halve and halve again.

Saying what is shown. On a hollow stop the page shows a live preview computed in your browser by the site’s own coarse solver (the 4 mm grid of Fig. 12 with about 25,700 particles and half the glass mirrored; its free swing is 3.21 Hz, 6 % slow). It runs in a background thread at about a fifth of real time, so the ride slows to wait for its frames. A label over the figure says that it is a preview and not Kaggle’s result. Only when no stored frame is left and the rate has eased to zero does the notice fade in that shows the way new data would take, from this page through the link kaggle-bridge to Kaggle’s graphics card and back; that link is not switched on yet, and the notice says so.

09What the picture shows

The glass, the cart and the road are drawn by the same code as Fig. 12 (stout-view.js), through a camera that looks down at 16° and rides with the cart. The surface of the stout is the stored grid: each of the 17 × 64 nodes is drawn at its stored height exactly, and between nodes the height is interpolated along straight lines. The cream head is a band 12 mm thick laid on that surface: drawing, not data. The cart’s position, speed and heights are the stored ones of the frame on screen. The phase space below the cart is the plane of section 5 with \(q\) from (9) and \(\dot q/\omega_{11}\) from (11): the whole ride pale, the last second dark, the frame on screen as a dot, and a dashed circle with the present amplitude, the path an undamped swing would keep to. The data panel (the Data tab) lists every one of these numbers with three proofs: the stored value, the value worked out again from stored inputs, and the colour read back from the picture.

10Limits, and the next model

The stored rides have the right pace and the wrong damping, and their lean at the wall is not trustworthy beyond about 1 mm. Two corrections are needed before more rides are worth computing.

First, the method’s own losses must be found and removed. The test is the one this note used, made clean: tip the stout in a glass at rest and let it go, with no cart and no road, and measure \(\zeta\) from the decay of \(q\). A correct inviscid solver should give a damping ratio well under 0.005, falling as the grid is refined. Second, the real wall friction must then be put back. The Stokes layer, 0.31 mm, is five times thinner than a 1.5 mm cell, so no affordable grid resolves it; its effect has to be added as a model, a drag on the liquid next to the wall chosen so that (6) is reproduced. The surface near the wall should be rebuilt from the particles there, not extended from the columns further in, and \(q\) from (9) is the safer reading to store and show.

Beyond that lie the parts no model here contains: the foam of the head, which damps more than the wall does, and the nitrogen bubbles that rise through a fresh pint.
