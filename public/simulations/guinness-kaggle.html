Lecture note · Fig. 12 computed on Kaggle · fluid dynamics · data

A full pint over the bumps

Numa Koudsie · for readers meeting the subject for the first time, from school to the second year of university; ★ marks the heavier mathematics, whose results are restated without it

Abstract. A pint of stout in its tulip glass rides a one-wheel cart over bumps. The rides on this page were computed ahead of time on a graphics card rented through Kaggle, with a million particles of stout, the head of foam lying on them and the cart they push against, and the page only plays the stored pictures back. In this chapter we build that model from the ground up. We measure the pint, find the rhythm of its surface (3.421 Hz), and work out how three thin layers the computer's grid cannot see, the stout's boundary layer on the glass, the stiff skin under its surface and the head of foam, take the swing's energy away: the first two together give a damping ratio of 0.011, the head adds a yield stress of 5.4 Pa and a rule for when it breaks over the rim. We derive how the cart and the stout push on each other, follow the computer through one time step, and then tell how a test of a pint standing still caught two faults that made a resting pint stir itself, a lesson in energy bookkeeping. The chapter ends with the way the page plays the stored data, the method by which every result is read from it, what the stored run shows and the limits of the model. In that run the pint at rest stays within 0.54 mm of level; without its head the stout rings at 3.331 Hz, 2.6 % below theory; the head stops a free swing within one period (damping ratio 0.47); five bumps in step lean the surface by no more than 9.8 mm; and a kerb at 1.5 m/s sends 4.1 mL of head and 0.008 g of stout over the rim.

01A full pint on a bumpy floor

Carry a full pint across a crowded room and your hand learns a piece of fluid mechanics before your head does.

Every step jolts the glass a little. The stout lags behind the glass, piles up against one side, runs back to the other, and keeps rocking a few times a second after the jolt has passed. If your steps happen to fall in time with that rocking, each one adds to it, and after four or five steps the stout climbs the wall. Experienced bar staff walk smoothly for this reason. A pint of stout has one more thing going for it: its head of foam. The head lies on the stout like a lid of whipped cream, a couple of centimetres thick, it rubs on the glass, and it is stiff enough to hold its shape when you tilt the glass gently. It is also the first thing to go over the rim, because it stands highest.

02The pint, measured

Before we write any equation we need the numbers: the shape of the glass, how heavy and how sticky the stout is, and what the head is made of.

The glass. The Guinness tulip widens from a narrow foot to a belly and narrows again a little towards the rim. The page uses its inner radius \(R(z)\) at every height \(z\) above the inner floor, in steps of 0.5 mm, taken from a section drawing of the glass scaled to its catalogue size, 160 mm tall and 85 mm wide. The inner floor is 32.6 mm across (\(R = 16.3\) mm), the widest point is \(R = 39.3\) mm at \(z = 116\) mm, and the rim is at \(z = 147.5\) mm. We fill the glass with stout to the depth \(h = 125\) mm, where the radius is \(R = 39.1\) mm; in the top 20 mm below that level the radius changes by only 0.29 mm. The stout fills 426.6 mL and weighs 429.6 g. On top lies the head, 19 mm thick, so its top stands at 144 mm, only 3.5 mm below the rim (Figure 1).

The stout. Benilov, Cummins and Lee measured the liquid of Guinness at 6 °C, the temperature at which it is served: density \(\rho = 1007\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and viscosity \(\mu = 2.06\ \mathrm{mPa\,s}\) [1, p. 1]. Watamura and co-workers found 1006 kg/m³ and 2.03 to 2.23 mPa s on degassed samples near 5 °C [2, p. 2 and Supplement], the same within their spread. A liquid's resistance to shear is measured by its viscosity \(\mu\); how quickly a disturbance in its velocity spreads by friction is measured by the kinematic viscosity,

\[ \nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho} = \frac{2.06\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}}{1007\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}} = 2.05\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}, \]

about twice the value for water. A freshly poured pint is cloudy with small bubbles, but they rise into the head within about two minutes (Benilov and co-workers measured a settling time of about 120 s [1, p. 2]), so we treat the settled stout as a plain liquid.

The head. The head is a foam of the stout's own bubbles. Guinness specifies a head 18 to 20 mm thick, and three pours measured by KegWorks two minutes after pouring gave 17.5, 20.6 and 22.2 mm [4]; we take 19 mm. Three more numbers describe the foam. The first is the size of its bubbles. Watamura and co-workers measured the stout's bubbles at a mean diameter of 61 µm [2, p. 2]; what matters for a foam is their Sauter radius \(R_{32}\), the ratio \(\sum r^3/\sum r^2\) over all bubbles, three times their total volume divided by their total surface, which the project's research notes derive from that distribution as \(R_{32} = 34\ \mu\mathrm m\). The second is the liquid fraction, the share of the foam's volume that is liquid: 0.27, a value derived in the same notes from how a 19 mm column of such bubbles drains, not a measurement. The foam's density follows from it, since the gas weighs almost nothing: \(\rho_f = 0.27\rho = 272\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\). The third is the surface of the stout itself. Its surface tension is \(\gamma = 44.2\ \mathrm{mN/m}\), and because proteins sit on it, the surface also resists being stretched, with a dilatational elasticity \(E_\infty = 18.7\ \mathrm{mN/m}\); Völp and Willenbacher measured both on degassed Guinness at 20 °C [3, Table 2].

03The rhythm of the surface ★

For small swings the stout's rocking is a sum of standing waves, the modes of the glass. The slowest is the one that tips the surface as a whole, and we can calculate its frequency and how a push of the glass drives it.

Everything the stout does is contained in the Navier–Stokes equations of an incompressible liquid. We write them in the frame of the glass, as a passenger on the cart would see the stout, because the walls are then at rest (section 6 explains what that frame adds). Let \(\mathbf u\) be the velocity of the stout relative to the glass, \(p\) its pressure, \(\mathbf g\) gravity and \(\mathbf a_g\) the acceleration of the glass. Then

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = -\frac{\nabla p}{\rho} + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \mathbf g - \mathbf a_g , \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0 . \tag{1} \]

The first equation is Newton's second law for a small parcel of stout, per unit mass: its acceleration (the left side) equals the push of the pressure, the friction of viscosity, gravity, and the inertial force of the accelerating frame. The second says that the stout cannot be compressed: as much flows out of any small volume as flows in. For small swings both the viscous term and the term \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\) are small. Viscosity matters only in thin layers at the glass and under the surface, which section 4 treats on their own, and \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\) is of second order in the size of the swing. We drop both for now.

3.1The first mode

Near the surface the tulip is almost a cylinder, and that is where the swing lives, because its flow fades with depth over \(1/k = 21\) mm (see (3) below). We therefore take an upright cylinder of the radius at the surface, \(R = 39.1\) mm, filled to the depth \(h = 125\) mm, and a glass held still. A liquid without viscosity that starts from rest stays free of rotation (Kelvin's circulation theorem), so its velocity is the gradient of a potential, \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), and incompressibility, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0\), makes the potential obey Laplace's equation, \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\). In cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\), with \(z\) up from the floor and \(\theta = 0\) pointing forward along the road, we look for a standing wave that tips the surface once around the glass:

\[ \phi = \cos\omega t\;\cos\theta\;J_1(kr)\,\cosh kz . \tag{2} \]

Here \(J_1\) is the Bessel function of order one; it solves the radial part of Laplace's equation for a \(\cos\theta\) dependence, and \(\cosh kz\) the vertical part, with the same constant \(k\). The hyperbolic cosine makes \(\partial\phi/\partial z = 0\) at the floor, \(z = 0\): no flow through the bottom. The wall allows no flow through it either, \(\partial\phi/\partial r = 0\) at \(r = R\), so

\[ J_1'(kR) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad kR = \xi = 1.8412 , \tag{3} \]

the first zero of \(J_1'\). At the free surface two conditions hold; for small swings we apply them at the rest height \(z = h\) instead of at the moving surface \(z = h + \eta\), which changes them only at second order in the height \(\eta\) of the wave. The surface moves with the liquid, \(\partial\eta/\partial t = \partial\phi/\partial z\), and the pressure there is the air's, which for small motions is Bernoulli's law \(\partial\phi/\partial t + g\eta = 0\). Differentiating the second in time and using the first eliminates \(\eta\):

\[ \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2} + g\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z} = 0 \quad\text{at } z = h . \tag{4} \]

Putting (2) into (4) gives \(-\omega^2\cosh kh + gk\sinh kh = 0\), that is,

\[ \omega_{11}^2 = \frac{g\,\xi}{R}\,\tanh\frac{\xi h}{R} . \tag{5} \]
Key result · the first sloshing frequency

An upright cylinder of radius \(R\) filled to depth \(h\) rocks its surface at \(\omega_{11}^2 = (g\xi/R)\tanh(\xi h/R)\), \(\xi = 1.8412\). For a glass deeper than its radius, \(\tanh \approx 1\) and the frequency depends on the width alone.

Example 3.1 · The pint

With \(R = 0.0391\) m and \(h = 0.125\) m, \(\xi h/R = 5.886\) and \(\tanh 5.886 = 0.99998\): the depth plays no part. Then \(\omega_{11}^2 = 9.81 \times 1.8412/0.0391 = 461.9\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), \(\omega_{11} = 21.49\ \mathrm{rad/s}\) and \(f_{11} = \omega_{11}/2\pi = 3.421\ \mathrm{Hz}\), one swing every 0.292 s. Check of units: \(g/R\) is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}/m = s^{-2}}\), as \(\omega^2\) must be. The next two modes with the same \(\cos\theta\) shape have \(kR = 5.331\) and \(8.536\) and rock at 5.821 and 7.365 Hz.

The surface of this mode is \(\eta \propto J_1(kr)\cos\theta\): it rises at the front wall (\(\theta = 0\)), falls by as much at the back wall (\(\theta = \pi\)) and stays at rest along the line across the glass. We call its height at the wall \(q(t)\), so that at the wall \(\eta = q\cos\theta\).

3.2How the glass drives the swing ★

Now let the glass accelerate, with \(a(t)\) forward along the road and \(a_z(t)\) upward, so that \(\mathbf a_g = (a, 0, a_z)\). The inertial force \(-\mathbf a_g\) of (1), per unit mass, has a potential, like gravity, so Bernoulli's law at the surface picks up two terms:

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + (g + a_z)\,\eta + a\,r\cos\theta = 0 \quad\text{at } z = h , \]

because \(x = r\cos\theta\) is the distance along the road. The push enters through \(r\cos\theta\), and we need to know how much of it the first mode feels. On the interval \(0 \le r \le R\) the functions \(J_1(kr)\) of the different modes (the different \(k\) with \(J_1'(kR) = 0\)) are orthogonal with the weight \(r\), so the share of the first mode in the function \(r\) is the coefficient

\[ c_1 = \frac{\int_0^R r\cdot J_1(kr)\,r\,\mathrm dr}{\int_0^R J_1(kr)^2\,r\,\mathrm dr} = \frac{R^3J_1(\xi)/\xi^2}{\tfrac12R^2\left(1 - 1/\xi^2\right)J_1(\xi)^2} = \frac{2R}{(\xi^2 - 1)\,J_1(\xi)} . \]

The numerator uses \(\int_0^R r^2J_1(kr)\,\mathrm dr = R^3J_2(\xi)/\xi\) and \(J_2(\xi) = J_1(\xi)/\xi\), which follows from \(J_1'(\xi) = 0\); the denominator is the standard integral of \(J_1^2\) for a function whose slope vanishes at \(r = R\). At the wall the first mode carries the height \(c_1J_1(\xi) = 2R/(\xi^2 - 1) = 0.837\,R\) of the straight line \(r\). Now write the first mode's part of the surface as \(\eta = q(t)\,J_1(kr)\cos\theta/J_1(\xi)\), so that \(q\) is its height at the wall, and of the potential as \(Q(t)\,J_1(kr)\cos\theta\,\cosh kz/\cosh kh\). The two surface conditions, kinematic and Bernoulli, become for this mode

\[ \frac{\dot q}{J_1(\xi)} = k\tanh kh\;Q, \qquad \dot Q + (g + a_z)\,\frac{q}{J_1(\xi)} + a\,c_1 = 0 . \]

Differentiating the first in time and putting in the second, with \(\omega_{11}^2 = gk\tanh kh\) from (5), gives \(\ddot q = -k\tanh kh\,\big[(g + a_z)\,q + a\,c_1J_1(\xi)\big]\), the equation of the driven swing.

Key result · the driven swing \[ \ddot q + \omega_{11}^2\Big(1 + \frac{a_z}{g}\Big)\,q = -\beta\,a(t), \qquad \beta = \frac{2\xi\tanh(\xi h/R)}{\xi^2 - 1} = 1.541 . \tag{6} \]

A forward acceleration lowers the front of the surface and raises the back. A vertical acceleration of the glass changes the restoring force, as if gravity were \(g + a_z\). This is the equation (17) of the Fig. 12 note [14, section 4].

Example 3.2 · A steady push and a sudden one

A steady push tilts the whole surface at right angles to the effective gravity, and the wall rises by \(Ra/g\). To lift the wall by 3.5 mm, enough to bring the top of the head to the rim, takes \(a = 9.81 \times 3.5/39.1 = 0.88\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\); to lift the stout itself 22.5 mm to the rim takes \(5.65\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). A sudden change of speed \(\Delta v\), quicker than a swing, is more dangerous. By (6) it gives \(\dot q\) the jump \(-\beta\Delta v\), so the wall swings with the amplitude \(\beta\Delta v/\omega_{11} = (\omega_{11}/g)\,(2R/(\xi^2 - 1))\,\Delta v\). For \(\Delta v = 0.1\ \mathrm{m/s}\): \((21.49/9.81) \times 0.837 \times 0.0391 \times 0.1 = 7.2\ \mathrm{mm}\).

One more number makes the five-bump road of section 6 dangerous. Its bumps are 0.29 m apart; at 1.0 m/s they come 3.448 times a second, 0.8 % faster than \(f_{11}\). Each bump then pushes the swing in step with the last, as a child on a swing is pushed once per swing.

04Three thin layers

Viscosity acts everywhere in the stout, but its friction matters only in thin layers where the stout is forced to move differently from its neighbours: along the glass, and just under the surface.

4.1The Stokes layer on the glass ★

Picture the stout sliding to and fro along the glass a few times a second. A short distance from the glass it slides freely with the swing's velocity, which we write \(U\cos\omega t\). At the glass itself it must be at rest, because a viscous liquid sticks to a solid (the no-slip condition). In between lies a thin layer in which the velocity \(u(y, t)\) changes from zero at the wall, \(y = 0\), to \(U\cos\omega t\) outside. The pressure gradient that drives the outer flow, \(-\partial p/\partial x = \rho\,\partial(U\cos\omega t)/\partial t\), is the same across the thin layer, so the difference \(u' = u - U\cos\omega t\) obeys the diffusion equation \(\partial u'/\partial t = \nu\,\partial^2 u'/\partial y^2\), with \(u' = -U\cos\omega t\) at the wall and \(u' \to 0\) far from it. Try \(u' = \mathrm{Re}\,[\hat u\, e^{i\omega t - (1 + i)y/\delta}]\), with \(i\) the imaginary unit: substituting gives \(i\omega = \nu(1 + i)^2/\delta^2 = 2i\nu/\delta^2\), so \(\delta = \sqrt{2\nu/\omega}\), and the wall condition fixes \(\hat u = -U\). The velocity in the layer is

\[ u(y, t) = U\left[\cos\omega t - e^{-y/\delta}\cos\!\Big(\omega t - \frac{y}{\delta}\Big)\right], \qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{\omega}} . \tag{7} \]

The layer is called the Stokes layer, and \(\delta\) its thickness: within about \(2\delta\) the velocity reaches the outer value, and every half swing the slide reverses before friction can reach further into the liquid (Figure 2). With the stout's \(\nu\) and the frequency the code uses, \(\omega = 2\pi \times 3.42\ \mathrm{Hz}\), the layer is \(\delta = 0.44\) mm thick, a third of one grid cell.

The glass feels the stress \(\tau_w = \mu\,\partial u/\partial y\) at \(y = 0\). Differentiating (7) and using \(\mu/\delta = \rho\nu/\delta = \rho\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\),

\[ \tau_w = \rho\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\;U\big(\cos\omega t - \sin\omega t\big) . \tag{8} \]

The stress has two parts. The first is in step with the sliding velocity \(U\cos\omega t\) and always opposes it: it takes energy from the swing, on average \(\langle\rho\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,U^2\cos^2\omega t\rangle = \tfrac12\rho\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,U^2\) per unit area of wall and per second. The second is in step with the acceleration; over a full swing it does no work, and its only effect is to make the stout seem slightly heavier, which lowers the frequency by the fraction \(\zeta_\text{wall}\) of 4.2 below, half a per cent [7, eq. (18)]. Integrating the viscous dissipation \(\mu(\partial u/\partial y)^2\) through the whole layer gives the same mean power, \(\tfrac12\rho\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,U^2\), as it must.

The computer's grid, with cells of 1.5 mm, cannot hold a layer of 0.44 mm, so the code lets the stout slide along the glass and puts the friction back by hand. On the glass's area in each cell cut by the glass it applies the stress \(\rho\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,u\), with \(u\) the sliding velocity on the grid. That is exactly the in-phase part of (8): for a swing at \(\omega\) it removes the same energy as the true layer.

Example 4.1 · The numbers of the layer

\(\omega = 2\pi \times 3.42 = 21.49\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), \(\delta = \sqrt{2 \times 2.05\times10^{-6}/21.49} = 0.436\ \mathrm{mm}\), and \(\sqrt{\nu\omega/2} = 4.69\ \mathrm{mm/s}\). Stout sliding at 10 mm/s along the glass therefore feels \(1007 \times 0.00469 \times 0.01 = 0.047\ \mathrm{Pa}\), a gentle drag that matters only because it acts on every swing.

4.2How fast the wall damps the swing ★

To turn the drag into a rate we compare the energy lost per second with the energy of the swing. Take the mode (2) with amplitude \(B\) in \(\phi\). Its energy \(E\) is its largest kinetic energy, \(\tfrac12\rho\int|\nabla\phi|^2\,\mathrm dV\). By the divergence theorem this volume integral equals the surface integral \(\tfrac12\rho\oint\phi\,\partial\phi/\partial n\,\mathrm dS\), and only the free surface contributes, because \(\partial\phi/\partial n = 0\) on the wall and the floor:

\[ E = \tfrac12\rho\,B^2\,k\cosh kh\,\sinh kh\;\pi\!\int_0^R J_1(kr)^2\,r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\pi\rho B^2}{4}\,k\cosh kh\,\sinh kh\;R^2\Big(1 - \frac1{\xi^2}\Big)J_1(\xi)^2 . \]

At the wall the stout slides with \(u_\theta = (1/R)\,\partial\phi/\partial\theta\) and \(u_z = \partial\phi/\partial z\), so the integral of the squared sliding velocity over the wetted wall is

\[ \int_{\text{wall}}\!\big(u_\theta^2 + u_z^2\big)\,\mathrm dS = \pi R\,B^2J_1(\xi)^2\int_0^h\Big(\frac{\cosh^2kz}{R^2} + k^2\sinh^2kz\Big)\mathrm dz . \]

The mean power lost is \(\Pi = \tfrac12\rho\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\) times this integral. For a deep glass, \(kh = 5.9\), both \(\cosh^2 kz\) and \(\sinh^2 kz\) are close to \(e^{2kz}/4\) wherever they matter, so the \(z\)-integral is \(e^{2kh}(1/R^2 + k^2)/(8k)\) and \(\cosh kh\sinh kh = e^{2kh}/4\). The energy decays as \(E \propto e^{-2\sigma t}\) and the amplitude as \(e^{-\sigma t}\), with \(\sigma = \Pi/2E\), because \(\mathrm dE/\mathrm dt = -\Pi\). Dividing, everything but the geometry cancels:

\[ \sigma_\text{wall} = \sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\;\frac{\xi^2 + 1}{2R\,(\xi^2 - 1)}, \qquad \zeta_\text{wall} = \frac{\sigma_\text{wall}}{\omega} = \frac{\delta}{4R}\,\frac{\xi^2 + 1}{\xi^2 - 1} . \tag{9} \]

The damping ratio \(\zeta\) is the decay rate measured in units of the frequency: in one radian of the swing the amplitude falls by the fraction \(\zeta\). For the pint, \(\sigma_\text{wall} = 0.110\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(\zeta_\text{wall} = 0.0051\); the exact integrals at the true depth give the same to three digits. This is the classical laminar result. Bongarzone and Gallaire print it with its three parts, side wall, floor and bulk [7, eq. (17)], and our side-wall factor \((\xi^2 + 1)/(2(\xi^2 - 1)) = 0.918\) is theirs for a mode with one wave around the glass.

A useful check is the form engineers use. With \(\omega^2 = g\xi/R\) for a deep glass, (9) becomes \(\zeta_\text{wall} = 0.5575\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\), or, as a logarithmic decrement (the natural logarithm of the ratio of two successive swing heights, \(\Lambda = 2\pi\zeta\)), \(\Lambda = K\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\) with \(K = 3.50\). Stephens, Leonard and Perry quote Miles's laminar theory as \(K = 3.52\) [5, eqs. (2)–(3)], the same within rounding. Their own experiments, in tanks from 3 inches to 10 feet across, gave \(K = 5.23\), 50 % more [5, eq. (13)], and the handbook of Silveira and Abramson repeats both values [6, eq. (4.10b)]. Real walls and contact lines add friction that the laminar theory leaves out; with \(K = 5.23\) the pint's wall damping would be \(\zeta = 0.0077\).

4.3The skin under the surface, and the bulk

A clean water surface slides freely. The surface of beer does not: the proteins on it resist stretching (that is what \(E_\infty\) measures), and under a head of foam the surface is held from above. Either way the surface behaves like a lid, and just under it the stout forms a second Stokes layer, like the one on the glass, in which the horizontal velocity changes from the lid's to the swing's. The same calculation as in 4.2, now with the horizontal velocity at \(z = h\), gives

\[ \sigma_\text{skin} = \sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\;\frac{k}{2}\coth kh, \qquad \zeta_\text{skin} = 0.0051 , \tag{10} \]

where we used \(\int|\nabla_h\phi|^2\,\mathrm dA = k^2\int\phi^2\,\mathrm dA\) over the surface (the horizontal Laplacian of \(J_1(kr)\cos\theta\) is \(-k^2\) times the function, and its slope vanishes at the wall). The code applies the same drag \(\rho\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,u\) to the horizontal velocity of the top cells of stout, over one cell's area each. Finally viscosity also acts inside the stout, away from any boundary; for a swing this adds the rate \(2\nu k^2 = 0.009\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) [7, eq. (17)], a damping ratio of 0.0004, which the code obtains by applying viscosity on the grid.

Key result · the damping of the stout without its head

Wall, skin and bulk together give the first mode \(\zeta = 0.0051 + 0.0051 + 0.0004 = 0.0107\). The swing's amplitude falls by the factor \(e\) in \(1/(\zeta\omega_{11}) = 4.4\ \mathrm{s}\) and halves in \(\ln 2/(2\pi\zeta) = 10\) swings. This is what the code's thin-layer model is built to reproduce; the head of foam adds to it.

05The head of foam

A head of foam is a solid that can flow. Press it gently and it springs back; push harder and it gives way.

5.1Why a foam is stiff

Pour marbles into a box and they stop settling when about 64 % of the space is filled: they are jammed. The bubbles of the head fill 73 % of it, more than round bubbles can, so they are squeezed into rounded polyhedra pressed against each other. To shear the foam we must deform these bubbles, which stretches their films and costs surface energy. The natural scale of the resulting stress is the pressure inside a bubble, about \(\gamma/R\). Völp and Willenbacher showed that for foams with stiff, protein-covered surfaces the right scale also contains the surface's elasticity, the generalized Laplace pressure \((\gamma + 2E_\infty)/R_{32}\), and that two laws describe their measurements [3, conclusions]:

\[ p_L = \frac{\gamma + 2E_\infty}{R_{32}}, \qquad G_0 = p_L\,\Phi\,(\Phi - \Phi_c), \qquad \tau_y = 0.25\,p_L\,(\Phi - \Phi_c)^2 . \tag{11} \]

Here \(\Phi = 0.73\) is the gas fraction (one minus the liquid fraction) and \(\Phi_c = 0.635\) the gas fraction at jamming. \(G_0\) is the shear modulus, the foam's stiffness against small shears, and \(\tau_y\) the yield stress, the shear stress beyond which the bubbles slide past each other and the foam flows.

Example 5.1 · The head of the pint

\(p_L = (0.0442 + 2 \times 0.0187)/(34\times10^{-6}) = 2400\ \mathrm{Pa}\). With \(\Phi - \Phi_c = 0.095\): \(G_0 = 2400 \times 0.73 \times 0.095 = 166\ \mathrm{Pa}\) and \(\tau_y = 0.25 \times 2400 \times 0.095^2 = 5.4\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Compare the head's own weight per area, 50.7 Pa (Example 2.1): the head can carry about a tenth of its weight in shear before it flows. That is why it keeps its shape when the glass is moved gently and gives way under a jolt.

5.2How it flows

Once the head flows, its stress grows with the shear rate \(\dot\gamma\), the rate at which neighbouring layers slide past each other (the dot tells it apart from the surface tension \(\gamma\)): \(\tau = \tau_y + \tau_v(\dot\gamma)\). The viscous part comes from the stout squeezed in the films and the corners between bubbles. Tcholakova and co-workers derived it from the flow in those films and corners [8, eq. (54)]:

\[ \frac{\tau_v}{\gamma/R_{32}} = 0.7\,\frac{\mathrm{Ca}^{0.47}\Phi^{5/6}}{(1 - \Phi)^{0.5}} + 8\,\frac{\mathrm{Ca}^{0.7}\Phi^{5/6}}{(1 - \Phi)^{0.15}}, \qquad \mathrm{Ca} = \frac{\mu\dot\gamma R_{32}}{\gamma} . \tag{12} \]

The capillary number Ca compares the viscous stress \(\mu\dot\gamma\) with the bubble's pressure scale \(\gamma/R_{32}\). At \(\dot\gamma = 10\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm{Ca} = 1.6\times10^{-5}\); the films give 7.5 Pa and the corners 4.2 Pa, so \(\tau_v = 11.7\ \mathrm{Pa}\) and \(\tau = 17.1\ \mathrm{Pa}\). Their fits cover gas fractions of 0.80 and above; our head, at 0.73, lies a little beyond that range.

5.3The head as a sheet

In the model the head rides on the stout: in every column of the grid it moves up and down with the stout's surface below it, at the vertical velocity \(w(x, y)\). In a swing neighbouring columns move at different speeds, so the head between them is sheared on vertical planes at the rate \(\dot\gamma = |\nabla w|\), and over its thickness \(h_f\) it resists with the force per unit area of surface \(\nabla\cdot(h_f\,\tau\,\nabla w/|\nabla w|)\). Writing \(\tau/\dot\gamma = \mu_f\), an effective viscosity of the foam that is large for slow shear and small for fast shear, the code solves for the new vertical velocity \(w'\) of the top two cells of stout, which carry the head's forces, in one implicit step:

\[ \rho\,2\Delta x\,\frac{w' - w}{\Delta t} = \nabla\cdot\big(h_f\,\mu_f\,\nabla w'\big) - \kappa\,w' . \tag{13} \]

Here \(\Delta x = 1.5\) mm is the cell size, \(\Delta t\) the time step, \(\rho\,2\Delta x = 3.0\ \mathrm{kg/m^2}\) the mass per area of the two cells, and \(\kappa\) the friction of the head's edge on the glass (5.4 below). Below \(\dot\gamma = 0.05\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) the code holds \(\mu_f\) at its value there, 123 Pa s, so that a head sheared very slowly is practically locked. The equation is solved by conjugate gradients on the columns, and the pressure step then passes the change down into the stout.

A rough balance shows what the yield stress alone can do. The head resists a swing of wavenumber \(k\) with a force per area of order \(h_f\tau_yk\); gravity pushes the swing back with \(\rho g q\). The two are equal at \(q = h_f\tau_yk/(\rho g) = 0.019 \times 5.4 \times 47.1/(1007 \times 9.81) = 0.5\ \mathrm{mm}\). Swings much smaller than half a millimetre at the wall cannot shear the head and stop; larger ones shear it and lose energy on every swing. This is how dry friction behaves, and it stops a motion in a finite time instead of letting it fade for ever. The estimate is an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

5.4The head on the glass

Where the head touches the glass, its bubbles slide on the thin film of stout that wets the glass. Cantat worked out the friction of such sliding bubbles; for stiff surfaces, with her fitted prefactor, the stress on the wall is [9, eq. (49) and Fig. 14]

\[ \tau_\text{edge} = Z\,\frac{\gamma}{R_{32}}\Big[6.82\,\mathrm{Ca}^{2/3} + 0.94\,\frac{l}{r_m}\,\mathrm{Ca}^{1/3}\Big], \qquad \mathrm{Ca} = \frac{\mu v_s}{\gamma}, \tag{14} \]

with \(v_s\) the sliding speed, \(l\) the length of a bubble's contact with the wall and \(r_m\) the radius of the stout's meniscus around it, \(Z = 0.18\) and \(l/r_m = 0.004\), her fitted values at liquid fraction 0.25 (held at 0.27). The code caps \(\tau_\text{edge}\) at what the foam itself can carry in a layer one bubble across, \(\tau_y + \tau_v\) at the shear rate \(v_s/2R_{32}\), though at swing speeds the cap is never reached: (14) gives 2.1, 9.7, 28 and 45 Pa at 1, 10, 50 and 100 mm/s, the cap 20 to 223 Pa. Because the stress grows only as \(v_s^{2/3}\), the drag per unit speed, \(\tau_\text{edge}/v_s\), grows without bound as the head slows; like the yield stress, this friction stops small motions in a finite time.

The head does not slide along its whole thickness. Sauret and co-workers found that in a sloshing foam the bubbles' speed falls off exponentially upward, over a length comparable to \(1/k\) [10, pp. 8 and 10]. The code takes the sliding speed as \(v_se^{-z'/\ell}\) at the height \(z'\) above the stout, \(\ell = R/\xi = 21.2\) mm. The power lost per unit area of glass, \(\tau_\text{edge}v_s\), then goes as \(v_s^{5/3}\), and integrating it over the head's thickness gives the effective height

\[ h_\text{eff} = \int_0^{h_f} e^{-5z'/3\ell}\,\mathrm dz' = \frac{3\ell}{5}\Big(1 - e^{-5h_f/3\ell}\Big) = 9.9\ \mathrm{mm} \]

over which the edge rubs at its full speed. The coefficient \(\kappa\) in (13) is \(\tau_\text{edge}h_\text{eff}/|w|\) times the length of glass per area of column, with \(v_s = |w|\).

5.5The head's weight, and when it breaks off

The head presses on the stout with \(\rho_f h_f g_\text{eff}\), where \(g_\text{eff} = g + a_z\) is the effective gravity in the glass's frame: 50.7 Pa at rest. The code puts this pressure on the stout's free surface in the pressure step (section 7). Where the stout's surface rises at the glass, the head rises with it, and its top can pass the rim. The part above the rim, of height \(\epsilon\), has nothing beside it. It breaks away when its weight and the outward push of the glass's acceleration beat its strength:

\[ \rho_f\big(g_\text{eff}\,\epsilon/2 + a_\text{out}\,\epsilon\big) \ge \tau_y . \tag{15} \]

The first term is the stress of the part's own weight averaged over its free face, the second the outward inertial push per unit area of its base, with \(a_\text{out}\) the outward acceleration felt in the glass's frame. A broken part leaves as a lump in free flight, falling under gravity minus the glass's acceleration, until it lands on the cart's body or falls away.

Example 5.2 · How far the wall must rise before the head goes

At rest, (15) gives \(\epsilon^* = 2\tau_y/(\rho_f g) = 2 \times 5.4/(272 \times 9.81) = 4.1\ \mathrm{mm}\). The head's top starts 3.5 mm below the rim, so the stout must rise \(3.5 + 4.1 = 7.6\ \mathrm{mm}\) at the wall before any head breaks off. An outward push of \(5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) lowers \(\epsilon^*\) to 2.0 mm. The stout itself spills only when it rises 22.5 mm at the wall, three times higher: on a pint with a head, foam leaves first.

06The cart, and who pushes whom

The glass does not move by itself. It sits on a cart that rolls over the road, and the stout pushes back on the cart.

The cart. It is the one-wheel cart of Fig. 12 [14, sections 6–8]: a body of 1.5 kg on a spring of 600 N/m and a damper of 8 N s/m over one wheel of 0.3 kg and radius 40 mm with a tyre of stiffness 20 kN/m, driven by a small DC motor through a 10:1 gear. The motor gives 5 N of push at the road per ampere and at most 3 A, and a speed loop measures the speed every millisecond and sets the current to hold the speed chosen for the ride. The wheel follows the road with its whole rim, so its axle rides over the highest point of a 40 mm circle rolled along the road, which rounds off short, sharp bumps. Each bump is a cosine hump, \(z_r = \tfrac12 h_b\,(1 + \cos 2\pi s)\) for \(|s| \lt \tfrac12\), with \(s = (x - x_b)/L\), height \(h_b\), length \(L\) and centre \(x_b\).

The glass's frame. The stout is computed in the frame of the glass, equation (1), because the walls are then at rest. That frame accelerates with the glass, and Newton's second law holds in it only if every parcel of mass \(\delta m\) is given the extra force \(-\delta m\,\mathbf a_g\), the force that pushes you back into your seat when a car speeds up. Per unit mass this is the term \(-\mathbf a_g\) of (1), and in the stout it acts together with gravity as an effective gravity \(\mathbf g - \mathbf a_g\). The code gives every particle this effective gravity, with \(\mathbf a_g\) the glass's acceleration averaged over the time step, puts the head's weight on the free surface as a pressure, and adds the thin layers of sections 4 and 5 as stresses at the glass and under the surface.

Who pushes whom. Let \(m\) be the stout's mass, \(\mathbf v_g\) the glass's velocity and \(\mathbf P\) the stout's momentum relative to the glass. Seen from the road, the stout's momentum is \(m\mathbf v_g + \mathbf P\). Newton's second law says that its rate of change equals the forces on the stout, the glass's push \(\mathbf F_{gs}\) and gravity: \(m\mathbf a_g + \mathrm d\mathbf P/\mathrm dt = \mathbf F_{gs} + m\mathbf g\). By Newton's third law the stout pushes the glass with \(-\mathbf F_{gs} = -m\mathbf a_g - \mathrm d\mathbf P/\mathrm dt + m\mathbf g\). The first and last terms are what a rigid load of mass \(m\) would do; the code includes them by counting the stout's mass, and the head's, in the cart's mass. What remains is the push of the moving stout.

Key result · the stout's push on the cart \[ \mathbf F = -\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf P}{\mathrm dt}, \qquad \mathbf P = \text{the stout's momentum relative to the glass} . \tag{16} \]

When the stout surges forward in the glass, the glass is pushed back, and the reverse.

The code works out \(\mathbf F_n = -(\mathbf P_n - \mathbf P_{n-1})/\Delta t_n\) after each fluid step \(n\), from the particles' momentum, and adds \(\mathbf F_n\Delta t_n\) to an impulse book. The sum telescopes: \(\sum_{n=1}^N\mathbf F_n\Delta t_n = -(\mathbf P_N - \mathbf P_0) = -\mathbf P_N\), because the stout starts at rest in the glass. The impulse booked on the cart is therefore minus the stout's momentum exactly, up to rounding, and the result check check_run.py tests that the two agree to one part in a thousand. One detail is worth knowing: the cart takes each step's push into its own sub-steps during the next fluid step, so it feels the stout's push one step, about 2 ms, late.

07How the computer does it

The computer follows a million small parcels of stout and a grid of boxes around them, and hands the motion back and forth between the two in every step.

7.1Particles and a grid

Equation (1) has two kinds of work in it. Carrying the stout along with its own motion, the term \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\), is easiest with parcels that move with the liquid: nothing is smeared, and a drop that leaves the glass simply keeps flying. The pressure and the friction are easiest on a fixed grid of boxes, where neighbours are known. The method used here, called particle-in-cell, uses both. The box around the glass has 57 × 57 × 114 = 370,386 cubic cells 1.5 mm wide. The stout is 1,001,952 particles, seeded two by two by two in every cell it fills (126,399 cells' worth, so 7.93 per cell), each carrying 0.429 mg, a sphere 0.93 mm across. On the grid the pressure lives at the cell centres and each component of the velocity on the cell faces at right angles to it, the usual staggered arrangement, because the difference of two neighbouring pressures then acts exactly on the face between them.

The particles hand their motion to the grid and take it back in every step, and each hand-over can lose some of it. The plain method gives each particle one velocity, and every hand-over smooths the motion as a strong viscosity would. The affine particle-in-cell method of Jiang and co-workers (APIC) gives each particle \(p\), for each component of the velocity, a value and a gradient, so that it carries a small linear velocity field [11, section 6]. For the component along the road, \(u\), carried on the faces \(j\) at right angles to the road, the two transfers read

\[ m_j u_j = \sum_p m_p W_{jp}\big(u_p + \mathbf c_p\cdot(\mathbf x_j - \mathbf x_p)\big), \qquad u_p = \sum_j W_{jp}\,u_j, \qquad \mathbf c_p = \sum_j \nabla W_{jp}\,u_j , \tag{17} \]

and the same for the other two components. Here \(\mathbf x_j\) is a face's position and \(\mathbf x_p\) the particle's, \(m_p\) the particle's mass, \(W_{jp}\) the trilinear weight of the particle at that face (the product of three tent functions, each falling from 1 to 0 over one cell), \(m_j = \sum_p m_pW_{jp}\) the mass the face receives, and \(\mathbf c_p\) the particle's gradient of \(u\). A linear velocity field survives both transfers unchanged, and angular momentum is conserved across them [11, abstract]; that keeps the swing from being damped by the transfers themselves as much as the plain method would damp it.

7.2One step

One fluid step of the code (Stout.step) does the following, in this order:

  1. the particles hand their velocities to the faces of the grid, (17);
  2. the liquid's level set is built from the particles (7.4), and the height of the surface in every column is read from it;
  3. viscosity is applied on the grid, explicitly; the step is safe because \(\nu\Delta t/\Delta x^2 = 0.0018\), far below the limit 1/6 for three dimensions;
  4. the three thin layers act: the glass's drag \(u \to u\,e^{-\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,\lambda\,\Delta t}\) with \(\lambda\) the area of glass per unit volume at the face, the skin's drag on the top cells, and the head's sheet (13); the energy each takes is added to its book;
  5. gravity and the glass's acceleration give every face the kick \(\Delta t\,(\mathbf g - \mathbf a_g)\);
  6. the head's weight is set as the pressure on the free surface;
  7. the pressure is found that makes the velocity free of divergence, and its gradient is subtracted (7.3);
  8. velocities are extended three cells into the air and the glass, so that particles near the boundaries read sensible values;
  9. the particles take their velocities back, (17), and move; a particle that would cross the glass is put back inside it, and one that crosses the rim outside the glass's radius becomes a drop in free flight;
  10. the head above the rim breaks off where (15) says so.

The order of steps 4 and 5 matters, and section 8 explains why.

7.3The pressure

Subtracting \(\Delta t\,\nabla p/\rho\) from the face velocities must leave no net flow into any cell. That condition is one linear equation per cell, a discrete Poisson equation for \(p\) with seven unknowns per row. The glass cuts through cells at an angle; the code weights each face by the fraction of it that is open to the stout, as Batty, Bertails and Bridson proposed [13, section 2.1]. They point out a property we shall need in section 8: with these weights a liquid at rest has the exact hydrostatic pressure as its solution, and that pressure cancels gravity exactly, so nothing moves. The equations are solved by conjugate gradients, preconditioned by the diagonal, until the largest residual is below \(10^{-5}\) of the largest right-hand side, with at most 600 iterations; at rest run 3 needed 90 iterations per step on average and 226 at most.

At the free surface the pressure is fixed: zero, or the head's weight. The surface usually lies between a cell centre in the liquid, pressure \(p_l\), and one in the air, at the fraction \(\alpha\) of the way across. The code gives the air cell the ghost pressure that puts the surface value \(p_s\) exactly at the surface when the two are joined by a straight line, \(p_l + \alpha(p_g - p_l) = p_s\):

\[ p_g = p_l\Big(1 - \frac1\alpha\Big) + \frac{p_s}{\alpha}, \qquad \alpha \ge 0.05 , \tag{18} \]

the second-order boundary condition that Batty and co-workers use as well [13, section 2.1].

7.4Where the surface is

The particles do not say by themselves where the liquid ends. Following Zhu and Bridson [12, section 5], the code computes at every cell centre \(\mathbf x\) the function

\[ \chi(\mathbf x) = |\mathbf x - \bar{\mathbf x}| - r_0, \qquad \bar{\mathbf x} = \frac{\sum_i b_i\mathbf x_i}{\sum_i b_i}, \qquad b_i = \Big(1 - \frac{|\mathbf x - \mathbf x_i|^2}{(2\Delta x)^2}\Big)^3 , \tag{19} \]

where \(\mathbf x_i\) are the positions of the particles within \(2\Delta x\) of \(\mathbf x\) and \(\bar{\mathbf x}\) their weighted mean. For a single particle \(\chi\) is the distance from it minus \(r_0\); for many, \(\chi \lt 0\) inside the liquid, \(\chi \gt 0\) outside, and the surface is where \(\chi = 0\), the level set of the liquid. Particles within two cells of the glass are mirrored in it, so the wall does not look like a surface. The radius \(r_0\) decides where the zero falls, and the code uses two values. For the pressure step \(r_0 = 0.9\Delta x\): it closes the small gaps that open between particles near the glass, where a gap would act as a bubble of air. For reading the height of the surface, \(r_0\) must put the zero on the liquid's true top. Seen from a point on the top of a flat layer seeded as the code seeds it, the weighted mean position lies \(0.505\Delta x\) below the top (we computed it: \(0.5054\Delta x\)), so \(r_0 = 0.505\Delta x\) does that. Read between cell centres as the code reads it, for 40 positions of the top relative to the cells, a flat layer then reads 0.001 to 0.063 of a cell low, while \(r_0 = 0.9\Delta x\) would read it 0.52 to 0.56 of a cell, about 0.8 mm, high. In each column the code finds the top of the stout's body from the \(0.9\Delta x\) field, then the highest zero of the \(0.505\Delta x\) field in the body's top three cells; a column where that field finds none takes the body's top less 0.68 cell and is counted. A column at the glass whose body ends because the glass ends there, not because air begins, has no surface of its own and is left out: the tulip is widest at 116 mm (39.3 mm across the radius), so a few columns at the edge have their centre inside the glass lower down but outside it at the fill, and run 4 read them at 120 mm, 5 mm low. A particle counts as a drop when it stands more than \(1.5\Delta x\) above the surface of its own column and of the eight around it; with that rule a pint at rest has no drops, where run 4 showed 84 dark dots along the glass.

7.5Time step and precision

The step is \(\Delta t = 2\) ms, shorter whenever the fastest particle would cross more than 0.8 of a cell in one step. The last step before each stored picture is shortened so that the picture falls exactly on its time; at rest that makes 16 steps of 2 ms and one of 1.33 ms per picture, 17 steps for every thirtieth of a second. Inside each fluid step the cart moves in sub-steps of 0.1 ms. The stout is computed in single precision (the Tesla T4 is 32 times slower in double), the sums inside the conjugate gradients and the energy books in double precision, the cart entirely in double. At rest run 3 took 72.6 ms per step on the T4.

08A pint at rest must stay at rest

The simplest test of a fluid program is to give it nothing to do and see that it does nothing.

Stout standing in a glass held still is in equilibrium: the pressure grows with depth as \(p = p_s + \rho g(h - z)\), and in every parcel the pressure's push from below balances the parcel's weight. Nothing moves, so nothing is sheared, and the friction of sections 4 and 5 has nothing to act on. Energy gives a sharp form of this. Let \(E_k\) be the stout's kinetic energy, \(E_p\) its potential energy and \(D\) the energy turned into heat by friction since the start. With the glass held still no outside force does work on the stout, so

\[ E_p(0) - E_p(t) = E_k(t) + D(t) . \tag{20} \]

Whatever potential energy the stout loses must reappear as motion or as heat. For a pint at rest all three terms are zero. A program that keeps these three books, as this one does, can be checked against (20) at every stored picture. In the computer the balance is just as exact: gravity's kick in step 5 gives every face \(\Delta t\,g = 19.6\ \mathrm{mm/s}\) downward, and the pressure of step 7, being hydrostatic, takes exactly that back, leaving zero (section 7.3).

Every run therefore begins with 2 s of the pint at rest on the full grid, and a guard stops the whole session before any ride if the surface in any column more than one cell inside the glass ends up more than half a cell (0.75 mm) from the fill, if the root mean square is above a quarter of a cell (0.375 mm), or if any stout or head is lost. On 13 November 2026 the guard stopped run 3. Its surface was off by 4.62 mm at worst and 0.484 mm in root mean square, 108 of the 1977 columns more than 0.75 mm off; nothing had spilled. The glass's drag then multiplied the kicked, downward velocity of the faces at the wall by a factor slightly below one, and the head's friction on the glass slowed the kicked top cells at the edge. The pressure step takes back the full kick everywhere, so at those faces it took back more than was left, and an upward velocity appeared that no force had caused. Every step the drags counted as heat the kinetic energy of a motion that was never real, the kick that the pressure was about to remove, and every step they left a real, spurious flow behind. That is why the books overflowed, and why most of the false heat, 648 of 774 µJ, was booked by the head, whose friction on the glass is the strongest of the three. The fix is to let the stresses act on the velocity the stout actually has, the last step's, which is free of divergence, and to add gravity's kick after them, just before the pressure step that cancels it (the order given in section 7.2).

We repeated the test on this computer with the same 1.5 mm grid, once with run 3's code and once with the code after both fixes, for one step from rest and for the first 0.1 s. For one step of 2 ms from rest, run 3's code gave the fastest particle 30.3 mm/s; the fixed code gives 0.76 mm/s, at the corner where the surface meets the glass, exactly as much as the same code with every friction and the head switched off (its model inviscid). That small remainder is the method's own and has nothing to do with the head or the thin layers. Over the first 0.1 s, run 3's code on this computer reproduced the books of the run on the T4 closely: potential energy lost 2.3 µJ, kinetic energy 0.37 µJ, counted as dissipated 57.2 µJ, against 2.4, 0.32 and 57.5 µJ there. The fixed code gives 0.27, 0.005 and 0.033 µJ. The false heat has fallen 1700-fold, and the stout's particles now settle by 0.06 µm on average instead of sinking. The books still do not close exactly: of the 0.27 µJ lost, 0.04 µJ appear as motion and booked heat, and the rest is taken by the method itself, by the pressure step and the transfers between particles and grid, which keep no book of their own.

Key result · the books of a pint at rest

For stout in a glass held still, the potential energy lost equals the kinetic energy gained plus the energy dissipated, (20), and at rest all three are zero. A friction term may only act on motion the stout really has; acting on a half-finished step, it turns the step's gravity into false heat and a false flow. Reading these books with nothing to do is the first test of every run.

09Playing the stored rides

The page shows only what was computed: one stored picture after another, at the speed of the ride.

What is stored. For every thirtieth of a second of each ride the run stores the height of the stout's surface in each of the 57 × 57 columns of the grid (in steps of 0.02 mm, each picture as the change from the one before), the thickness of the head on each column (steps of 0.1 mm), the position of every drop of stout outside the main body (steps of 0.1 mm), the position and volume of every lump of head that has broken off (volume in steps of 0.1 mm³), and the cart's position, speed, the heights of its body and wheel, the motor current and the stout's push (16), with the stout and the head spilled so far. A file lists every ride with its size and its SHA-256 fingerprint, a 64-digit number computed from every byte of the file (index-jax.json).

How it is played. The page downloads a ride, computes the fingerprint of what arrived and refuses the file if it differs from the listed one. The ride's clock \(T\) then advances by the real time between two pictures on your screen, at most 0.1 s at a time, so that a stalled browser resumes where it was instead of jumping. The page draws the stored picture number \(\mathrm{round}(30\,T)\), the one nearest in time, and never a blend of two: a blend of two stored surfaces is not a surface anybody computed. It never slows down or speeds up; at the end of the stored time it stops and offers to ride again. We checked this in a browser without a screen (headless Chromium) on the built page with the packed files of an earlier run, which have the same form: in every one of 152 to 174 sampled pictures the frame drawn was \(\mathrm{round}(30\,T)\); the ride's clock advanced 1.0000 s per second; the words under Readings changed 11 times in 3 s, since they are rewritten at most four times a second to stay readable; and the ride stopped at 8.00 s on picture 241 of 241.

The readings. The cart's speed is the stored speed of the picture on screen. The stout spilled is the number of particles that have left the glass times their mass, 0.429 mg each; the head spilled is the volume of the lumps broken off. The last reading counts the drops of stout and the lumps of head the picture holds outside the stout's body: in flight, or come to rest on the cart's body.

What is drawn from data, and what is drawing. The surface is drawn on a mesh of 14 rings and 96 angles; each point of the mesh takes its height from the four stored columns around it, weighted by distance (bilinear), leaving out columns without stout, and the outer ring is the glass at the height the surface has there. This is interpolation in space, for drawing; there is none in time. The head is a layer of the stored thickness on the stored surface, its colour fading to the stout's where it is thinner than 1.5 mm. Drops are dark dots 1.1 mm in radius at their stored places, lumps cream balls of their stored volume. The cart, its wheel and its body stand at their stored positions. The glass's tint and highlights, the ruby edge of the stout where the light path through it is short, the lettering on the glass and the camera are drawing only.

10What the computed rides show

A computation is worth trusting when it agrees with what we can work out without it, and where it goes beyond that, when we can say how each of its numbers was read.

What we expect. Sections 3 to 6 make predictions before we look at any stored picture. The surface should rock at 3.421 Hz; the model gives the head weight but no inertia, so it should not show the drop to about 3.07 Hz that the head's mass would cause in a real pint (section 11). Without the head the stout would damp its swing with \(\zeta \approx 0.011\); the head adds friction that grows less than linearly with speed and should stop the swing in a finite time once it is smaller than about half a millimetre at the wall; the method itself adds a numerical loss of its own, which the free swing without the head measures together with the glass's layer. Head should leave the glass when the stout rises about 7.6 mm at the wall, less while the glass pushes outward, and stout only when it rises 22.5 mm. Without friction the five bumps in step would build a swing of 45 mm, the kerb 24 mm and the single big bump 8.5 mm (Example 6.1).

How each number is read. Every number below is read from the stored data; nothing is recomputed by the solver.

  1. The pint at rest: after 2 s, the largest and the root-mean-square difference between the stored surface and the fill, over the 1977 columns more than one cell inside the glass; the columns read by the fallback of 7.4; the stout and head lost; and the three books of (20).
  2. The free swing: the run samples the stored surface on rings around the axis; on the ring at \(r = \tfrac{13}{16}(R - \Delta x/2) = 31.2\) mm, the \(\cos\theta\) part of the height, \(q_\text{ring} = \tfrac{2}{64}\sum\eta\cos\theta\) over 64 equally spaced angles \(\theta\), removes any lift of the whole surface and every shape except those with one wave around the glass. From 0.1 s after the push ends, \(q_\text{ring}(t)\) is fitted by least squares with \(q_0e^{-t/t_d}\cos(\omega t + \psi) + q_c\), which gives the frequency at which the swing rings, \(\omega/2\pi\), and the decay time \(t_d\). The solution of (6) rings at \(\omega = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}\) and decays at the rate \(1/t_d = \zeta\omega_0\), so the swing's own frequency is \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{\omega^2 + 1/t_d^2}\) and its damping ratio \(\zeta = 1/(\omega_0t_d)\). For a lightly damped swing the two frequencies are the same; for a heavily damped one they are not, and only \(\omega_0\) can be compared with 3.421 Hz. The push of Table 2 would leave an undamped swing of 5.2 mm at the wall, 5.0 mm on that ring.
  3. The rides: the largest lean, the height at the front wall minus the height at the back wall on the outermost ring; the stout spilled, in grams; the head spilled, in millilitres; the picture in which the first of either leaves; the range of the cart's speed; the impulse book of section 6 against the stout's final momentum; and the run's cost, milliseconds and pressure iterations per step on the T4.

11Limits, and the next model

Every model leaves things out. The useful question is which of them would change what we see, and by how much.

The head's own inertia ★. The model gives the head weight but no inertia in the swing: it presses on the stout with \(\rho_f h_f g_\text{eff}\), but the stout does not have to accelerate the head up and down. In a real pint it does. If the head moves up and down with the surface, the pressure under it is \(m_s(g + \ddot\eta)\), with \(m_s = \rho_f h_f = 5.17\ \mathrm{kg/m^2}\), and the surface condition (4) becomes \(\rho\,\partial^2\phi/\partial t^2 + \rho g\,\partial\phi/\partial z + m_s\,\partial^3\phi/\partial z\,\partial t^2 = 0\). With (2) this gives

\[ \omega^2 = \frac{gk\tanh kh}{1 + m_sk\tanh kh/\rho} . \]

For the pint \(m_sk/\rho = 5.17 \times 47.1/1007 = 0.24\), and the frequency falls from 3.421 to 3.07 Hz. The head adds a quarter to the mass that the swing moves, \(\rho/k = 21.4\ \mathrm{kg/m^2}\) of stout per unit area. Sauret and co-workers saw a shift of this sign in their foam-covered cell, 3.1 Hz against 3.3 Hz predicted without foam [10, p. 7]. A computed pint with a head should therefore rock faster than a real one, and this is the first thing the next version of the model should add.

What else is left out. The head does not flow sideways over the surface; its thickness changes only where it breaks off. It does not drain (that takes tens of minutes) and leaves no film on the glass. The contact line, where the stout's surface meets the glass, has its own friction, which the experiments of Stephens and co-workers suggest is part of the extra 50 % in their constant. The flow law (12) is used a little beyond the gas fractions it was fitted to, and the spill rule (15) is a simple balance: a wedge of foam sliding on the most dangerous plane through the rim's edge, the textbook estimate for a vertical cut in a material with a yield stress, would stand up to twice the height, 8.1 mm at rest, so the rule lets the head break early rather than late. A drop is a single particle of 0.43 mm³ with no surface tension of its own, so drops neither merge nor break. The cart feels the stout's push 2 ms late (section 6). The free swing without the head (Table 2) measures the method's own loss only together with the glass's layer and viscosity, whose share section 4 gives; a swing with every friction switched off (the code's model inviscid) would measure it alone.

The next model. The point of the cart is to carry the pint without spilling it: a controller that reads the swing and drives the motor against it, computed in the same solver with the controller inside the loop, ride by ride, beside these rides without it.
