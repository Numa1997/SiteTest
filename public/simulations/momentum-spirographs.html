Lecture note · Fig. 13 · chaos and order in one picture

A hundred pendulums on one sheet, and the eye behind them

Numa Koudsie · 11 November 2026 · the picture is 2swap's idea; the derivations and measurements here are our own (tools/fig13/chaos_map.py, hunt.py, this page's script)

Abstract. Fig. 13 shows a hundred double pendulums at once. They are separate experiments: each hangs still and is struck once, and the place of its cell on the sheet is the strike it received, \((p_1, p_2)\). What is drawn inside the cell is the real path of that pendulum's tip. Behind them glows a map of 921 600 further strikes, bright where two almost equal strikes soon disagree. The glow has the shape of an eye, and each part of it has a reason. The dark pupil holds the strikes too gentle to throw a rod over the top: less than 9.8 J, none of them chaotic. The bright iris holds the strikes that can flip a rod while gravity still decides which way: between 29.4 and 39.2 J, 64 % of them are chaotic. The two long streaks lie on the two strike directions for which the elbow just fails to fold through: within 0.02 rad of them 66 % of the hard strikes are chaotic, beyond 0.1 rad none. Everywhere else a hard strike makes gravity a small correction, and the tip draws a ring or a rosette. The note has four parts: the sheet and the eye; the one strike that draws a butterfly; the hunt for the strikes whose drawings close; and the motor that takes a chaotic pendulum, sets it on the butterfly and lets go.

01The picture

Hang a hundred identical wind chimes in a hall, in ten rows of ten. Give each one a different tap: harder to the right, harder on the lower piece towards the back. Then open a camera's shutter and let a small lamp on each chime's tip write on the film.

That photograph is Fig. 13. The chimes are double pendulums: a rod hanging from a pin, a second rod hanging from the end of the first. Where a chime hangs in the hall tells you how it was tapped. What its lamp writes tells you what it then did. Some lamps write a clean ring. Some write a flower with a fixed number of petals, again and again. A few write a scribble that never repeats. Neighbours in the hall were tapped almost alike, so walking through the hall you see how the motion changes as the tap changes, and where a small change of the tap changes everything.

Behind the hundred lamps the page lays a second picture, made the same way with many more pendulums and a different question: not "what does it draw" but "would its twin draw the same". That second picture glows in the shape of an eye. Sections 4 to 6 say what it measures and why it has a pupil, an iris and two long streaks.

02The machine and the strike

Two rods, two pins, gravity; no friction, no motor.

Rod \(i\) has mass \(m_i\), length \(l_i\), its centre of mass at \(a_i\) below its upper pin, and the moment of inertia \(I_i\) about that centre. For the page's default, two equal uniform rods, \(m = 1\ \mathrm{kg}\), \(l = 1\ \mathrm{m}\), \(a = l/2\), \(I = ml^2/12\), and \(g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). The angles \(\theta_1, \theta_2\) are measured from straight down. With

\[ A = m_1a_1^2 + I_1 + m_2l_1^2,\quad B = m_2a_2^2 + I_2,\quad C = m_2l_1a_2,\quad U_1 = g(m_1a_1 + m_2l_1),\quad U_2 = g\,m_2a_2, \tag{1} \]

(for the equal rods \(A = \tfrac43\), \(B = \tfrac13\), \(C = \tfrac12\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), \(U_1 = 14.7\), \(U_2 = 4.9\ \mathrm{J}\)) the kinetic and potential energies are

\[ T = \tfrac12A\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac12B\dot\theta_2^2 + C\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2,\qquad V = -U_1\cos\theta_1 - U_2\cos\theta_2 . \tag{2} \]

The momenta conjugate to the angles are \(p_1 = \partial T/\partial\dot\theta_1 = A\dot\theta_1 + C\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\dot\theta_2\) and \(p_2 = B\dot\theta_2 + C\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\dot\theta_1\). Solving for the angular speeds and writing \(H = T + V\) in terms of the momenta gives Hamilton's equations, the four first-order equations the page integrates:

\[ \dot\theta_1 = \frac{Bp_1 - C\cos\Delta\,p_2}{AB - C^2\cos^2\Delta},\quad \dot\theta_2 = \frac{Ap_2 - C\cos\Delta\,p_1}{AB - C^2\cos^2\Delta},\quad \dot p_1 = -C\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\sin\Delta - U_1\sin\theta_1,\quad \dot p_2 = +C\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\sin\Delta - U_2\sin\theta_2, \tag{3} \]

with \(\Delta = \theta_1 - \theta_2\), the angle of the elbow.

What a strike is. A hammer blow lasts so short a time that the rods do not move during it; it only changes the momenta. A sideways blow of impulse \(\hat J\) (in N·s) on a point that moves by \(\delta x = r_1\,\delta\theta_1 + r_2\,\delta\theta_2\) adds \(\hat J r_1\) to \(p_1\) and \(\hat J r_2\) to \(p_2\). A blow on the upper rod at the distance \(r\) below the top pin gives \((p_1, p_2) = (\hat J r,\ 0)\). A blow on the lower rod at the distance \(r\) below the elbow gives \((\hat J l_1,\ \hat J r)\), because moving that point also swings the upper rod. Two blows reach any \((p_1, p_2)\). The unit is \(\mathrm{N\,s\cdot m = kg\,m^2/s}\). So \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are not mysterious: they are how hard, and where, the hanging pendulum was hit.

The energy of a strike. At the strike both rods hang down, \(V = -(U_1+U_2) = -19.6\ \mathrm{J}\), and the strike supplies the kinetic energy

\[ K(p_1, p_2) = \frac{Bp_1^2 - 2Cp_1p_2 + Ap_2^2}{2(AB - C^2)} = \tfrac67p_1^2 - \tfrac{18}{7}p_1p_2 + \tfrac{24}{7}p_2^2\quad\text{(equal rods, J for } p \text{ in kg m}^2\text{/s)}. \tag{4} \]

Strikes of equal energy lie on an ellipse in the \((p_1, p_2)\) plane. For the equal rods its long axis points 22.5° above the \(p_1\) axis and is 3.49 times its short axis (the eigenvalues of the quadratic form are 0.3246 and 3.961). Hold on to that ellipse: it is the outline of the eye.

03Why they sit on one sheet

Two different spaces are drawn on the same paper, at two different scales. Keeping them apart is the whole trick of reading the figure.

The sheet is momentum space. Across the whole figure, left to right is \(p_1\) from \(-35.6\) to \(+35.6\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\), and bottom to top is \(p_2\) from \(-20\) to \(+20\). A point of the sheet is a strike. Every pendulum starts from the same position, hanging straight down, so the strike is all that distinguishes one from another: the sheet lists every possible experiment of this kind. A full state of the pendulum needs four numbers \((\theta_1, \theta_2, p_1, p_2)\); the sheet is the two-dimensional slice \(\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0\) of that four-dimensional space.

Each cell is a small window into real space. The sheet is cut into \(N \times N\) cells (10 × 10 by default). At the centre of each cell stands one pendulum, struck with the \((p_1, p_2)\) of that centre: \(p_1 = \pm3.56, \pm10.67, \pm17.78, \pm24.89, \pm32\) and \(p_2 = \pm2, \pm6, \pm10, \pm14, \pm18\). Inside the cell, the drawing is the path of the tip of its lower rod in ordinary space, in metres, with the pin at the cell's centre and down pointing down the page:

\[ x = l_1\sin\theta_1 + l_2\sin\theta_2,\qquad y = l_1\cos\theta_1 + l_2\cos\theta_2 . \tag{5} \]

The tip can reach \(l_1 + l_2 = 2\ \mathrm{m}\) from the pin; that reach is drawn as 0.4 of the cell's shorter side. So one cell's height stands for 4 kg·m²/s of strike when you read the sheet, and (on a wide screen) for 5 m when you read the drawing inside it. On a phone the sheet is drawn square, the same strikes with larger drawings.

They do not touch. No pendulum feels its neighbour. A drawing never crosses into the next cell because the tip cannot leave its 2 m circle. If two drawings look alike, it is because two nearly equal strikes led to nearly equal motions, not because one influenced the other.

Why draw them together at all. A single pendulum answers "what happens after this strike". The question of the figure is the next one: "how does what happens depend on the strike". That is a question about a whole family, and a family is best seen laid out by its parameter, the way a weather map shows a small wind arrow at each town or the periodic table puts each element where its numbers put it. The eye reads three things at once from the layout. Where neighbours agree, the motion depends smoothly on the strike. Where a picture is mirrored through the centre of the sheet, a symmetry is at work: striking with \((-p_1, -p_2)\) gives the mirror-image motion, because equations (3) are unchanged when all four variables change sign. And where neighbours disagree wildly, the outcome is sensitive to the strike. The glow behind marks exactly those places.

Key result

Position on the sheet = the cause (the strike, in kg·m²/s). Drawing in the cell = the effect (the tip's path, in metres). The pendulums share a sheet as entries share a table, not as bodies share a room.

04What the glow measures

The glow is a second experiment with twins.

For each of \(1280 \times 720 = 921\,600\) strikes covering the sheet, take the pendulum and a twin that differs by one ten-thousandth of a radian in the upper rod's angle, \(\delta\theta_1 = 10^{-4}\), about 0.1 mm at the end of the rod. Run both for 6.67 s. After every step measure how far apart the twins are in their four numbers, never counting more than 1:

\[ d = \Big\langle \min\!\Big(1,\ \sqrt{\delta\theta_1^2 + \delta\theta_2^2 + \delta p_1^2 + \delta p_2^2}\,\Big)\Big\rangle_{6.67\ \mathrm{s}},\qquad v = \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\frac{\ln(1000\,d)}{\ln 1000},\ 0,\ 1\Big). \tag{6} \]

The brightness is \(v\): dark where the twins stay within a thousandth (\(d \le 10^{-3}\)), full where they end up as far apart as two unrelated pendulums (\(d \to 1\)). This is the measure of 2swap's program, computed here with a step four times finer (16 000 Runge–Kutta steps of 0.417 ms).

Two regular pendulums that start 0.1 mm apart drift apart slowly, in proportion to time, like two runners at slightly different speeds. Two chaotic ones part like compound interest: the gap multiplies by a fixed factor every fraction of a second, so a ten-thousandth becomes one within seconds. The map separates these two behaviours. We call a strike chaotic below when \(v > 0.5\), which is \(d > 0.032\): the twins have parted more than 300 times their starting distance on average. By this count 5.5 % of the sheet is chaotic.

The map exists for the default pendulum only (equal uniform rods, \(g = 9.8\), the range \(P = 20\)); move a slider under Pendulum and the page hides it, because it would no longer be true.

05The eye: pupil and iris

The eye is drawn by energy. Switch on Explain the eye above to see the curves of this section on the figure.

On this map chaos appears only where a rod can go over the top: as long as both rods only rock about the bottom, the motion stays close to two coupled swings, and the twins stay together. Going over the top costs energy, and the strike must pay it. From (2), with the pendulum hanging at \(V = -(U_1 + U_2)\):

\[ \text{lower rod up, upper rod down: } K \ge 2U_2 = 9.8\ \mathrm{J};\qquad \text{upper rod up, lower hanging from it: } K \ge 2U_1 = 29.4\ \mathrm{J};\qquad \text{both straight up: } K \ge 2(U_1+U_2) = 39.2\ \mathrm{J}. \tag{7} \]

(The first threshold is Heyl's energy argument for a flip; the other two follow the same way.) Each threshold is one of the ellipses of (4).

The pupil is the inside of the smallest ellipse, \(K < 9.8\ \mathrm{J}\): semi-axes 5.49 and 1.57 kg·m²/s, tilted by 22.5°. No strike in it can lift even the lower rod over the top. The pendulum rocks, the twins stay together, the map is dark. Measured on the map: of the 0.96 % of the sheet inside it, 0.0 % is chaotic.

The iris is the ring around it. Here a rod can go over, but the strike's energy is of the same size as what gravity gives and takes during the motion, so at every near-miss at the top gravity decides between falling back and going over, and a hair's difference decides differently. That is where twins part.

Example 5.1 · two cells in the pupil

Of the hundred cells, two lie in the pupil: the strikes \((3.56,\ 2)\) and \((-3.56,\ -2)\), each with \(K = \tfrac67(3.56)^2 - \tfrac{18}{7}(3.56)(2) + \tfrac{24}{7}(2)^2 = 6.26\ \mathrm{J}\). Pin one: its tip only rocks below the pin. Their mirror partners across the \(p_1\) axis, \((3.56,\ -2)\) and \((-3.56,\ 2)\), have the same size of strike but \(K = 42.8\ \mathrm{J}\): they lie just outside the 39.2 J ellipse, where a third of the strikes are still chaotic, and the map calls both chaotic.

06The two streaks★

Far outside the eye a strike carries ten or a hundred times what gravity can give. Chaos should be gone. Yet two bright streaks run out to the edge of the sheet, like lashes.

Switch gravity off (the Gravity slider at 0). The pendulum is then a free jointed stick spinning about the pin. Nothing twists it from outside, so its total angular momentum \(p_1 + p_2\) is conserved, as well as its energy \(K\): two conserved quantities for two angles, and the motion is regular for every strike. What remains to decide is what the elbow does. It behaves like a pendulum of its own: either the angle \(\Delta\) keeps turning, the arms folding through each other once per turn and the tip passing through the pin (a petal), or \(\Delta\) swings back and forth and the tip stays out on a ring.

To fold through, the stick must pass the folded position, where its moment of inertia about the pin is smallest, \(I_{\text{fold}} = A + B - 2C\) (set \(\theta_2 = \theta_1 + \pi\) in (2); \(\tfrac23\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) for the equal rods). Spinning there with the angular momentum \(p_1 + p_2\) costs \((p_1+p_2)^2 / 2I_{\text{fold}}\), the way a skater who pulls her arms in spins faster and must supply energy to do so. The elbow folds through when the strike provides more:

\[ K(p_1, p_2) > \frac{(p_1+p_2)^2}{2(A + B - 2C)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 3p_1^2 - 114\,p_1p_2 + 75\,p_2^2 > 0\quad\text{(equal rods)}. \tag{8} \]

Both sides are quadratic in the strike, so the border does not depend on how hard the strike is, only on its direction: it is two straight lines through the centre of the sheet,

\[ p_1 = (19 \mp 4\sqrt{21})\,p_2:\qquad p_1 = 0.6697\,p_2\ \ (56.2^\circ \text{ above the } p_1 \text{ axis}),\qquad p_1 = 37.33\,p_2\ \ (1.5^\circ). \tag{9} \]

On these lines the elbow has exactly enough to reach the folded position and none to pass it: it creeps up to it and takes for ever, like a pendulum given exactly enough to stand on its head. Such a borderline motion is called a separatrix. Now switch gravity back on, however weak compared with the strike. Near the separatrix the elbow arrives at the folded position almost stopped, and there the small push of gravity is enough to decide whether it folds through or turns back, differently each time. Two twins disagree at the first such decision. This is the general rule for a regular system that is slightly disturbed: chaos appears first in a thin layer around its separatrices.

07Reading the cells

Four kinds of drawing, and where on the sheet each lives.

  • Rings. Upper right and lower left of the steep streak, towards the corners where \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) have the same sign. The elbow does not fold through; the arm stays nearly straight and the whole pendulum whirls round the pin like a single long rod. The tip stays near the 2 m circle.
  • Rosettes. Where (8) holds. Each turn of the elbow draws one petal through the pin; the next petal lies turned by a fixed angle \(\delta\). When \(\delta/2\pi\) is a fraction \(q/k\) the flower closes with \(k\) petals. The flower hunt finds these strikes.
  • Butterflies. The column \(p_1 = 3.56\), upper half: pin the cell \((3.56,\ 10)\). Here each petal lands almost on the last one, so the petal direction drifts slowly, and gravity makes it swing about straight down like a pendulum; the petals pile up at the two turning points, the wings. Tuned to \((3.557,\ 12.211)\) it closes after 24 petals: the butterfly of the dashboard and of the catch.
  • Tangles. The cells on the glow. Six of the hundred strikes are chaotic by the map, three mirror pairs: \((3.56,\ 6)\) and \((-3.56,\ -6)\), \((-3.56,\ 2)\) and \((3.56,\ -2)\), \((17.78,\ 2)\) and \((-17.78,\ -2)\). The last pair sits on the flat streak. Their drawings never settle into a figure; restart and they draw the same tangle again, because the page repeats the same arithmetic, but strike a hair differently (change \(N\) or \(P\) slightly) and the tangle is another one.

Every drawing has a twin on the opposite side of the centre, mirrored left to right: the symmetry \((\theta, p) \to (-\theta, -p)\) of section 3.

08How the page computes it

A hundred pendulums integrated live, and a photographic plate that forgets.

Integration. Each pendulum's four numbers are advanced by the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method with the step \(h = 1/4500\ \mathrm{s} = 0.222\ \mathrm{ms}\); the error of one step falls as \(h^5\). The step is fixed, so the hardest strikes, which move fastest, are the least accurate. Measured by running this page for 10 s of motion: the energy of the median pendulum has changed by \(2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{J}\); the worst is the corner strike \((-32,\ 18)\) with 0.14 J out of 3450 J, a relative error of \(4\times10^{-5}\). For a regular pendulum such an error only shifts the timing slightly. For a chaotic one any error grows like the twins' distance, so after a few seconds the drawn tangle is one possible tangle, not the one an exact computation would draw; what stays true is its kind.

The plate. The tip is recorded every fifth step, 900 points a second. Each short piece of path deposits light on the pixels it crosses, the same amount per unit length, so a place the tip visits ten times is ten times as exposed: the brightness counts passes, as on a long-exposure photograph. Every frame the whole plate fades by \(e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\); the trail memory \(\tau\) (1.5 s by default) is how long the plate remembers. The exposure \(D\) is turned into colour through \(\ln(1 + D/D_0)\), which keeps both faint single passes and bright crossings visible.

The map was computed once, offline (tools/fig13/chaos_map.py, 921 600 twin pairs, 16 000 steps each), and is shown as an image under the plate.

  • The pinned pendulum: tap a cell. The panel shows its strike, \(K\) from (4), the map's \(v\) at its strike, and which part of the eye it is in. A tap on it again lets it go. The small picture has three views.
  • Tip path: that pendulum enlarged, with the last 3 s of its tip's path.
  • Angles: \(\theta_2\) against \(\theta_1\), each taken between −180° and +180°, hanging straight down in the middle. The left edge is the same line as the right one and the bottom the same as the top, so the square is a torus cut open and laid flat. The path is kept from the moment of pinning. A rod that goes over the top leaves through one edge and comes back through the opposite one.
  • Section: a Poincaré section. Each time the upper rod passes straight down, \(\theta_1 = 0\) (mod \(2\pi\)), one dot is placed at \((\theta_2, p_2)\). The energy is fixed, so the motion lives on a three-dimensional surface, and the section cuts that surface down to a plane. A regular motion winds round a torus, and its dots fall on the closed curve where the plane cuts that torus. A chaotic motion scatters its dots over an area. A closed orbit returns to the same few dots. A pass with the upper rod turning backwards is drawn at \((-\theta_2, -p_2)\): the mirror image of a motion is again a motion, so all dots belong to one section, the one with \(\dot\theta_1 > 0\). The moment of passing is found inside one integration step of 1/4500 s by a straight line.
  • Zoom: the sheet is a window on the plane of strikes. Zoom in halves its width and height around the pinned strike, which stays pinned; with nothing pinned it narrows around the middle. The pendulums in the cells are computed afresh for the new strikes. The map behind is one stored picture, a value every 0.056 kg·m²/s; zoomed in further than that it shows its own grain and nothing new.
  • Atlas: the list holds the fourteen closed curves of the flower hunt of Part III, ten flowers and four butterflies, read from the same file as the hunt's garden. Choosing one sets the pendulum to equal rods under \(g = 9.8\) m/s², puts that strike on the centre of a cell, pins it, and shows 1 kg·m²/s of \(p_2\) either side of it. The neighbours then show how the curve opens when the strike is slightly off.
  • Where drawing and model differ: the plate forgets (the model does not); the colour is a logarithm of the exposure; the map is 6.67 s of twins, so a strike whose twins part only later counts as regular.

10Limits and what comes next

The pendulum has no friction and the strike is instantaneous. The sheet shows one slice of the four-dimensional state space, the pendulums that start hanging still; a pendulum released from a raised position is not on it. The map's verdict depends on its 6.67 s and on the threshold \(v > 0.5\); it is a finite-time measure, not a Lyapunov exponent. The argument for the streaks is the standard picture of chaos near a separatrix, supported here by measurement, not proved for this system. The section of the pinned pendulum is one cut, at \(\theta_1 = 0\); a motion whose upper rod seldom passes straight down fills it slowly. Next: the same map for point masses and unequal rods, and the stable partners of the butterflies.

Part II

One cell becomes the butterfly

One strike of the sheet draws a butterfly. This part looks at that one pendulum: the wall its tip moves in, the two waves that are its height and its sideways position, how turning the paper makes a wave into the butterfly, and what the machine does in numbers. The dashboard above (The butterfly) shows it; the film "built by hand" draws it with a ruler.

12The plane the motion lives in

Hold a torch level and let a bicycle wheel turn in front of a wall: the shadow of a spoke tip on the floor slides to and fro, its shadow on the side wall goes up and down. Neither shadow is a circle; both are waves. The circle is only seen from the front.

Put the front view in numbers. Call the sideways position \(x\) and the height \(h\), both in metres, and write the point as one complex number,

\[ z = x + i\,h . \tag{10} \]

The plane of \(z\) is not an abstraction here: it is the wall the motion happens in. For a point on a wheel of radius \(r\) turning at angular speed \(\omega\),

\[ z(t) = r\,e^{i\omega t},\qquad x = r\cos\omega t,\qquad h = r\sin\omega t . \tag{11} \]

For the double pendulum of two rods of length \(l\), each angle measured from straight down, the lower rod's tip sits at \(x = l(\sin\theta_1+\sin\theta_2)\), \(h = -l(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)\). Since \(-i\,e^{i\theta} = \sin\theta - i\cos\theta\),

\[ z(t) = -i\,l\left(e^{i\theta_1(t)} + e^{i\theta_2(t)}\right). \tag{12} \]

The two rods are two rotating arrows added head to tail; the plane on the left of the figure is the real wall the pendulum swings in. Nothing about it is a momentum picture: the kick \((p_1,p_2)\) only chooses which motion happens (Fig. 13).

13The waves and the straight lines

The height \(h = \operatorname{Im} z\) is drawn against time to the right of the plane, at the same height scale, the newest instant at the left edge and earlier ones further right. The line joining the moving point to the head of that wave is horizontal at every instant, because both ends are at the same \(h\). The sideways position \(x = \operatorname{Re} z\) is drawn below the plane, time running downward, and joined by a vertical line, because both ends share \(x\). For the wheel these are the sine and the cosine of (11).

14Turning the paper

Give every instant a third coordinate, the time axis: \(\tau = k\,a\), where \(a\) is how long ago the instant was and \(k\) is a scale in pixels per second. The motion becomes a curve in space, \((x, h, \tau)\): a helix for the wheel. Each panel is a view of that one curve. Turn the view by \(\varphi\) about the vertical axis through the middle of the panel, \(\tau_c\):

\[ \text{screen } x = \tau_c + (\tau-\tau_c)\cos\varphi + x\sin\varphi,\qquad \text{screen } y = h . \tag{13} \]

At \(\varphi = 0\) the panel shows \((\tau, h)\), the wave. At \(\varphi = 90^\circ\) the time axis points at the reader, every \(\tau\) lands on the same place, and the panel shows \((x, h)\): the circle, or the butterfly. In between, the wave is seen at a slant and looks like a spring. The height never changes under this turn, so the horizontal line stays horizontal throughout. The panel below turns the same way about a horizontal axis, keeping \(x\), so its line stays vertical.

The turn is a rotation, so it is undone by turning back: a circle seen edge-on along time is a wave, and a wave seen end-on along time is a circle. Which picture appears depends only on where one looks from.

15What the butterfly needs

The butterfly is the pendulum kicked with \(p = (3.557, 12.211)\) kg·m²/s from hanging still. Its state returns to the start after \(T = 2.659\) s (to about \(10^{-11}\), found by Newton's method on Hamilton's equations; see The Flower Hunt), so its path closes after 24 petals. It is shown here at a quarter of real speed. In a frictionless model it repeats for ever; a real one loses energy to friction, and this orbit is weakly unstable (a small error grows by about 2 % per lap), so it drifts after a minute or two.

16The butterfly as a machine: its strike, its speeds, its forces

One cell of the sheet, moved by a few thousandths of its strike until the drawing closes. Here is what that pendulum does, in numbers one could measure on a bench.

The strike. The butterfly is the pendulum struck from hanging still with \(p^* = (3.557313,\ 12.211005)\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\). In the hammer picture of section 2 this is two blows: \(12.21\ \mathrm{N\,s}\) forwards on the free end of the lower rod, which gives \((12.21,\ 12.21)\), and \(8.65\ \mathrm{N\,s}\) backwards on the elbow, which gives \((-8.65,\ 0)\). One blow alone cannot do it: a blow on the lower rod at the distance \(r\) below the elbow gives \(p_2/p_1 = r/l\), and the butterfly needs \(p_2/p_1 = 3.43\), a point 3.43 m down a rod that is 1 m long.

The speeds follow from the momenta. With both rods hanging, \(\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2) = 1\), and solving the two definitions of section 2 for the angular speeds gives

\[ \dot\theta_1 = \frac{Bp_1 - Cp_2}{AB - C^2} = -25.30\ \mathrm{rad/s},\qquad \dot\theta_2 = \frac{Ap_2 - Cp_1}{AB - C^2} = +74.59\ \mathrm{rad/s}. \tag{14} \]

The upper rod starts backwards at 4.0 turns a second, the lower rod forwards at 11.9 turns a second. The strike supplies \(K = 410.38\ \mathrm{J}\) by (4), so the energy is \(E^* = K - 19.6 = 390.78\ \mathrm{J}\).

One lap. After \(T = 2.659405\ \mathrm{s}\) the pendulum is back in its starting state, the lower rod exactly 24 turns further on. In between, the two rods do very different things. The upper rod behaves like a slow pendulum: it swings out to \(\pm156.7^\circ\) and back, never over the top, its speed between \(-32.7\) and \(+33.1\ \mathrm{rad/s}\). The lower rod behaves like a spinner: it never stops and never reverses, turning at 44.7 to 74.6 rad/s, 9.0 turns a second on average. The tip moves at 38 to 52 m/s, the speed of a car on a motorway. The top pin has to hold all this: it pulls with about 0.9 kN on average and with up to 4.3 kN, 220 times the weight of the two rods (19.6 N).

Why the drawing is a butterfly. Each time the elbow is straight, the tip is at its full reach \(2l\), in the direction of the upper rod: that is the end of a petal. The lower rod overtakes the upper one 24 times a lap, so there are 24 petals, and each points where the upper rod pointed at that moment. The upper rod swings like a pendulum, slow at its turning points, so the petals crowd together there: the outermost ones lie at \(\pm135.6^\circ\) from straight down. Those two crowds are the wings. If the upper rod went over the top, the petals would go all the way round and the drawing would be a flower.

Why it is not chaos. The motion carries 390.8 J. Gravity can give or take at most 19.6 J of it, one part in twenty. So gravity cannot stop a rod or decide which way it goes; it only bends the slow swing of the upper rod. Followed for one lap, an error in the state grows by the factor 1.0198 (section 25): the exact closed drawing is slowly lost, but the butterfly shape is not.

Key result · the butterfly

Strike \((3.557,\ 12.211)\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\) from hanging still; energy 390.78 J; period 2.6594 s; 24 turns of the lower rod and 24 petals a lap; upper rod within \(\pm156.7^\circ\); an error grows 2 % a lap.

Part III

The flower hunt: which strikes close

A double pendulum that hangs still and is kicked once draws a curve with the tip of its lower rod. For most kicks the curve never closes. We show that the tip draws one petal each time the arms fold through, that without gravity a single number, the kick's direction, decides how far each new petal turns, and that a flower closes when that turn is a rational part of a full circle. With gravity we find the closed flowers exactly, as periodic orbits, by Newton's method, and we measure which of them survive a small push. A butterfly turns out to be a pendulum inside the pendulum: the direction of the petals swings back and forth about straight down.

17Where a petal comes from

Put your forearm in line with your upper arm and your hand is as far from your shoulder as it can be. Fold the elbow all the way and your hand comes back to the shoulder. A double pendulum does the same with its tip.

Each rod has length \(l\), the upper rod makes the angle \(\theta_1\) with the downward vertical and the lower rod the angle \(\theta_2\). The tip is the sum of two arrows of length \(l\). Written as a complex number, with the real axis pointing down and the imaginary axis pointing right,

\[ z_{\text{tip}} = l\,e^{i\theta_1} + l\,e^{i\theta_2} = 2l\cos\frac{\Delta}{2}\;e^{i\psi}, \qquad \Delta = \theta_1 - \theta_2,\quad \psi = \frac{\theta_1 + \theta_2}{2}. \tag{15} \]

To see the second form, take \(e^{i\psi}\) out of both terms: what is left is \(l(e^{i\Delta/2} + e^{-i\Delta/2}) = 2l\cos(\Delta/2)\). So the tip lies at the distance \(2l\,|\cos(\Delta/2)|\) from the pivot. When the arms line up (\(\Delta = 0\) or a whole number of turns) the tip is on the rim, \(2l\) away. When they fold (\(\Delta = \pi\)) the tip is at the pivot.

If the angle between the arms keeps turning, the tip runs out to the rim, back through the pivot, out to the rim on the other side, and so on. One full turn of \(\Delta\) draws one petal, a loop through the centre. The tip of that petal lies in the direction of the lower rod at the moment the arms are lined up. Call \(\delta\) the angle the lower rod turns between two such moments. Then the next petal tip is \(\delta\) further round, and the flower closes after \(k\) petals exactly when

\[ \frac{\delta}{2\pi} = \frac{q}{k} \pmod 1, \qquad q, k \text{ whole numbers with no common factor}. \tag{16} \]

Every pendulum on this page starts hanging straight down, \(\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0\). The arms are lined up, so the start is itself a petal tip: every flower here has a petal pointing straight down. The arms fold through only for some kicks. For the others \(\Delta\) rocks back and forth without reaching \(\pi\), the tip never passes the pivot, and the pendulum draws rings instead of petals.

18Without gravity, one number decides

A hard kick makes gravity a small correction. So first switch gravity off.

Without gravity the pendulum is a free, jointed stick spinning about the pivot. Nothing outside twists it, so its total angular momentum \(p_1 + p_2\) stays constant. It also has no length scale in time: if a motion \(\theta(t)\) is possible with the kick \(\mathbf p\), then \(\theta(\lambda t)\) is possible with the kick \(\lambda\mathbf p\), because every term of the kinetic energy scales with \(\lambda^2\). Kick twice as hard and the same curve is drawn twice as fast. So \(\delta\) depends only on the direction of the kick, the angle \(\alpha\) in \(\mathbf p = |\mathbf p|(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\).

Whether the arms fold through follows from energy alone. At the start, for two equal uniform rods of mass \(m\), the energy is \(E = \tfrac{6}{7ml^2}(p_1^2 - 3p_1p_2 + 4p_2^2)\). To fold, the stick must spin with its arms folded, where its moment of inertia about the pivot is smallest, \(\tfrac23 ml^2\). Spinning with the angular momentum \(p_1+p_2\) then costs \(\tfrac{3(p_1+p_2)^2}{4ml^2}\), the way a skater pulling in her arms spins faster and must supply energy to do so. The arms fold through when the kick provides more than that:

\[ 3p_1^2 - 114\,p_1p_2 + 75\,p_2^2 > 0 . \tag{17} \]

The edge of this region is two straight lines through the origin, \(p_1 = (19 \mp 4\sqrt{21})\,p_2\), which is \(p_1 = 0.6697\,p_2\) and \(p_1 = 37.33\,p_2\). They are the two arms of the X in Fig. 13's chaos map.

We integrate one turn of \(\Delta\) for 1800 kick directions, 0.1° apart, and read off \(\delta(\alpha)\). It is the curve in the bench's chart. Solving (16) on it gives 80 directions for flowers of 2 to 12 petals.

Key result · without gravity

A flower's shape is fixed by the direction of the kick alone. Its size in time is set by the strength of the kick. Closed flowers sit at the directions where \(\delta/2\pi\) is a fraction \(q/k\).

19With gravity: closed orbits

Gravity spoils both symmetries: the time scaling and the freedom to turn the flower. A closed flower must now be found as a whole orbit.

A flower of \(k\) petals is closed if, after \(k\) turns of \(\Delta\), the pendulum is back where it started: hanging straight down, \(\theta_2\) a whole number of turns, with the same momenta. Energy is conserved, so once \(p_1\) matches, \(p_2\) matches too. That leaves two conditions for the two unknowns of the kick:

\[ F(p_1, p_2) = \Bigl( \theta_2(t_k) - 2\pi n,\;\; p_1(t_k) - p_1(0) \Bigr) = (0,\,0), \tag{18} \]

where \(t_k\) is the moment of the \(k\)-th alignment. We solve (18) by Newton's method, starting from the gravity-free flower at a kick strength of 18 or 30 kg·m²/s, and integrate with an eighth-order Runge–Kutta method (DOP853) at a relative tolerance of \(10^{-11}\). Every orbit in the garden closes to better than \(10^{-9}\).

Example 19.1 · the seven-petal daisy

The kick \(\mathbf p = (-28.032,\ 0.476)\) kg·m²/s gives the energy \(E = 689.0\) J, 17.6 times the 39.2 J it takes to lift both rods straight up. The tip draws seven petals and returns in \(T = 0.794\) s; after seven petals the state differs from the start by \(1.8\times10^{-11}\).

A closed orbit is useful only if it survives a small push. Watch the moments when the arms line up: the state there is \((\theta_2, p_1)\), and one flower is a map of that plane onto itself. Its Jacobian \(J\) has determinant 1, because Hamiltonian motion keeps phase-space area. If \(|\operatorname{tr} J| < 2\), nearby kicks circle the flower and draw nearly the same picture: the orbit is stable. If \(|\operatorname{tr} J| > 2\), nearby kicks run away from it and the picture smears within a few periods: unstable. Within 0.01 of 2 our finite differences cannot tell, and we say neutral. This is common at high energy, where gravity is weak and almost a whole family of kicks nearly closes. In the garden the 3-petal flower at \(\mathbf p = (24.42,\ 0.00)\) has \(\operatorname{tr} J = 1.96\): stable. The 7-petal flower at \((7.51,\ 12.34)\) has \(\operatorname{tr} J = -14.8\): it is there on purpose, so you can watch an unstable flower fall apart.

20The butterfly

A butterfly is a flower that cannot decide where to point.

Take the kick \(\mathbf p = (3.556,\ 10)\), a cell of the video's grid. Its petal tips point, in order, 57°, 97°, 119°, 126°, 121°, 104°, 74°, 33°, −14°, … from straight down. Each petal lands close to the previous one: the lower rod turns by almost a whole turn between petals, so \(\delta/2\pi\) is close to 0, the one-petal resonance. Without gravity the petals would creep round the circle at a steady rate. Gravity pulls the petal direction towards straight down, so it swings about the bottom like a pendulum, out to \(\pm126°\) and back. A pendulum is slowest at its turning points, so the petals pile up there: two dense fans, left and right. Those are the wings.

Along the line \(p_1 = 3.556\) every kick from \(p_2 = 7.0\) to \(17.9\) is caught in this swing (89 of 161 kicks, 0.1 apart). The wings open between about \(\pm123°\) and \(\pm165°\), widest for the hardest kicks, and one swing takes between 10 and 45 petals. When one swing takes a whole number \(N\) of petals, the butterfly closes. It is then a periodic orbit with \(N\) turns of \(\Delta\), and (18) finds it.

Example 20.1 · the 24-petal butterfly

\(\mathbf p = (3.557,\ 12.211)\) kg·m²/s, \(E = 390.8\) J. The petal direction swings out to \(\pm135.6°\), the tip draws 24 petals and returns in \(T = 2.659\) s; the state closes to \(9\times10^{-12}\). Its trace is 2.000: neutral.

The butterflies with 12, 14 and 16 petals have traces of 2.39, 2.17 and 2.04: unstable. This is expected. Resonant closed orbits come in pairs, one stable and one unstable (the Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem), and Newton's method found the unstable member of each of these three pairs. Their stable partners are not yet computed.

Part IV

Catching the butterfly with a motor

The butterfly is a periodic orbit of the double pendulum: struck from rest with \(p = (3.557, 12.211)\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\), it returns to its start after \(T = 2.659\ \mathrm{s}\) with the lower rod 24 turns further on. It is unstable, but barely: an error grows by a factor 1.0198 a lap. We show how a single motor at the top pin, acting as a torque \(\tau\) on the upper rod, can bring the pendulum from anywhere into this orbit. Two exact identities carry the method: the motor changes the energy at the rate \(\tau\,\dot\theta_1\) and the total angular momentum at the rate \(\tau\) minus gravity's torque. With them the motor pumps the energy to the butterfly's 390.8 J and sizes the slow swing; then it waits, motor off, for the free motion to pass close; then a feedback designed on the orbit itself, a periodic linear-quadratic regulator, catches and holds it; then it lets go. A miss drains the pendulum back into chaos for a fresh try. Measured on 300 random starts (three sets of 100): all caught and held, half of them within 18 s after the motor switches on, the slowest after 229 s.

23The picture

A child on a playground swing who wants to go higher does not fight the swing. She waits for the right moment in each swing and pushes a little, in step with the motion. And a juggler does not grab a falling club anywhere: he lets it come to his hand and then closes his fingers.

Catching the butterfly is both of these. The double pendulum is a slow pendulum (the upper rod, swinging out to ±157°) carrying a fast spinner (the lower rod, whirling round 24 times a lap). The motor at the top pin can only push on the upper rod. It pushes in step with the motion to give the pendulum the right energy and the right size of swing, as the child does. Then it lets the motion come close by itself, as the juggler does, and only then holds it with small, quick corrections. When the corrections have done their work, it lets go, and the pendulum keeps drawing the butterfly for minutes on its own, because the orbit is only barely unstable.

Two facts make this possible, and both are exact. Torque times angular speed is power: a push on a rod that is turning the same way adds energy, one against the motion takes it away. And a torque at the pin is the only thing besides gravity that changes the pendulum's total angular momentum. Everything below follows from these two facts and from looking at the butterfly as a closed loop in the four-dimensional space of the pendulum's states.

24The machine and its equations

Two equal uniform rods, a frictionless pin at the top and one between the rods, gravity, and a motor at the top pin.

Each rod has mass \(m = 1\ \mathrm{kg}\) and length \(l = 1\ \mathrm{m}\); \(g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). The angles \(\theta_1, \theta_2\) are measured from straight down. A uniform rod about its centre has moment of inertia \(I = ml^2/12\), and its centre lies at \(a = l/2\) from its upper end. The kinetic energy is that of the upper rod turning about the pin plus that of the lower rod's centre moving and the lower rod turning about its centre; the potential energy is that of the two centres of mass:

\[ \mathcal L = \tfrac12 A\,\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac12 B\,\dot\theta_2^2 + C\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 + U_1\cos\theta_1 + U_2\cos\theta_2, \tag{19} \] \[ A = ma^2 + I + ml^2 = \tfrac43\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},\quad B = ma^2 + I = \tfrac13\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},\quad C = mla = \tfrac12\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},\quad U_1 = g(ma+ml) = 14.7\ \mathrm{N\,m},\quad U_2 = gma = 4.9\ \mathrm{N\,m}. \tag{20} \]

The conjugate momenta \(p_i = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\theta_i\) are \(p_1 = A\dot\theta_1 + C\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\dot\theta_2\) and \(p_2 = B\dot\theta_2 + C\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\dot\theta_1\). Solving this linear pair for the angular speeds and forming \(H = \sum p_i\dot\theta_i - \mathcal L\) gives the Hamiltonian

\[ H = \frac{B\,p_1^2 - 2C\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\,p_1p_2 + A\,p_2^2}{2\,\big(AB - C^2\cos^2(\theta_1-\theta_2)\big)} - U_1\cos\theta_1 - U_2\cos\theta_2 . \tag{21} \]

The motor exerts a torque \(\tau\) between the fixed support and the upper rod. Its virtual work is \(\tau\,\delta\theta_1\), so it enters as a generalised force on \(\theta_1\) only, and Hamilton's equations become

\[ \dot\theta_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad \dot p_1 = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta_1} + \tau,\qquad \dot p_2 = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta_2}. \tag{22} \]

One number sets the regime: the energy compared with what gravity can give or take. The potential energy ranges over \(\pm(U_1+U_2) = \pm 19.6\ \mathrm{J}\). The butterfly carries \(E^* = 390.78\ \mathrm{J}\), twenty times more. At such energies gravity steers the motion but does not dominate it; at energies of the order of \(U_1+U_2\) the double pendulum is chaotic almost everywhere. The motor's limit is \(|\tau| \le 60\ \mathrm{N\,m}\), about four times \(U_1\).

25The target: an unstable periodic orbit

The butterfly is not a place but a loop: a closed path in the space of states \(s = (\theta_1, \theta_2, p_1, p_2)\).

Struck from rest with \(p^* = (3.557313,\ 12.211005)\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\), that is with angular speeds \(\dot\theta_1 = -25.30\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), \(\dot\theta_2 = +74.59\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), the pendulum returns to exactly its starting state after

\[ s(T) = s(0) + (0,\ 2\pi\cdot 24,\ 0,\ 0),\qquad T = 2.659405\ \mathrm{s}. \tag{23} \]

Equation (23) is a boundary condition in time; the strike was found by Newton's method on it (tools/fig13/butterfly.py), closing to about \(10^{-11}\). Call the orbit \(s^*(t)\).

Stability. Follow a nearby state \(s^*(0)+\delta s\) for one lap. To first order the error obeys the variational equation \(\dot{\delta s} = J(s^*(t))\,\delta s\), with \(J = \partial f/\partial s\) the Jacobian of the right-hand side of (22); integrated over a lap it gives the monodromy matrix \(\Phi(T)\), \(\delta s(T) = \Phi(T)\,\delta s(0)\). Its eigenvalues, the Floquet multipliers, computed from the exact Jacobian (SymPy) and DOP853 at relative tolerance \(10^{-12}\), are

\[ \mu = 1.01977,\quad 0.98062,\quad 1 \pm 0.0017i,\qquad \det\Phi(T) = 1 \pm 2\times10^{-12}. \tag{24} \]

The pair near 1 belongs to every periodic orbit of a conservative system: a shift along the orbit stays a shift, and a change of energy changes the period (it would be a Jordan block at exactly 1; the 0.0017 is the numerical splitting of that block). The determinant 1 is Liouville's theorem: the flow keeps phase-space volume. The other pair, \(\mu\) and \(1/\mu\), says the butterfly is a saddle: one direction grows by 1.98 % a lap, one shrinks by the same factor. An error of \(10^{-6}\) needs \(\ln(10^5)/\ln 1.0198 \approx 590\) laps, 26 minutes, to reach 0.1. Measured on the full equations, on 40 pendulums the motor had caught and released (section 32): they stayed within 0.1 of the orbit for 403 s in the median (about 150 laps), the shortest for 41 s, the longest for more than 800 s. That is shorter than the 2 % a lap suggests, because the loop stretches a small error unevenly: within the first few laps it can grow twenty times before the slow growth takes over. One run followed for 1500 laps (66 minutes) kept its wings between 135.55° and 135.83°, never over the top: the exact drawing is lost within minutes, the butterfly shape is not.

Why catching is needed at all

A saddle is never reached by the free motion from outside: the states that flow into it form a thin surface (its stable manifold), and a chaotic or a regular motion crosses near it only now and then. Without friction, nothing can make the free pendulum settle on the butterfly; with friction, it would lose the 390.8 J. Something has to put energy in, choose the swing, and correct the last error: the motor.

26The motor's two levers

One torque, two exact effects.

Energy. Along a solution of (22),

\[ \frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_i\Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta_i}\dot\theta_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\Big) = \sum_i\Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta_i}\Big) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_1}\,\tau = \tau\,\dot\theta_1 . \tag{25} \]

The brackets cancel term by term; what is left is the power of the motor, torque times the angular speed of the rod it drives.

Angular momentum. Let \(P = p_1 + p_2\). The kinetic part of (21) depends on the angles only through \(\theta_1-\theta_2\), so its derivatives with respect to \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) are equal and opposite and drop out of \(\dot p_1 + \dot p_2\):

\[ \frac{dP}{dt} = \tau - U_1\sin\theta_1 - U_2\sin\theta_2 . \tag{26} \]

This is the torque balance about the pin for the whole pendulum: the motor's torque and gravity's torque, nothing else. Without gravity \(P\) would be conserved (Noether's theorem for rotations about the pin).

What \(P\) measures. On the butterfly the lower rod spins fast, and the sum \(P\) swings slowly with the upper rod, between 1.23 and 15.77 kg·m²/s each lap. Averaged over a whirl of the lower rod, the term \(U_2\sin\theta_2\) nearly cancels and (26) reads \(\dot P \approx \tau - U_1\sin\theta_1\): \(P\) is the momentum of the slow pendulum. Its largest value over a swing, \(P_{\text{reach}} = \max|P|\), measures how big the swing is; on the butterfly \(P^*_{\text{reach}} = 15.77\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\).

27Pump and steer

First give the pendulum the butterfly's energy and the butterfly's swing.

The law the motor follows in this phase is

\[ \tau = \Big(k_E\,(E^*-E) + k_I\,\tfrac{1}{20}\!\int\!(E^*-E)\,dt\Big)\,\dot\theta_1 \;+\; \Big(k_A\,(P^*_{\text{reach}}-P_{\text{reach}}) + k_I\!\int\!(P^*_{\text{reach}}-P_{\text{reach}})\,dt\Big)\,P, \tag{27} \]

clipped to \(|\tau| \le 60\ \mathrm{N\,m}\), with \(k_E = 0.01\), \(k_A = 0.05\), \(k_I = 0.005\) (SI units), \(P_{\text{reach}}\) taken over the last 3 s.

The energy term is energy shaping, the method Åström and Furuta used to swing a pendulum up. Alone, it gives by (25) \(\dot E = k_E(E^*-E)\dot\theta_1^2\). With \(V = \tfrac12(E-E^*)^2\),

\[ \dot V = (E-E^*)\,\dot E = -k_E\,(E-E^*)^2\,\dot\theta_1^2 \le 0, \tag{28} \]

so the energy error never grows and shrinks whenever the upper rod moves: a Lyapunov function. The push is in step with the rod's own motion, like the child on the swing.

The swing term pushes along \(P\) when the swing is too small and against it when too big; by (26) it feeds or brakes the slow pendulum. The integrals are memories against a steady error, as in the PID control of Fig. 9: a proportional push alone needs a leftover error to keep pushing. The two terms interact (energy given to the swing is energy taken from the spin, at fixed total), so (27) has no simple Lyapunov proof; it is tuned and measured (section 32).

When \(|E-E^*| < 5\ \mathrm{J}\) and \(|P_{\text{reach}} - P^*_{\text{reach}}| < 3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}\), and at least 3 s after the motor started, the pendulum is moving like a butterfly of nearly the right size; its phase, where it is along the loop compared with the spin, is still wrong.

28Wait, and the mirror butterfly

The motor switches off and watches.

This is the idea of Ott, Grebogi and Yorke: a motion that wanders near an unstable orbit comes close to it from time to time by itself; one waits for that moment and then uses small corrections only. Here the distance from the orbit is

\[ d(s) = \min_{k,\ \sigma=\pm1}\ \Big(\sum_{i=1}^{4} Q_i\,\big[s_i - \sigma\,s^*_i(t_k)\big]^2\Big)^{1/2},\qquad Q = (1,\ 1,\ 0.05,\ 0.05), \tag{29} \]

with the angles compared modulo \(2\pi\), over the 2400 stored points \(s^*(t_k)\) of the lap: the distance to the nearest point of the loop, whatever the timing. Angles count in radians and momenta in kg·m²/s, with the weight 0.05 because a momentum error of 1 kg·m²/s matters about as much as an angle error of 0.2 rad.

The mirror. The Hamiltonian (21) is even: \(H(-\theta,-p) = H(\theta,p)\). So if \(s(t)\) solves (22) with torque \(\tau(t)\), then \(-s(t)\) solves it with \(-\tau(t)\). The mirror butterfly \(-s^*(t)\), the same drawing flipped left to right, is an orbit just as good, held by the same gains with the torque reversed. That is the \(\sigma = \pm1\) in (29). When the distance falls below 0.6, the catch begins. If nothing comes within 0.6 in 12 s, it is a miss (section 30).

29The catch: feedback designed on the orbit★

Near the loop the motion is nearly linear, and a linear controller can be designed exactly for it.

Linearising along the orbit. Divide the lap into \(M = 2400\) steps of \(h = T/M = 1.108\ \mathrm{ms}\), the motor's control period. Over step \(k\), with the torque held constant,

\[ \delta s_{k+1} = \Phi_k\,\delta s_k + b_k\,\tau_k,\qquad \Phi_k = \frac{\partial s_{k+1}}{\partial s_k}\Big|_{s^*},\quad b_k = \frac{\partial s_{k+1}}{\partial\tau}\Big|_{s^*}. \tag{30} \]

The product \(\Phi_{M-1}\cdots\Phi_0\) is the monodromy matrix of (24). The 2400 matrices are periodic in \(k\): the problem is a linear system with periodic coefficients, the discrete twin of Floquet theory.

One motor, four directions. The controllability Gramian over one lap,

\[ W = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} \Phi_{M-1}\cdots\Phi_{k+1}\;b_k\,b_k^{\mathsf T}\;\big(\Phi_{M-1}\cdots\Phi_{k+1}\big)^{\mathsf T} h, \tag{31} \]

is the matrix whose quadratic form tells how much a unit of torque effort moves the end-of-lap state in each direction. Its eigenvalues are \(3.5\times10^{-8},\ 2.6\times10^{-7},\ 2.0\times10^{-5},\ 4.0\times10^{-5}\): all positive, so the pendulum is controllable through the top pin alone, although underactuated; the smallest belongs to a change of the energy (its eigenvector lies within 8° of the gradient of \(H\)), the costliest thing to change.

The periodic linear-quadratic regulator. Choose the torques to minimise

\[ \mathcal J = \sum_k \Big(\delta s_k^{\mathsf T}\,Q\,h\,\delta s_k + R\,\tau_k^2\Big),\qquad R = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{(N\,m)^{-2}}, \tag{32} \]

a balance between the error and the effort. Dynamic programming backwards from the end gives the Riccati recursion for a cost matrix \(\Pi_k\) and the gains,

\[ \Pi_k = Qh + \Phi_k^{\mathsf T}\Pi_{k+1}\Phi_k - \frac{\Phi_k^{\mathsf T}\Pi_{k+1}b_k\;b_k^{\mathsf T}\Pi_{k+1}\Phi_k}{R + b_k^{\mathsf T}\Pi_{k+1}b_k},\qquad K_k = \frac{b_k^{\mathsf T}\Pi_{k+1}\Phi_k}{R + b_k^{\mathsf T}\Pi_{k+1}b_k},\qquad \tau_k = -K_k\,\delta s_k . \tag{33} \]

Run backwards around the lap again and again, \(\Pi\) settles on a periodic solution (here after 5 laps); its gains \(K_k\) are the controller. With them, the closed-loop monodromy \(\prod_k(\Phi_k - b_kK_k)\) has all four multipliers of modulus 0.074 to 0.081: an error shrinks to 8 % of itself each lap, where the free orbit would have let it grow by 2 %.

Following the loop, not a clock. The reference \(s^*(t_k)\) used in \(\delta s_k = s - \sigma s^*(t_k)\) is re-chosen every step as the nearest point of the loop (29), searching 30 steps around the last one. A clock-locked reference would ask the motor to correct the timing too. The timing can only be changed by changing the energy and waiting while the period shifts, and (31) shows the energy to be the costliest direction; tried, it saturated the motor and lost the pendulum. The re-indexed reference lets the pendulum keep its own timing and corrects only the error across the loop.

How far away it can catch. Measured (section 32), from 200 random states at each distance: from 0.1 the catch holds 199 times, from 0.2 171, from 0.3 140, from 0.4 112, from 0.6 62, from 0.8 26. The threshold 0.6 is a trade. A catch tried from that far holds about one time in three, and a slip (distance above 1) is a miss and a new try; but a closer pass is rarer, and waiting for it costs more time than the lost tries (with the threshold at 0.2 only 29 of 40 starts were caught within 400 s).

30Hold, let go, and miss

The motor holds the pendulum at least four laps and until it is within 0.005 of the loop, then switches off.

Held, the corrections shrink below 0.1 N·m; released, the pendulum is free and keeps drawing the butterfly by the slow instability of section 25. Measured on 300 released runs: within 0.005 of the loop at the release by this rule, and four free laps later 0.0017 in the median, nine in ten below 0.016, the worst 0.058.

A miss (steering not settled within 12 s, nothing near within 12 s of waiting, or a catch that slips past 1) makes the motor drain the pendulum back down to 60 J with the energy law (27) aimed at 60 J (at most 6 s), and start again. At 60 J the double pendulum is chaotic, and chaos scrambles the next approach: each try is a fresh draw from the many ways of having the right energy and swing. The search is a sequence of independent tries, each with a fair chance.

34Limits and the next model

An ideal pendulum and an ideal motor.

The pins have no friction; the motor makes exactly the torque asked, at once, held over each 1.1 ms; the controller knows the state exactly. A real machine needs: friction, which at 9 whirls a second would drain the butterfly within seconds and make the motor work all the time, not only to catch; the motor's electrical dynamics (inductance, back-emf, \(V = R_mI + \Lambda\dot I + k_t\dot\theta_1\), as in Fig. 9) between the command and the torque; measurement, and an observer that estimates \(p_1, p_2\) from encoder angles; and limits on speed. The method has no proof of global success: the measured catch rate is a statistic over 100 starts, not a theorem. Natural next steps are a transverse (orbital) linearisation that removes the timing direction from the design itself (Shiriaev and co-workers), and model-predictive control that plans the approach over a lap instead of waiting for it.

  3. Start from Hamilton's equations with the torque \(\tau\) added to \(\dot p_1\), and derive \(\dot H = \tau\,\dot\theta_1\) in three lines. Which terms cancel, and why does the torque on the upper rod alone still change the energy of both rods?
