Lecture note · Fig. 12 · fluid dynamics · vehicle dynamics · control

Why the pint spills, and how a motor can stop it

Numa Koudsie · for physics undergraduates; ★ marks the heavier mathematics, whose results are restated without it

Abstract. A pint of stout in the Guinness tulip glass rides a cart with one wheel, one spring and one damper over a bump. The road pushes on the wheel along the normal to its surface, which leans backward while the wheel climbs and forward while it descends, so the cart is braked and then shoved. In the frame of the glass that jolt is an extra horizontal force on every parcel of stout, and the stout answers by swinging at its own frequency, about 3.4 times a second, sometimes over the rim. We write the stout’s equations in the glass’s frame, solve the linear problem for an upright cylinder (Bessel functions, the dispersion relation \(\omega^2 = (g\xi/R)\tanh(\xi h/R)\), the equivalent spring and mass), build the cart as a quarter-car with a DC gear motor, a PI speed loop and a skyhook suspension, couple the two through the stout’s momentum, and explain the numerical method the page uses: a particle–grid solution of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in three dimensions. The figure runs live, in real time, on a reduced model of the stout: its sloshing modes with the nonlinear terms of Narimanov–Moiseev theory, whose coefficients we derive, over the whole glass, so that the swing slows as it grows and can swirl, checked against the 3D solver; the full 3D solution is one switch away. Two further controllers hold the stout still: a feedforward that reads the road ahead, and a state feedback on the stout’s swing designed by the linear–quadratic regulator, which sees the stout only through two level probes on the glass’s wall and a Kalman filter, and so acts on the 3D fluid itself, in slow motion. Every number in the note was computed or measured from the page’s own code, with the command that reproduces it.

01Over the doorstep

Carry a full pint across a room and step over a doorstep. Lifting the glass straight up and setting it down spills almost nothing. What spills it is the moment your hand is checked or shoved along the floor.

Watch the stout when a bus you are standing in brakes hard with a drink in your hand. The dark liquid climbs the front of the glass as if the glass had been tipped forward, even though you held it upright; then it runs back, climbs the back wall, and rocks to and fro on its own for a second or two after the bus has stopped. You feel the same push in your own body: you lean back when the bus starts and are thrown forward when it brakes. Inside the glass every parcel of stout feels that push too, and the free surface tilts to stay at right angles to the combined pull of gravity and the push, like the bubble of a spirit level.

Two facts about that rocking decide whether the pint spills. It has a rhythm of its own, about three and a half swings a second for a pint, set by the glass and by gravity, not by your hand. And it is excited most strongly by a jolt that lasts about as long as one swing. A cart rolling at 1 m/s over a bump 25 cm long is jolted for a quarter of a second, and one swing of a pint lasts about 0.3 s: the bump is almost tuned to the stout. A careful waiter does two things: keeps the hand moving at a steady speed whatever the floor does, and lets the arm give under the up-and-down of each step. The cart in the figure does both, with a motor that holds its speed and a suspension that gives.

This note builds the three parts of the figure in turn and then joins them: the stout (sections 3 to 5), the cart with its motor, controller and suspension (sections 6 to 9), and the coupling through which each pushes the other (section 10). Section 11 explains how the computer solves the stout’s equations, section 12 how we know the answers are right, and section 13 what a small set of runs teaches. Nothing in the picture is drawn from a formula for waves: the stout is computed from the laws of motion of a liquid, and the cart from Newton’s laws.

02The glass and the stout

The Guinness pint is the tulip glass: narrow at the foot, swelling to its widest about three quarters of the way up, and closing a little towards the rim. Retailers list it as 160 mm tall and 85 mm across at the widest, an imperial pint of 568 ml [1]. No dimensioned drawing of that glass was found; the closest is dimensions.com’s section of the same tulip as a 16 oz glass, 149.2 mm tall and 76.2 mm (3 in) across [2]. Its inner outline was measured from the drawing (the widest outer diameter measures 76.6 mm against the 76.2 mm the drawing states) and stretched to the Guinness size: heights by 160/149.2, radii so that the widest outer diameter is 85 mm (tools/fig12/glass_profile.py).

We use cylindrical coordinates fixed to the glass: \(z\) up from the floor of the cavity, \(r\) out from the glass’s axis, and the angle \(\theta\) measured from the direction of travel \(x\); \(y\) is across the road. The glass is a surface of revolution, described by its inner radius \(R(z)\), stored every 0.5 mm (Figure 1). It is 16.3 mm at the floor, where the corner is rounded, 28.0 mm at \(z = 20\) mm, widest at 39.3 mm about 116 mm up, and 37.5 mm at the rim, \(z_\text{rim} = 147.5\) mm. Brim-full it holds 530.7 ml, less than an imperial pint: the drawing is the smaller US glass stretched, not the imperial glass itself, and that difference is the uncertainty in the shape. The stout is poured to \(h = 125\) mm, where the radius is \(R(h) = 39.1\) mm; its mass is \(m_\ell = \rho\int_0^h \pi R(z)^2\,dz = 426.6\) g with the density \(\rho = 1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\).

The page computes the stout in two ways at once (section 11 gives the details). The liquid is carried by about 25 000 small particles that move with it, and the pressure and velocity are computed on a grid of cubic cells of side \(\Delta x\), 4 mm by default or 3 mm on request, laid over the half of the glass on one side of the direction of travel. The computation advances in time steps \(\Delta t\) of at most 5 ms. A finer grid is closer to the true solution and slower; the grid’s error on the swing frequency is 4–6 % (section 12).

03The stout in the glass’s frame

We describe the stout from the glass, as a passenger would, because the walls are then at rest and the boundary conditions are simple. The price is one extra force.

3.1Newton’s law in a frame that translates

Let \(\mathbf X(t)\) be the position of a point fixed to the glass, measured in the room, and \(\mathbf a_g = \ddot{\mathbf X}\) the glass’s acceleration. A parcel of stout at room position \(\mathbf x_\text{room}\) has position \(\mathbf x = \mathbf x_\text{room} - \mathbf X(t)\) relative to the glass. The glass never turns: the model lets the body slide only straight up and down on the axle and does not let the cart pitch or roll (a modelling assumption; a real one-wheeled cart would need something to hold it upright). The axes fixed to the glass therefore stay parallel to the room’s, and differentiating twice gives simply

\[ \ddot{\mathbf x}_\text{room} = \ddot{\mathbf x} + \mathbf a_g(t) . \tag{1} \]

Newton’s law, which holds in the room, \(m\ddot{\mathbf x}_\text{room} = \mathbf F\), becomes in the glass’s frame

\[ m\ddot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf F - m\,\mathbf a_g(t) . \tag{2} \]

The last term is the fictitious force: it acts on every parcel in proportion to its mass, exactly like gravity, and points against the glass’s acceleration. Had the glass turned with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), the derivative of a rotating unit vector would have added the centrifugal force \(-m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf x)\), the Coriolis force \(-2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\dot{\mathbf x}\) and the Euler force \(-m\,\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf x\). With \(\boldsymbol\Omega = 0\) all three vanish, and (2) is exact. Since gravity is also a force per unit mass, the two combine into one apparent gravity

\[ \mathbf g_\text{eff}(t) = \mathbf g - \mathbf a_g(t), \qquad \mathbf g = (0, 0, -g),\ g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} . \tag{3} \]

A braking cart has \(a_{g,x} \lt 0\), so \(\mathbf g_\text{eff}\) leans forward: the stout behaves as if the glass had been tipped forward, which is what the bus showed us. The vertical part \(a_{g,z}\) makes the stout lighter or heavier for a moment, as in a lift.

3.2The equations of the stout

Let \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x, t)\) be the velocity of the stout relative to the glass, \(p\) its pressure and \(\nu\) its kinematic viscosity. Applying (2) to a small parcel of volume \(dV\), mass \(\rho\,dV\), on which the surrounding stout exerts the pressure force \(-\nabla p\,dV\) and the viscous force \(\rho\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u\,dV\), and dividing by \(\rho\,dV\), gives the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in the glass’s frame:

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \mathbf g - \mathbf a_g(t), \tag{4} \] \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0 . \tag{5} \]

The left side of (4) is the acceleration of a parcel followed as it moves: \(\partial\mathbf u/\partial t\) is the change at a fixed point and \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\) the change the parcel sees by moving to where the velocity is different. Equation (5) says the stout cannot be compressed: the volume flowing into any small region equals the volume flowing out. For a liquid moving at centimetres per second this is excellent; the speed of sound in it is about 1.5 km/s. The page takes \(\rho = 1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) and \(\nu = 1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), the values of water; those of stout were not measured for this page. Note that \(\mathbf a_g\) depends on time only, not on position: it is the same at every point of the glass.

3.3Boundary conditions

At the wall and the floor the stout cannot flow through the glass. With \(\mathbf n\) the outward unit normal of the wall,

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n = 0 \quad\text{on } r = R(z) \text{ and on } z = 0,\qquad \mathbf n \propto (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta,\ -R'(z)) . \tag{6} \]

The normal leans down where the wall leans out (\(R' \gt 0\)) and up where it closes in. A real viscous liquid also sticks to the wall, \(\mathbf u = 0\) there (no slip). The page imposes only (6) and lets the stout slide freely along the wall (free slip). Section 5 shows why that is a fair approximation for the swing and what it costs in damping.

At the free surface, the moving boundary \(z = h + \eta(x, y, t)\) between stout and air, two conditions hold. The surface moves with the liquid (the kinematic condition: a parcel on the surface stays on it), and the pressure equals that of the air (the dynamic condition). The air’s pressure is uniform and its gradient pushes on nothing, so we measure pressure from it:

\[ p = 0 \quad\text{on the free surface.} \tag{7} \]

We neglect surface tension here; section 5 shows that it shifts the swing frequency by under 1 %. Above the rim there is no wall at all: stout that rises over it leaves the glass.

3.4Mirror symmetry

The cart moves in the \(x\)–\(z\) plane, so \(\mathbf a_g\) has no \(y\) part, and the glass is a surface of revolution. Reflect a solution in the vertical plane of travel, \(y \to -y\), with \(v \to -v\) for the velocity component across. Every term of (4)–(7) keeps its form, so the reflected field is again a solution with the same forcing. If the stout starts symmetric, at rest, and the solution is unique, it stays symmetric: \(v = 0\) on \(y = 0\), and only the half \(y \ge 0\) need be computed. The page does exactly that. What this cannot show is a symmetric motion that becomes unstable and breaks the symmetry, as when a sloshing liquid starts to swirl around the glass; those motions are absent from the page by construction (section 15).

3.5The steady push: a tilted surface

Suppose the glass accelerates steadily, \(\mathbf a_g = (a, 0, 0)\) with constant \(a\), and the stout has come to rest relative to it, \(\mathbf u = 0\). Then (4) reduces to hydrostatics in the apparent gravity, \(\nabla p = \rho(\mathbf g - \mathbf a_g)\), that is \(\partial p/\partial x = -\rho a\) and \(\partial p/\partial z = -\rho g\). Integrating,

\[ p(x, z) = \rho\,\big[g\,(h_c - z) - a\,x\big], \tag{8} \]

with \(h_c\) a constant. The surface \(p = 0\) is the plane \(z = h_c - (a/g)\,x\): it tilts with slope \(-a/g\), at right angles to \(\mathbf g_\text{eff}\). The volume is conserved, and for a small slope the wedge that rises on one side equals the wedge that sinks on the other, so \(h_c = h\), the depth at rest. This holds for any glass shape, because (8) never mentioned the wall.

How far does the stout’s centre of mass move? To first order in the slope the stout is the stout at rest plus a thin layer of thickness \(\eta = -(a/g)\,x\) laid over the surface disc of radius \(R(h)\) (negative thickness where the surface sank). Its centre of mass moves along \(x\) by

\[ \bar x = \frac{1}{V}\int_\text{disc} x\,\eta\,dA = -\frac{a}{g}\,\frac{1}{V}\int_\text{disc} x^2\,dA = -\frac{a}{g}\,\frac{I}{V}, \qquad I = \int_\text{disc} x^2\,dA = \frac{\pi R(h)^4}{4}, \tag{9} \]

where \(V\) is the volume of stout and \(I\) the second moment of the surface disc about its diameter. For a straight cylinder, \(V = \pi R^2 h\) and \(\bar x = -(a/g)\,R^2/(4h)\).

Key result · the quasi-static tilt

Under a steady acceleration \(a\) of the glass, the stout comes to rest with a plane surface of slope \(-a/g\), and its centre of mass moves by \(\bar x = -(a/g)\,\pi R(h)^4/(4V)\), whatever the glass’s shape. These are the two yardsticks tools/fig12/check.cjs tilt and cyltilt test the solver against.

Example 3.1 · the tulip under a push of 2 m/s²

With \(a = 2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) the slope is \(a/g = 0.204\), an angle of 11.5°. At the wall, \(R(h) = 39.1\) mm out, the stout rises by \(R(h)\,a/g = 7.97\) mm and sinks as much on the other side. With \(V = 426.6\) ml, (9) gives \(\bar x = -0.204\times\pi\,(0.0391\ \mathrm m)^4/(4\times 4.266\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3}) = -0.877\) mm. The centre of mass moves by less than a millimetre while the wall height changes by 16 mm: the tilt moves only a thin wedge. For the straight test glass, \(R = 40\) mm and \(h = 100\) mm, \(\bar x = -0.204\times 0.040^2/(4\times 0.100) = -0.815\) mm. (node tools/fig12/theory.cjs.)

04Linear sloshing in an upright cylinder ★

A steady push tilts the surface; a push that changes sets it swinging. For a straight cylinder the swing can be found exactly when it is small. The tulip has no such solution, but the cylinder tells us what to expect of it.

4.1Potential flow

Three approximations make the problem solvable. (i) The swing is slow and wide compared with the thin layer at the wall where viscosity acts (section 5 measures it: 0.3 mm against a radius of 40 mm), so we drop viscosity, \(\nu = 0\). (ii) The motion is small: the surface displacement \(\eta\) is small compared with the radius, so we keep only terms of first order in \(\eta\) and \(\mathbf u\). (iii) The stout starts at rest. The forcing \(-\mathbf a_g(t)\) is uniform in space, so it is the gradient of \(-\mathbf a_g\cdot\mathbf x\) and exerts no torque on any parcel about its centre: by Kelvin’s circulation theorem an inviscid liquid set in motion from rest by such forces stays irrotational, \(\nabla\times\mathbf u = 0\). Then the velocity is the gradient of a velocity potential \(\phi\), \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), and (5) becomes Laplace’s equation:

\[ \nabla^2\phi = 0 . \tag{10} \]

For a straight cylinder of radius \(R\) filled to depth \(h\), the conditions at the wall and floor, (6), read \(\partial\phi/\partial r = 0\) at \(r = R\) and \(\partial\phi/\partial z = 0\) at \(z = 0\).

4.2The linearised surface conditions

With \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) and \(\nu = 0\), equation (4) can be integrated in space, using \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = \nabla(\tfrac12|\mathbf u|^2)\) for an irrotational flow. The result is Bernoulli’s equation in the glass’s frame,

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2 + \frac{p}{\rho} + g z + \mathbf a_g\cdot\mathbf x = B(t), \tag{11} \]

where \(B(t)\), a function of time alone, can be absorbed into \(\phi\). On the surface \(z = h + \eta\), \(p = 0\), and \(\mathbf a_g\cdot\mathbf x = a\,x + a_z(h + \eta)\) with the horizontal push \(a(t) = a_{g,x}\) and the vertical \(a_z(t) = a_{g,z}\). Keep the terms of first order in the small quantities \(\eta\) and \(\phi\), evaluate them at \(z = h\) instead of \(z = h + \eta\) (the difference is of second order), and choose \(B\) so that the stout at rest is undisturbed: this gives the dynamic condition

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \big(g + a_z(t)\big)\,\eta + a(t)\,x = 0 \quad\text{at } z = h . \tag{12} \]

The kinematic condition says the surface rises at the speed of the liquid under it: \(\partial\eta/\partial t = \partial\phi/\partial z\) at \(z = h\). For the free swing below we may take \(a_z = 0\); then differentiating (12) in time and substituting removes \(\eta\):

\[ \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2} + g\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z} = -\dot a(t)\,x \quad\text{at } z = h . \tag{13} \]

The vertical acceleration multiplies \(\eta\): it is first order in the swing, like gravity, and it acts as a change of gravity, \(g \to g + a_z\). It cannot start a swing of a level surface, but it changes the frequency of one that exists. It is not small on this page: over a 10 mm bump \(a_z/g\) reaches 0.38, over a 25 mm bump with speed control 0.96 (section 13). For a steady \(a\) and \(a_z = 0\), (12) gives back the tilt \(\eta = -(a/g)\,x\) of section 3.5.

4.3Separation of variables

Take first the free swing, \(a = 0\), and look for a mode that oscillates at one frequency \(\omega\): \(\phi = \Phi(r, \theta, z)\cos\omega t\). In cylindrical coordinates Laplace’s equation separates with \(\Phi = f(r)\cos(m\theta)\,Z(z)\). The angular factor must repeat after a full turn, so \(m\) is a whole number; a push along \(x = r\cos\theta\) excites \(m = 1\), the mode whose surface is high on one side and low on the other. Substituting,

\[ \frac{1}{f}\Big(f'' + \frac{f'}{r} - \frac{m^2}{r^2}f\Big) = -\frac{Z''}{Z} = -k^2 , \tag{14} \]

with a separation constant \(k\), the wavenumber. The vertical factor obeys \(Z'' = k^2 Z\) with \(Z'(0) = 0\) at the floor: \(Z = \cosh kz\), which grows towards the surface, so the motion is strongest near the surface and dies away below it over a depth of about \(1/k\). The radial factor obeys Bessel’s equation of order \(m\); the solution finite on the axis is \(f = J_m(kr)\). No flow through the wall, \(f'(R) = 0\), gives

\[ J_m'(kR) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad k_{mn} = \frac{\xi_{mn}}{R}, \qquad \xi_{11} = 1.8412 , \tag{15} \]

where \(\xi_{mn}\) is the \(n\)-th zero of \(J_m'\). The first, \(\xi_{11} = 1.84118\), is computed by tools/fig12/theory.cjs from the power series of \(J_0\) and \(J_1\) (\(J_1' = J_0 - J_1/x\)). The surface of the mode is \(\eta \propto J_1(\xi_{11}r/R)\cos\theta\): it is high at the front wall and low at the back, with a node line across the middle.

4.4The dispersion relation

Put \(\phi = J_1(kr)\cos\theta\cosh(kz)\cos\omega t\) into (13) with \(\dot a = 0\): \(-\omega^2\cosh kh + g k\sinh kh = 0\). Hence

\[ \omega_{11}^2 = \frac{g\,\xi_{11}}{R}\tanh\frac{\xi_{11}h}{R}, \qquad f_{11} = \frac{\omega_{11}}{2\pi} . \tag{16} \]
Key result · the first sloshing frequency of a cylinder

A straight upright cylinder of radius \(R\) filled to depth \(h\) swings, for small motion, at \(f_{11} = (2\pi)^{-1}\sqrt{(g\xi_{11}/R)\tanh(\xi_{11}h/R)}\) with \(\xi_{11} = 1.8412\), the first zero of \(J_1'\). This is Abramson [8], eq. (2.19), and Lamb [9], Art. 257, eq. (4), with the radial factor of Art. 191.

Checks. The units: \(g\xi/R\) is an acceleration over a length, \(\mathrm{s^{-2}}\). Deep liquid, \(h \gg R\): \(\tanh \to 1\) and \(\omega^2 = gk\), the dispersion relation of deep-water waves, with the wavelength fixed by the glass. The frequency then does not depend on the depth, because the motion lives within about \(R/\xi\) of the surface. Shallow liquid, \(h \ll R\): \(\tanh x \approx x\) and \(\omega = k\sqrt{gh}\), a long wave travelling at the shallow-water speed \(\sqrt{gh}\). A wide glass swings slowly, as \(R^{-1/2}\) when deep: a bucket rocks more lazily than a cup.

Example 4.1 · the test cylinder

The page’s solver is checked on a straight glass with \(R = 40\) mm and \(h = 100\) mm. Then \(\xi h/R = 4.60\), \(\tanh 4.60 = 0.9998\): the stout is effectively deep. Equation (16) gives \(\omega^2 = 9.81\times1.8412/0.040\times0.9998 = 451.5\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), \(\omega = 21.25\) rad/s, \(f_{11} = 3.382\) Hz, a period of 0.296 s. (node tools/fig12/theory.cjs.)

4.5The forced swing and the equivalent spring and mass

Now let the glass be pushed, \(a(t) \ne 0\). Describe the first mode by the height \(q(t)\) of the surface at the front wall, \(\eta = q(t)\,J_1(kr)\cos\theta/J_1(\xi)\), with \(k = \xi/R\) and \(\xi = \xi_{11}\). The kinematic condition gives the potential, \(\phi = \dot q\,J_1(kr)\cos\theta\cosh kz/\big(J_1(\xi)\,k\sinh kh\big)\). Put both into the dynamic condition (12), multiply by \(J_1(kr)\cos\theta\) and integrate over the surface disc. At \(z = h\) the three terms of (12) are \(\partial_t\phi = \ddot q\,\coth(kh)\,J_1(kr)\cos\theta/(k\,J_1(\xi))\), \(g\eta = g\,q\,J_1(kr)\cos\theta/J_1(\xi)\) and \(a\,x = a\,r\cos\theta\). The angular integral gives \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi\) in every term and cancels. Two radial integrals remain. The first is Lommel’s, \(\int_0^R J_1(kr)^2\,r\,dr = \tfrac12R^2\big[J_1'(\xi)^2 + (1 - \xi^{-2})J_1(\xi)^2\big] = \tfrac12R^2(1 - \xi^{-2})J_1(\xi)^2\), using \(J_1'(\xi) = 0\). The second follows from \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\big[x^2J_2(x)\big] = x^2J_1(x)\): \(\int_0^R r^2J_1(kr)\,dr = R^2J_2(\xi)/k = R^3J_1(\xi)/\xi^2\), using \(J_2(\xi) = 2J_1(\xi)/\xi - J_0(\xi) = J_1(\xi)/\xi\) at a zero of \(J_1' = J_0 - J_1/x\). So

\[ \Big(\frac{\coth kh}{k}\,\ddot q + g\,q\Big)\frac{R^2(1 - \xi^{-2})J_1(\xi)}{2} = -a\,\frac{R^3J_1(\xi)}{\xi^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\coth kh}{k}\,\ddot q + g\,q = -\frac{2R}{\xi^2 - 1}\,a . \]

The other modes, \(J_1(\xi_{1n}r/R)\) with \(n \ge 2\), are orthogonal to this one over the disc (they are eigenfunctions of the same Sturm–Liouville problem with the same condition \(f'(R) = 0\)), so their terms drop out of the projection; the push \(a\,x\) excites them too, each in its own equation. Multiplying by \(k\tanh kh\), with \(kR = \xi\), gives one forced oscillator for the first mode:

\[ \ddot q + \omega_{11}^2\Big(1 + \frac{a_z(t)}{g}\Big)\,q = -\beta\,a(t), \qquad \beta = \frac{2\xi\tanh(\xi h/R)}{\xi^2 - 1} . \tag{17} \]

(The term in \(a_z\) comes from carrying \(g + a_z\) of (12) through the same projection.) Equation (17) is the whole story of the doorstep in one line. A steady push gives \(q = -(2R/(\xi^2 - 1))\,a/g\): the first mode carries \(2/(\xi^2 - 1) = 0.837\) of the wall height of the exact tilt \(-R\,a/g\), the higher modes the rest. A push that changes on the time scale \(1/\omega_{11}\) excites the swing most, and the swing then continues on its own.

How much is left swinging after a jolt follows from (17) with \(a_z = 0\). Start at rest, let the jolt \(a(t)\) last a finite time, and solve by Green’s function (Duhamel’s integral): \(q(t) = -(\beta/\omega_{11})\int_{-\infty}^t a(t')\sin\omega_{11}(t - t')\,dt'\). Once the jolt is over the upper limit can be taken to infinity, and with \(\sin\omega(t - t') = \operatorname{Im}\,e^{j\omega(t - t')}\),

\[ q(t) = -\frac{\beta}{\omega_{11}}\operatorname{Im}\big[e^{j\omega_{11}t}\,\hat a(\omega_{11})\big] , \tag{17a} \]

where \(\hat a(\omega) = \int a(t)\,e^{-j\omega t}\,dt\) is the Fourier transform of the jolt.

Key result · what a jolt leaves behind

After a jolt the first mode swings with amplitude \((\beta/\omega_{11})\,|\hat a(\omega_{11})|\), the Fourier amplitude of the glass’s acceleration at the swing’s own frequency. The stout picks out of the jolt only the part at its own frequency; the rest of the jolt leaves nothing behind.

The stout pushes back on the glass through its momentum relative to the glass, \(\mathbf P = \int\rho\,\mathbf u\,dV\) (section 10). For the mode, \(u_x = \partial_x\phi\), and since \(\partial_x\phi = \nabla\cdot(\phi\,\mathbf e_x)\), Gauss’s theorem turns the volume integral into one over the liquid’s boundary: \(P_x = \rho\int\partial_x\phi\,dV = \rho\oint\phi\,n_x\,dS\). The floor contributes nothing, because there \(n_x = 0\). Neither does the surface to first order: its normal tilts by the small slope of \(\eta\), so \(n_x\) is itself of first order and \(\phi\,n_x\) of second. On the wall \(r = R\), \(n_x = \cos\theta\), \(dS = R\,d\theta\,dz\), and \(J_1(kR)/J_1(\xi) = 1\), so

\[ P_x = \frac{\rho R\,\dot q}{k\sinh kh}\,I_\theta\,I_z , \qquad I_\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\cos^2\theta\,d\theta, \quad I_z = \int_0^h\!\cosh kz\,dz , \] \[ \phantom{P_x} = \rho\,\dot q\,R\cdot\pi\cdot\frac{1}{k\sinh kh}\cdot\frac{\sinh kh}{k} = \frac{\rho\pi R}{k^2}\,\dot q , \]

using \(I_\theta = \pi\) and \(I_z = \sinh(kh)/k\). With \(k = \xi/R\):

\[ P_x = \frac{\rho\pi R^3}{\xi^2}\,\dot q , \tag{18} \]

independent of the depth: the \(\sinh kh\) of the potential’s normalisation cancels that of the depth integral. Now compare a mass \(m_1\) on a spring inside the glass, displaced by \(x_1\) relative to the glass and obeying \(\ddot x_1 + \omega_{11}^2x_1 = -a\) (it feels the same fictitious force). Its relative momentum is \(m_1\dot x_1\). With \(q = \beta x_1\) the two systems have the same equation of motion and the same momentum if \(m_1 = \beta\rho\pi R^3/\xi^2\):

\[ \frac{m_1}{m_\ell} = \frac{2R}{\xi(\xi^2 - 1)\,h}\tanh\frac{\xi h}{R}, \qquad m_\ell = \rho\pi R^2 h . \tag{19} \]
Key result · the equivalent spring and mass

As far as its push on the glass is concerned, the first sloshing mode of a cylinder is a mass \(m_1\) on a spring of frequency \(\omega_{11}\), given by (19); the rest of the stout, \(m_\ell - m_1\), moves rigidly with the glass. This is the mechanical model used for sloshing in rocket tanks; (19) is Abramson’s eq. (6.27) and Table 6.1 [8], written there with the diameter \(d = 2R\). For deep liquid \(m_1 \to 2\pi\rho R^3/(\xi(\xi^2 - 1))\): the swinging mass depends only on the surface radius.

Example 4.2 · how much of the pint swings, and how hard it pushes

For the test cylinder, (19) gives \(m_1/m_\ell = 0.182\): 91.4 g of the 502.7 g swing. The tulip is deep, \(h/R(h) = 3.2\), and near its surface it is nearly straight (\(R'(h) = -0.04\)), so we estimate its swinging mass with the deep-cylinder formula and the surface radius 39.1 mm: \(m_1 \approx 85\) g, 20 % of the pint. 05Dimensionless numbers and damping

Which forces matter is decided by ratios. Each ratio below uses this page’s numbers.

What section 4 found, for a reader who skipped it. A straight glass of radius \(R\) filled to depth \(h\) swings at \(f_{11} = (2\pi)^{-1}\sqrt{(g\xi_{11}/R)\tanh(\xi_{11}h/R)}\), \(\xi_{11} = 1.8412\): 3.382 Hz for the test glass, \(R = 40\) mm, \(h = 100\) mm, eq. (16). The swing behaves like a mass on a spring, (17), driven by the glass’s horizontal acceleration; after a jolt it keeps the part of the jolt at its own frequency, (17a). As far as its push on the glass is concerned, about 85 g of the pint’s 427 g swing, the rest moves with the glass, eq. (19). The tulip has no formula; the page computes 3.21 Hz on its 4 mm grid and 3.26 Hz on its 3 mm grid, and correcting for the grid’s error suggests about 3.41 Hz.

The push against gravity. The ratio of the glass’s horizontal acceleration to gravity, \(\mathrm{Fr} = a/g\), a Froude number for the forcing, is the slope the surface would take if the push were steady, by section 3.5. Over a 10 mm bump in open loop the largest \(a\) is 1.24 m/s² (section 13), \(\mathrm{Fr} = 0.13\): small, so linear theory is a fair guide. Over a 25 mm bump in open loop it is 3.58 m/s², \(\mathrm{Fr} = 0.36\), well outside it, and the stout spills.

Surface tension against gravity. Surface tension \(\sigma\) adds a restoring pressure \(\sigma k^2\eta\) to the gravity’s \(\rho g\eta\) for a wave of wavenumber \(k\), so (16) becomes \(\omega^2 = (gk + \sigma k^3/\rho)\tanh kh\). Their ratio is the Bond number, \(\mathrm{Bo} = \rho g/(\sigma k^2) = \rho g R^2/(\sigma\xi^2)\). With \(\sigma = 0.072\) N/m (water’s) and \(R = 40\) mm, \(\mathrm{Bo} = 64.3\); the frequency rises by the factor \(\sqrt{1 + 1/\mathrm{Bo}} = 1.0077\), 0.77 % (0.78 % to first order, \(1/(2\mathrm{Bo})\)). For the tulip’s surface radius, 0.81 %. The capillary length \(\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho g)} = 2.7\) mm, much less than the glass, says the same. The page leaves surface tension out.

Viscosity, and the boundary layer. The Reynolds number of the swing, \(\mathrm{Re} = \omega R^2/\nu\), compares inertia with viscous friction over the glass. With \(\omega = 2\pi\times3.263\ \mathrm{Hz} = 20.5\) rad/s and \(R = 39.1\) mm, \(\mathrm{Re} = 3.1\times10^4\). Viscosity therefore matters only in a thin layer at the wall and floor where the no-slip condition forces the velocity to zero. For a wall oscillating at \(\omega\) under a liquid, Stokes’s second problem gives that layer’s thickness, \(\delta = \sqrt{2\nu/\omega} = 0.31\) mm: one thirteenth of a 4 mm cell. The energy lost in the layer per cycle, compared with the energy of the swing, scales as \(\delta/R\), so the damping ratio of the swing scales as

\[ \zeta \sim \sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\omega R^2}} = \mathrm{Re}^{-1/2} . \tag{20} \]

With this page’s numbers \(\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2} = 0.0056\). For a deep cylinder the boundary-layer theory gives the logarithmic decrement \(\delta_\text{log} = K\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\), with \(K = 3.52\) from the laminar boundary layer (Miles, as quoted by Stephens, Leonard and Perry [10], eq. (3)) and \(K = 5.23\) from their experiments [10]; Abramson [8], eq. (4.10b), gives the same, and the damping ratio is \(\zeta = \delta_\text{log}/2\pi\) (eq. (4.4)). The group \(\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\) is (20) with \(\omega^2 = g/R\), apart from the factor \(\xi\). For the tulip’s surface radius it is 0.0064, so \(\zeta = 0.0036\) by the theory and \(0.0053\) by experiment, for a clean liquid in a smooth glass.

What the solver does. The page does not resolve this layer: the cell is 13 times thicker, and the wall is free-slip. The swing it computes is damped instead by the numerical method itself (interpolation between particles and grid, section 11). Measured from the decay of the free swing, the cylinder’s damping ratio is 0.026, 0.016, 0.005 and 0.005 on grids of 5, 4, 3 and 2.5 mm; the tulip’s 0.030, 0.008, 0.002 and 0.005 (section 12). These are of the same order as (20) by coincidence, not by design. A real pint is also damped by its foam head, which this model leaves out and which was not measured for this page.

06The cart as a quarter-car

Ride a bicycle over a kerb-side ramp and you feel two things: the handlebars kick back as the wheel climbs, and the saddle heaves you up. The first is the road pushing backward along its sloping surface; the second is the tyre and spring lifting you. The cart has both.

The model is what vehicle engineers call a quarter-car: one wheel, the mass it carries, a spring and a damper between them, a tyre between wheel and road. It has three coordinates: the position \(x\) of the axle along the road, the height \(z_b\) of the body’s bottom (the body carries the glass), and the height \(z_w\) of the axle. The body slides straight up and down on the axle, so it never pitches.

6.1The path of the axle

The road is flat with cosine bumps, \(z_r(x) = \tfrac12 h_b\big(1 + \cos(2\pi(x - x_c)/L)\big)\) for \(|x - x_c| \lt L/2\), of height \(h_b\) (0 to 25 mm on the slider) and length \(L = 25\) cm, one every 2 m. A rigid wheel of radius \(R_w = 40\) mm whose axle is at \(x\) can sit no lower than the highest point at which its rim touches the road:

\[ z_a(x) = \max_{|s - x| \le R_w}\Big[z_r(s) + \sqrt{R_w^2 - (s - x)^2}\Big] . \tag{21} \]

This curve, the envelope of circles of radius \(R_w\) rolled along the road, is the path the axle of an undeformed wheel follows. Let \(s^*\) be the point of contact, where the maximum is attained. Because the bracket is stationary in \(s\) there, differentiating (21) in \(x\) needs only the explicit \(x\): \(z_a'(x) = (s^* - x)/\sqrt{R_w^2 - (s^* - x)^2}\). The vector from the contact point to the axle is \((x - s^*,\ \sqrt{R_w^2 - (s^* - x)^2})\), and its scalar product with the path’s tangent \((1, z_a')\) is \((x - s^*) + (s^* - x) = 0\):

(22)   The line from the contact point to the axle is normal to the axle path \(z_a(x)\).

For this page’s bumps the road’s smallest radius of curvature, \(L^2/(2\pi^2 h_b) = 127\) mm at 25 mm, exceeds \(R_w\), so the wheel always touches at one point and \(z_a\) is the road displaced by \(R_w\) along its normal; its steepest slope equals the road’s, \(\pi h_b/L = 0.126\) for a 10 mm bump. For a sharp step, such as a doorstep, the maximum in (21) produces the arc the axle describes as the wheel pivots on the edge; the page computes (21) on a 0.5 mm table in either case (Road.build).

6.2The tyre and the road’s push

The tyre is a stiff radial spring \(k_t = 2\times10^4\) N/m with damping \(c_t = 5\) N s/m. Its load, the vertical part of the road’s force on the wheel, is

\[ N = \max\big(0,\ k_t(z_a - z_w) + c_t(\dot z_a - \dot z_w)\big), \qquad \dot z_a = z_a'(x)\,\dot x , \tag{23} \]

zero when the wheel leaves the road: the road can push but not pull. By (22) the road’s force points along the normal of the axle path, which leans back by the angle \(\alpha\) with \(\tan\alpha = z_a'\). A force of vertical part \(N\) along that line has the horizontal part

\[ F_\text{road} = -N\,z_a'(x) , \tag{24} \]

backward while the wheel climbs (\(z_a' \gt 0\)), forward on the way down. That is the jolt, and it is the main thing that rocks the stout.

6.3The equations of motion

The wheel rolls without slipping, turning at \(\omega_w = \dot x/R_w\), and the motor’s rotor turns \(N_g = 10\) times faster through the gearbox, \(\omega_m = N_g\dot x/R_w\). (On a slope the wheel’s rim travels \(\sqrt{1 + z_a'^2}\) times further than the axle moves along \(x\); the page neglects this, an error of \(z_a'^2/2 \le 0.8\,\%\) on a 10 mm bump.) The kinetic energy is

\[ T = \tfrac12(M_s + m_w)\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 I_w\Big(\frac{\dot x}{R_w}\Big)^2 + \tfrac12 J_m\Big(\frac{N_g\dot x}{R_w}\Big)^2 + \tfrac12 M_s\dot z_b^2 + \tfrac12 m_w\dot z_w^2 , \tag{25} \]

with \(M_s = M_b + m_\ell\) the body and glass (\(M_b = 1.5\) kg) plus the stout, \(m_w = 0.3\) kg the wheel, \(I_w = \tfrac12 m_wR_w^2\) its moment of inertia as a disc, and \(J_m = 2\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) the rotor’s. All the \(\dot x\) terms combine into \(\tfrac12M_x\dot x^2\) with the effective horizontal mass

\[ M_x = M_s + m_w + \frac{I_w + N_g^2J_m}{R_w^2} . \tag{26} \]

The gearbox multiplies the rotor’s inertia by \(N_g^2\), once because the rotor turns \(N_g\) times faster and once because its energy goes as the square of that: a small fast rotor is felt at the road as a large mass.

The forces come from three sources. The springs and gravity have the potential \(U = \tfrac12k(z_b - z_w - L_s)^2 + U_t + M_sgz_b + m_wgz_w\), with the tyre’s \(U_t\) of section 6.4. The dampers and the drag have Rayleigh’s dissipation function \(\mathcal D = \tfrac12c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w)^2 + \tfrac12c_x\dot x^2 + \tfrac12c_t(\dot z_a - \dot z_w)^2\), whose derivatives \(-\partial\mathcal D/\partial\dot Y\) are the damping forces. Everything else enters through its virtual work \(\delta W = \sum_Y Q_Y\,\delta Y\), summed over the three coordinates \(Y = x, z_b, z_w\). The motor’s torque \(\tau_m = K_mi\) does \(\tau_m\,\delta\theta_m = K_mi\,N_g\,\delta x/R_w\), so \(Q_x = N_gK_mi/R_w\), the push \(F_m\) of (33). The actuator pushes body and axle apart with \(u_s\): \(\delta W = u_s(\delta z_b - \delta z_w)\). Your push and hand act along \(x\), and the stout acts on the body, which moves with \(x\) and \(z_b\): \(\delta W = F_{\ell,x}\,\delta x + F_{\ell,z}\,\delta z_b\). Lagrange’s equations,

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot Y} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial Y} = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial Y} - \frac{\partial\mathcal D}{\partial\dot Y} + Q_Y, \qquad Y = x,\ z_b,\ z_w , \]

have no \(\partial T/\partial Y\) terms, because \(T\) depends on the speeds only. For \(Y = x\), \(-\partial U_t/\partial x\) and the \(x\)-derivative of the tyre damper’s term (through \(\dot z_a = z_a'\dot x\)) combine into \(-N z_a'\), the road’s push (24); for \(Y = z_w\) the same two give \(+N\). While the stout spills, \(m_\ell\) and with it \(M_x\) fall, but the stout that leaves takes away exactly its share \(\Delta m\,\mathbf V_g\) of the cart’s momentum, so the cart’s speed is not changed by the loss; its relative momentum is in \(F_\ell\) (section 10). The three equations the page integrates are:

\[ M_x\ddot x = F_\text{road} + F_m - c_x\dot x + F_\text{push} + F_\text{hand} + F_{\ell,x}, \tag{27} \] \[ M_s\ddot z_b = -k(z_b - z_w - L_s) - c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w) + u_s - M_s g + F_{\ell,z}, \tag{28} \] \[ m_w\ddot z_w = k(z_b - z_w - L_s) + c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w) - u_s - m_w g + N . \tag{29} \]

Here \(k = 600\) N/m and \(c = 8\) N s/m are the suspension spring and damper, \(L_s = 135\) mm the spring’s unloaded length, \(c_x = 2\) N s/m the rolling and air drag, \(F_m\) the motor’s push (section 7), \(u_s\) the active suspension (section 9), \(F_\text{push}\) your buttons or arrow keys, \(F_\text{hand}\) your hand when you drag the cart (a spring of 300 N/m and a damper of 30 N s/m pulling towards the pointer, at most 25 N), and \(F_\ell\) the stout’s push beyond that of a rigid load (section 10). The stout counts in \(M_s\) and \(M_x\) as if it were frozen; everything its sloshing adds is in \(F_\ell\).

Key result · the cart

Equations (27)–(29) with the tyre load (23) and the road’s push (24). The horizontal mass is \(M_x = 1.927 + 0.300 + 0.150 + 0.125 = 2.502\) kg with a full glass: the wheel’s rolling adds 0.150 kg and the rotor, seen through the gearbox, 0.125 kg.

6.4Energy

The spring forces of (23) and (24) are the two derivatives of one potential. Take \(U_t = \tfrac12k_t\,[\max(0, z_a(x) - z_w)]^2\): then \(-\partial U_t/\partial z_w = k_t(z_a - z_w)\), the tyre’s spring force on the wheel, and \(-\partial U_t/\partial x = -k_t(z_a - z_w)\,z_a'\), the road’s spring push along \(x\). So the road does no net work on a wheel that returns to the level: what the climb takes, the descent gives back. With the drag, the dampers and the motor switched off, the energy

\[ E = \tfrac12M_x\dot x^2 + \tfrac12M_s\dot z_b^2 + \tfrac12m_w\dot z_w^2 + \tfrac12k(z_b - z_w - L_s)^2 + U_t + M_sgz_b + m_wgz_w \tag{30} \]

is constant, and with them on, it changes by exactly the work they do. node tools/fig12/energy.cjs 10 runs the page’s Cart over a bump and checks this: \(E = 4.366\) J holds to \(2\times10^{-4}\) J without losses over a 10 mm bump, and to \(1.7\times10^{-3}\) J over a 25 mm bump on which the wheel leaves the road; with the page’s dampers, \(E\) minus their work holds to \(5\times10^{-4}\) J. The residue is the error of the symplectic Euler rule the cart is stepped with (section 10.2): halving the cart’s step from 0.1 ms halves it, \(2.0\times10^{-4}\), \(0.95\times10^{-4}\), \(0.45\times10^{-4}\) J over the 10 mm bump, as a first-order method’s should (measured by the independent check, notes/fig12/check.md). Even without losses the cart comes off a 10 mm bump slower, 0.967 m/s instead of 1: the bump has passed some of its forward kinetic energy into the bounce of body and wheel.

6.5Natural frequencies

Hold \(x\) fixed, leave out the dampers, and measure \(z_b\), \(z_w\) from the resting position. Then \(M_s\ddot z_b = -k(z_b - z_w)\) and \(m_w\ddot z_w = k(z_b - z_w) - k_tz_w\). With \(z \propto e^{j\omega t}\) (\(j^2 = -1\); \(i\) is kept for the motor current) the determinant of the pair vanishes when

\[ \omega^4 - \omega^2\Big(\frac{k}{M_s} + \frac{k + k_t}{m_w}\Big) + \frac{k\,k_t}{M_s\,m_w} = 0 . \tag{31} \]
Example 6.1 · bounce and wheel hop

With \(M_s = 1.927\) kg, (31) gives 2.77 Hz, the body bouncing on the spring, and 41.7 Hz, the wheel hopping between spring and tyre. They are close to the values of each mass alone, \(\sqrt{k/M_s}/2\pi = 2.81\) Hz and \(\sqrt{(k + k_t)/m_w}/2\pi = 41.7\) Hz, because the two masses and stiffnesses are so different. The body’s damping ratio is \(c/(2\sqrt{kM_s}) = 0.118\): it bounces several times before it settles. At rest the spring is compressed by \(M_sg/k = 31.5\) mm and the tyre by \((M_s + m_w)g/k_t = 1.1\) mm. The bounce at 2.77 Hz lies close to the stout’s swing at about 3.4 Hz, but it moves the glass vertically, which hardly rocks the stout (section 9).

6.6What one bump does

A rough estimate of the jolt takes the tyre load at its static value, \(N \approx (M_s + m_w)g = 21.8\) N, and the steepest slope, 0.126 for 10 mm: \(|F_\text{road}| \approx 2.74\) N and \(|a_{g,x}| \approx 2.74/2.502 = 1.10\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). The page measures 1.24 m/s² (section 13): while climbing, the wheel is also lifting the body, so \(N\) exceeds its static value. At 1 m/s the axle crosses the 25 cm of bump in 0.25 s, and the push is roughly one cycle of a sine, backward then forward: the Fourier amplitude of one sine cycle 0.25 s long peaks at 3.35 Hz (computed), almost exactly the stout’s swing, and by (17a) that amplitude is what the stout keeps. That is why a bump of this size and a pint at walking pace are a bad match.

07The DC gear motor

A DC motor is a coil turning in a magnetic field. The field pushes on the current, which gives the torque; the turning coil cuts the field, which gives a voltage against the supply. One constant sets both.

Let \(i\) be the current in the armature, \(V\) the voltage across it, \(R_a\) its resistance, \(L_a\) its inductance and \(\omega_m\) the rotor’s angular speed. Kirchhoff’s voltage law and the force on the current-carrying coil give

\[ L_a\frac{di}{dt} = V_a - R_a\,i - e, \qquad e = K_e\,\omega_m, \qquad \tau_m = K_t\,i . \tag{32} \]

The back-EMF \(e\) is the voltage induced by the turning coil, with the back-EMF constant \(K_e\), and \(\tau_m\) the torque, with the torque constant \(K_t\). Multiply the first equation by \(i\): \(V_ai = R_ai^2 + \tfrac{d}{dt}(\tfrac12L_ai^2) + e\,i\). The electrical power is spent on heating the resistance, on the magnetic energy of the coil, and on \(e\,i = K_e\omega_mi\), the power that leaves the electrical circuit through the field. The motor has no other store or loss in this ideal model, so that power must equal the mechanical power the rotor delivers, \(\tau_m\omega_m = K_ti\,\omega_m\), for every \(i\) and \(\omega_m\). Hence \(K_e = K_t\): in SI units the back-EMF constant in V s/rad and the torque constant in N m/A are one number, which we call \(K_m\).

Through the gearbox and the wheel (section 6.3) the motor’s torque becomes a push at the road and its back-EMF a voltage proportional to the cart’s speed:

\[ F_m = b\,i, \qquad e = b\,\dot x, \qquad b = \frac{N_gK_m}{R_w} = 5\ \mathrm{N/A} = 5\ \mathrm{V\,s/m}, \tag{33} \]

with \(K_m = 0.02\) N m/A. The page drives the motor from a current-controlled drive: an electronic inner loop adjusts \(V\) so that \(i\) follows the commanded current. The page does not model that loop or the inductance; it lets the current follow the command it can reach, \(i_\text{reach}\), with a first-order lag of \(\tau_i = 2\) ms,

\[ \tau_i\frac{di}{dt} = i_\text{reach} - i, \tag{34} \]

where \(i_\text{reach}\) is the command clipped first to \(\pm I_\text{max}\) and then to the interval \([(-V_\text{max} - e)/R_a,\ (V_\text{max} - e)/R_a]\),

updated exactly over each cart step \(h_c\) (\(i \leftarrow i_\text{reach} + (i - i_\text{reach})e^{-h_c/\tau_i}\)). Two limits act: the drive’s current limit \(I_\text{max} = 3\) A, and the supply \(V_\text{max} = 12\) V, which cannot drive more current than \((V_\text{max} - e)/R_a\) through the resistance \(R_a = 2\ \Omega\) once the back-EMF has taken its share. The motor voltage the page reports is \(V_m = R_ai + e\), the inductive term left out.

Example 7.1 · the drive at 1 m/s

At 1 m/s the back-EMF is \(e = 5\times1 = 5.0\) V; the supply can then drive \((12 - 5)/2 = 3.5\) A, so the current limit, 3 A, binds first: the most the motor can push is \(5\times3 = 15\) N, which accelerates the cart at \(15/2.502 = 6.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). Holding 1 m/s against the drag takes \(i_0 = c_x v_0/b = 0.40\) A. With no current at all the back-EMF would equal the supply at \(12/5 = 2.4\) m/s, the drive’s top speed. 08The speed loop, and why it calms the stout

A waiter keeps the tray moving at an even pace whatever the floor does. A speed controller does the same with the motor: when the road brakes the cart, the motor pushes harder at once.

8.1The plant

Collect in \(F_d = F_\text{road} + F_\text{push} + F_\text{hand} + F_{\ell,x}\) every horizontal force except the motor’s and the drag. With \(v = \dot x\), equation (27) is

\[ M_x\dot v = b\,i - c_x v + F_d . \tag{35} \]

In the Laplace variable \(s\) (for an input \(e^{st}\)), \(v = (b\,i + F_d)/(M_xs + c_x)\): the cart is a mass with a little drag, a first-order plant with time constant \(M_x/c_x = 1.25\) s.

Open loop. The command is fixed at the current that balances the drag at the set speed, \(i_\text{cmd} = i_0 = c_xv_0/b\). The motor’s push then never changes, and a bump’s jolt reaches the cart unopposed: \(a_{g,x} \approx F_d/M_x\).

8.2The PI controller

Every \(T_s = 1\) ms a controller reads the speed and sets

\[ i_\text{cmd} = i_0 + K_p\,(v_0 - v) + K_i\int(v_0 - v)\,dt , \tag{36} \]

a proportional–integral (PI) law: the first term pushes in proportion to the speed error, the second in proportion to its accumulated history, so that a steady error cannot survive. With \(C(s) = K_p + K_i/s\) the loop gain, from error back to speed, is \(L(s) = b\,C(s)/(M_xs + c_x)\), and the closed loop’s poles solve

\[ M_xs^2 + (c_x + bK_p)\,s + bK_i = 0 . \tag{37} \]

Choosing the gains. Near the frequency \(\omega_c\) we want the loop to act on, the drag and the integral are small, and \(L(j\omega) \approx bK_p/(jM_x\omega)\). It has magnitude 1, the crossover, at \(\omega_c\) if \(K_p = M_x\omega_c/b\). We take \(\omega_c = 2\pi\times8\) Hz, well above the stout’s swing. The integral gain \(K_i = K_p\omega_c/4\) puts the PI zero, \(-K_i/K_p\), a quarter of the way down at \(\omega_c/4\), where it costs little phase at crossover; and with \(c_x \to 0\) it turns (37) into \(s^2 + \omega_cs + \omega_c^2/4 = 0\), a double pole at \(-\omega_c/2\): critical damping, no overshoot.

Example 8.1 · the loop’s numbers

With \(M_x = 2.502\) kg and \(b = 5\) N/A: \(K_p = 2.502\times50.27/5 = 25.1\) A/(m/s) and \(K_i = 316\) A/m. The poles of (37) are \(-21.0\) and \(-30.0\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) (damping ratio 1.02; the drag splits the double pole). The exact crossover is 8.23 Hz with a phase margin of 77°. Adding the current lag \(\tau_i\) and the half-sample delay \(T_s/2\) of a controller that holds its output for a sample lowers them to 8.19 Hz and 70°. Since \(\omega_cT_s = 0.05\) rad, sampling every millisecond is nearly continuous. (node tools/fig12/theory.cjs.)

Anti-windup. While the commanded current is beyond \(\pm I_\text{max}\) the motor cannot deliver it, and an integral left running would grow (wind up) and overshoot later. The page integrates the error only while \(|i_\text{cmd}| \lt I_\text{max}\) (conditional integration). The voltage limit of (34) is not part of this test; at the set speeds of the slider it is reached only above about 1.2 m/s with a full 3 A, where \((12 - 5\dot x)/2 \lt 3\).

8.3Rejecting the jolt

Write \(v = v_0 + \delta v\) and \(i = i_0 + \delta i\). The steady state, \(b\,i_0 = c_xv_0\), cancels from (35), leaving \((M_xs + c_x)\,\delta v = b\,\delta i + F_d\). In open loop \(\delta i = 0\); with the loop closed \(\delta i = -C(s)\,\delta v\). So

\[ \delta v_\text{open} = \frac{F_d}{M_xs + c_x}, \qquad \delta v_\text{closed} = \frac{F_d}{M_xs + c_x + b\,C(s)} . \]

The accelerations are \(s\,\delta v\) in both cases. For the same disturbing force \(F_d\), their ratio, closed over open, is the sensitivity; multiplying numerator and denominator by \(s\) and putting in \(C = K_p + K_i/s\),

\[ S(s) = \frac{s\,(M_xs + c_x)}{D(s)} , \quad D(s) = M_xs^2 + (c_x + bK_p)s + bK_i , \tag{38} \]

which is \(1/(1 + L(s))\). With the gains of section 8.2 and \(\gamma = c_x/M_x\), it reads \(S = s(s + \gamma)/\big(s^2 + (\gamma + \omega_c)s + \omega_c^2/4\big)\).

At low frequencies \(S \to 0\): the integral cancels any steady force entirely, and this is why pushing a speed-controlled cart only makes the motor brake against you. At high frequencies \(S \to 1\): the loop is too slow to act. In between it attenuates.

Key result · why speed control calms the stout

The stout is rocked by the horizontal acceleration of the glass, through (17), and mostly by the part of it near its own frequency. The speed loop cuts that part by \(|S(j\omega_{11})|\): with this page’s gains and lags, \(|S| = 0.39\) at 3.21 Hz and 0.40 at 3.26 Hz. The motor absorbs the jolt before the glass feels it.

Figure 2 shows one 10 mm bump at 1 m/s on the 4 mm grid. In open loop the road brakes the cart at up to 1.24 m/s² and shoves it forward, and the stout swings to a front-minus-back lean of 25.0 mm and keeps swinging; the cart’s acceleration keeps a small ripple at 3.2 Hz, which is the stout pushing back (Example 4.2). With speed control the jolt peaks at 0.53 m/s² and the lean stays under 5.8 mm, of which about 1 mm is the noise of reading the surface (the same reading on a flat road gives 1.2 mm). The ratios measured are: peak acceleration 0.43; the Fourier amplitude of the acceleration at the swing frequency, \(|\int a_{g,x}e^{-2\pi i f_{11}t}\,dt|\), 0.0453/0.1643 = 0.28; the lean 0.23 (0.23 also on the 3 mm grid). The linear estimate, 0.39, compares the two loops for the same disturbing force; it is a fair guide to the jolt (0.43 measured on the peak) and too pessimistic for the lean. Part of the gap is the open loop’s extra forcing: after the bump the swinging stout feeds its own push back into the free cart (Example 4.2), which raises the open-loop amplitude at the swing frequency from 0.145 to 0.164 m/s. The larger part is that the solver’s stout swings less under speed control than the one-mode theory predicts for the jolt it receives (Example 4.2, last paragraph); that part is not explained here.

8.4Holding the stout still: road preview and slosh feedback

A speed loop only reacts: the road must first slow the cart before the loop can push back, and by (38) about 40 % of the jolt at the swing’s frequency gets through. Two better controllers use information the speed loop ignores. The first knows the road. The second knows the stout.

Road preview. The road ahead is known, and its push follows from (24): \(F_\text{road} = -N z_a'(x)\). Estimate the tyre load by its static value, \(\hat N = (M_s + m_w)\,g\), and command, besides the PI law (36), the current that cancels the push the cart is about to feel, one current lag ahead:

\[ i_\text{ff}(t) = \frac{\hat N\,z_a'\big(x + \dot x\,\tau_i\big)}{b} . \tag{50} \]

The motor then delivers \(b\,i_\text{ff} \approx -F_\text{road}\) just as the road pushes, and only the error \(-(N - \hat N)\,z_a'\) reaches the cart, the part of the tyre load that the body’s bounce adds, which the PI loop then fights as before. A real cart would read the road from a camera or a map; the page reads it from its own road.

Slosh feedback. Put the cart and the first sloshing mode together. With \(\delta v = \dot x - v_0\) the speed error, \(\xi_i = \int(v_0 - \dot x)\,dt\) its integral, \(q = q_1\) and \(u = \delta i\) the current beyond \(i_0\), equations (35) and (17) form one linear system \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x + B u\) with \(\mathbf x = (\delta v, q, \dot q, \xi_i)\):

\[ A = \small\begin{pmatrix} -\gamma & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \beta\gamma & -\omega^2 & -2\zeta\omega & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \tag{51} \] \[ B = \frac{b}{M_x}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -\beta \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{c_x}{M_x} , \tag{51a} \]

Here \(2\zeta\omega\) is written in the matrix as one entry. The third row is (17) with \(a_{g,x} = \delta\dot v\) substituted from the first: the motor’s push reaches the stout through the glass’s acceleration. The linear–quadratic regulator chooses the feedback \(u = -K\mathbf x\) that minimises

\[ J = \int_0^\infty\big(\mathbf x^TQ\mathbf x + r\,u^2\big)\,dt , \tag{52} \]

a sum of the squared errors and the squared effort. The answer is \(K = B^TP/r\), with \(P\) the symmetric positive-definite solution of the algebraic Riccati equation

\[ A^TP + PA - \frac{1}{r}PBB^TP + Q = 0 . \tag{53} \]

The weights follow Bryson’s rule, one over the square of the largest value each quantity may take: 0.05 m/s for the speed error, 2 mm for the swing, \(2\ \mathrm{mm}\times\omega\) for its rate, 2 cm for the integral, and the drive’s 3 A for the current. The page solves (53) by integrating the Riccati flow \(\dot P = A^TP + PA - PBB^TP/r + Q\) from \(P = 0\) until it stops changing (Cart.lqrGains, 0.2 s). The gains are \(K = (75.0\ \mathrm{A/(m/s)},\ -1939\ \mathrm{A/m},\ -38.8\ \mathrm{A/(m/s)},\ -150\ \mathrm{A/m})\). The closed loop’s poles, the eigenvalues of \(A - BK\), are \(-12.0 \pm 8.8j\), \(-2.6\) and \(-244\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The pair \(-12.0 \pm 8.8j\) is the swing: its damping ratio, \(12.0/\sqrt{12.0^2 + 8.8^2} = 0.81\), was 0.005 without control. The controller does not stop the glass from accelerating; it shapes the acceleration so that the swing it starts is taken out again. The price is the slow pole \(-2.6\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\): the speed recovers in about a second instead of a tenth.

The slosh feedback needs the swing \(q\) and its rate, and nobody can see them directly. What a real cart can carry is a sensor on the glass and an encoder on the wheel. Section 8.5 builds both and the filter that turns their readings into the state the regulator needs; the page uses that path in both models, so the regulator never reads the stout’s state from the solver.

8.5Seeing the swing: two level probes and a Kalman filter ★

Hold a full glass and walk: you do not see the swing, you see where the stout stands against the glass, front and back, and your hand corrects from that. The cart does the same with two level probes, thin strips on the inside of the wall at the front (\(\theta = 0\), the direction of travel) and at the back (\(\theta = \pi\)), at the radius of the surface, \(r_p = R(h)\). Each reads the height of the stout where it touches the wall, as a capacitive strip does, whose capacitance grows with the wetted length. In the figure they are the two dark strips; the white dot on each is the height it reads now.

The measurement. The first mode’s surface is \(\eta = q\,J_1(kr)\cos\theta/J_1(\xi_{11})\) (section 4.5); at the wall it is \(q\cos\theta\): \(+q\) at the front, \(-q\) at the back. Anything that lifts the whole surface alike (the fill, an axisymmetric wave) adds the same to both readings, so half their difference keeps the swing and drops the rest:

\[ y_q = \tfrac12\big[\eta(r_p, 0) - \eta(r_p, \pi)\big] + n_q = q + e + n_q . \tag{K1} \]

Here \(e\) is what the one-mode picture leaves out (in the live model the higher modes at the wall, \(q_2 + q_3\); in the 3D model whatever its stout does that is not mode 1), and \(n_q\) is the probe’s own error. The lean of the figure is twice this, \(2y_q\). The probes read the surface the solver has just computed, after every fluid step of 5 ms: in the 3D model the column heights of section 11 at \(r_p\), in the live model (55). The cart’s speed comes from a wheel encoder at every 1 ms sample, \(y_v = v + n_v\). The switch Sensor noise adds Gaussian errors of 1 mm to each probe and 0.2 mm/s to the speed, from a seeded generator so that a run repeats.

How large are \(e\) and \(n_q\)? With the stout at rest on the 4 mm grid, \(y_q\) wanders by 0.08 mm (standard deviation over 2 s) about an offset of 0.39 mm, a lean of 0.79 mm (node tools/fig12/observer.cjs 3d). While it swings, a single damped swing fitted to the 3D reading misses \(y_q\) by 0.6–1.2 mm rms (1.2–2.4 mm on the lean; tools/fig12/linfit.cjs). That misfit is \(e\): an oscillation of its own, not white noise. The filter below treats it as white noise of standard deviation \(\sigma_q = 0.8\) mm, an approximation chosen to cover it. For the speed it assumes \(\sigma_v = 2\) mm/s, ten times the noise the switch adds: the encoder is exact, but the model’s speed equation misses the stout’s own push, and a larger \(\sigma_v\) keeps the filter from trusting it too much.

One number, two sources. Begin with a single quantity, say the swing \(q\), and two independent opinions of it: a prediction \(q^-\) from the model, uncertain by a variance \(C^-\), and a reading \(y\) uncertain by \(\sigma^2\). Any blend \(\hat q = q^- + L\,(y - q^-)\) is unbiased; its error is \((1 - L)\) times the prediction’s error plus \(L\) times the reading’s, so its variance is \((1 - L)^2C^- + L^2\sigma^2\). Setting the derivative in \(L\) to zero gives the best blend:

\[ L = \frac{C^-}{C^- + \sigma^2}, \qquad C = (1 - L)\,C^- . \tag{K2} \]

The estimate moves toward the reading by the share of the total uncertainty that was the model’s, and the blend is surer than either opinion. Between readings the model carries the estimate forward and its uncertainty grows; at each reading (K2) pulls it back. That is the whole of the Kalman filter [13]; what follows writes it for three coupled quantities.

The model the filter carries. Take \(\mathbf z = (v, q, \dot q)\), the speed itself rather than its error: since \(b\,i_0 = c_xv_0\), the first row of (51) is \(M_x\dot v = b\,i - c_xv + F_d\) with the full current \(i\), and the set speed drops out. With the third row of (51),

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot{\mathbf z} &= A_z\mathbf z + B_z\,i + G\,\mathbf d(t), \\ A_z &= \small\begin{pmatrix} -\gamma & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \beta\gamma & -\omega^2 & -2\zeta\omega \end{pmatrix}, \\ B_z &= \frac{b}{M_x}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -\beta \end{pmatrix},\quad G = \begin{pmatrix} 1/M_x & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \\ -\beta/M_x & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \end{aligned} \tag{K3} \]

Here \(i\) is the motor current, which the drive measures, and \(\mathbf d = (F_d, a_q)\) is what the model cannot know: \(F_d\), every other force along the road (the bump, the stout’s own push, your hand), and \(a_q\), what the one-mode model gets wrong about the swing. The heave is left out of (K3): it changes \(\omega^2\) by the factor \(1 + a_z/g\) (section 4), which the filter does not track. Both parts of \(\mathbf d\) are taken as white noise. White noise of intensity \(W\) is a signal whose integral over a time \(\tau\) has the variance \(W\tau\); a quantity that can change by \(\Delta\) within \(\tau\) is modelled by \(W \approx \Delta^2/\tau\). We take \(W_F = 0.5\ \mathrm{N^2\,s}\), a change of push of about 0.22 N s within 0.1 s, the size of a 10 mm bump; the 25 mm bump in open loop slows the cart by 0.30 m/s in 0.11 s, ten times that intensity, so there the filter follows the speed more slowly than it could. For the swing, \(W_a = 0.002\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-3}}\): its rate may drift by about 0.02 m/s over a period. The intensities form the matrix \(W = \mathrm{diag}(W_F, W_a)\). The force enters the swing through \(-\beta/M_x\), the same path as the motor’s push; so a bump first shows up as a jump in the speed, and through this path the filter infers at once the swing that the push must start, before the probes see much of it.

The parameters come from the stout the filter watches. In the live model they are mode 1’s own: 3.421 Hz and \(\zeta = 0.0053\). In the 3D model they are the grid’s: the tulip swings at 3.214 Hz on the 4 mm grid with \(\zeta = 0.008\) (section 12; 3.263 Hz and 0.002 on 3 mm). A filter tuned to the continuum’s 3.421 Hz would slip against the 3D stout by a third of a swing every five swings. In both, \(\beta = 1.541\) from (17).

From one sample to the next. The controller samples every \(T_s = 1\) ms and holds its current command in between (the current itself follows with the drive’s 2 ms lag, which the filter ignores). Integrating (K3) over one sample with \(i\) held gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z_k &= A_d\,\mathbf z_{k-1} + B_d\,i_{k-1} + \mathbf d_k, \\ A_d &= e^{A_zT_s}, \qquad B_d = \int_0^{T_s} e^{A_zs}\,ds\;B_z, \\ Q_d &= \mathrm{cov}\,\mathbf d_k = \int_0^{T_s} e^{A_zs}\,GWG^T\,e^{A_z^Ts}\,ds . \end{aligned} \tag{K4} \]

The first line is the variation-of-constants formula over one sample; \(Q_d\) is the variance that white noise of intensity \(GWG^T\) builds up while the dynamics carry it. The page computes \(A_d\) and \(B_d\) as the upper blocks of one matrix exponential, \(\exp\!\big[\begin{smallmatrix}A_z & B_z\\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\big]T_s\), and \(Q_d\) by Van Loan’s method [14]: the exponential of \(\big[\begin{smallmatrix}-A_z & GWG^T\\ 0 & A_z^T\end{smallmatrix}\big]T_s\) has \(A_d^T\) as its lower right block and \(A_d^{-1}Q_d\) as its upper right one (Observer.design; the data panel recomputes all three by integrating the matrix equations again with Runge–Kutta).

Predict, then correct. The filter keeps an estimate \(\hat{\mathbf z}\) and its error covariance \(C\), the uncertainty it believes it has. Each sample it predicts, then blends as in (K2):

\[ \hat{\mathbf z}_k^- = A_d\,\hat{\mathbf z}_{k-1} + B_d\,i_{k-1}, \qquad C_k^- = A_d\,C_{k-1}A_d^T + Q_d , \tag{K5} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \boldsymbol\nu_k &= \mathbf y_k - H\hat{\mathbf z}_k^-, \qquad \hat{\mathbf z}_k = \hat{\mathbf z}_k^- + L_k\,\boldsymbol\nu_k, \\ L_k &= C_k^-H^T S_k^{-1}, \qquad S_k = HC_k^-H^T + \Sigma_n, \\ C_k &= (I - L_kH)\,C_k^-\,(I - L_kH)^T + L_k\Sigma_nL_k^T . \end{aligned} \tag{K6} \]

The prediction (K5) is the model run forward, and the covariance grows by the model’s own uncertainty \(Q_d\). \(H\) picks what is measured: the row \((1, 0, 0)\) when only the speed is new, and also \((0, 1, 0)\) when the probes have a new reading, once per fluid step, so on one sample in five; \(\Sigma_n = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_v^2, \sigma_q^2)\). The innovation \(\boldsymbol\nu\) is the surprise, reading minus prediction, and \(L\) is (K2) for several quantities at once: \(S\) is the innovation’s covariance, the matrix form of \(C^- + \sigma^2\). The last line, Joseph’s form, equals \((I - LH)C^-\) for this gain, the matrix form of the second half of (K2), but stays symmetric and positive in floating point. For a linear model with Gaussian noise, (K5)–(K6) give the estimate of least mean squared error at every step.

Key result · what the filter does with the probes

After a few hundred samples \(C\) settles into a cycle of five samples. At the sample with a probe reading the gain on the swing’s innovation is \(L_{qq} = 0.143\) for \(\hat q\) and 2.19 s\(^{-1}\) for \(\hat{\dot q}\) (3D plant; 0.139 and 2.08 s\(^{-1}\) live), and the estimate’s standard deviations are 0.30 mm for \(\hat q\) and 9.7 mm/s for \(\hat{\dot q}\) (node tools/fig12/observer.cjs 3d).

Worked example. A gain of 0.143 means a reading pulls \(\hat q\) a seventh of the way to itself: \(1/0.143 = 7.0 = \sigma_q^2/C_{qq}\), so the filter weighs about seven readings, 35 ms of them. A plain average of seven readings would lag a 3.2 Hz swing by about 40°. The filter does not, because between readings the prediction (K5) carries the swing forward along its own oscillation; each reading only corrects the small drift of that prediction, and noise in the reading moves the estimate by a seventh of itself.

The regulator on the estimate. The current is the LQR law \(u = -K\mathbf x\) of section 8.4 with the estimate in place of the state,

\[ i = i_0 - K\,(\hat v - v_0,\ \hat q,\ \hat{\dot q},\ \xi_i) , \tag{K7} \]

\(\xi_i\) being the controller’s own running sum of the speed error (the page forms it from the solver’s speed; with the noise switch on, the encoder would change it by less than 0.1 mm over a run). For a linear plant with Gaussian noise, designing the regulator and the filter separately and joining them this way is optimal: the separation principle [15]. For the 3D plant the gains are \(K = (76.7, -1934, -41.3, -150)\) in the units of section 8.4, and the swing’s closed-loop poles are \(-11.3 \pm 7.5j\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), damping ratio 0.83.

And the plant is linear. A wave breaks when its crest accelerates downward faster than gravity, \(a\omega^2 \gtrsim g\), at a wall amplitude \(a = g/\omega^2\) (Taylor [16]; a linear estimate): 21.2 mm, a lean of 42 mm, at the continuum’s 3.421 Hz, or 24.1 mm, a lean of 48 mm, at the 3D grid’s 3.214 Hz. The push (lean 45 mm) stays just below the grid’s limit; the bump (61 mm) goes past it. There the filter’s model is wrong: its estimate misses the probes’ lean by 3.9 mm rms, against 2.2 mm for the push. The regulator still calms the stout, faster in time (\(\sigma = 9.4\) against 5.9 s\(^{-1}\)) though less damped per swing (\(\zeta = 0.35\) against 0.70), because the errors of its estimate stay about ten times smaller than the swing it is removing.

09The skyhook suspension

A damper slows the motion of one end relative to the other. Fixed between body and wheel, it also drags the body along with every jolt of the wheel. Imagine instead a damper whose other end is hooked to the sky, fixed in space: it would resist only the body’s own motion.

No hook in the sky exists, but an actuator between body and wheel can produce the force such a damper would. With the wheel held to the road (a rigid tyre, \(z_w\) given), the body would obey

\[ M_s\ddot z_b = -k(z_b - z_w) - c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w) - c_\text{sky}\,\dot z_b . \tag{39} \]

Let the road make the axle oscillate, \(z_w = Z_we^{j\omega t}\) (with \(j^2 = -1\), as in section 8), and measure \(z_b = Z_be^{j\omega t}\) from the resting position. Then \(\ddot z_b = -\omega^2z_b\), \(\dot z_b = j\omega z_b\), and (39) becomes \(\big(k - M_s\omega^2 + j\omega(c + c_\text{sky})\big)Z_b = (k + j\omega c)\,Z_w\). The ratio of body to axle amplitude, the transmissibility, is therefore

\[ \Gamma(\omega) = \frac{|k + j\omega c|}{|k - M_s\omega^2 + j\omega(c + c_\text{sky})|} . \tag{40} \]

The skyhook term appears in the denominator only. At resonance it damps exactly like a passive damper of \(c + c_\text{sky}\). Above resonance a passive damper transmits the road through its own force, \(\propto \omega c\) in the numerator, while the skyhook does not. The idea goes back to Karnopp, Crosby and Harwood [7]; their paper could not be obtained for this note, and only its abstract was read, which describes a semi-active force generator, one that controls a damper and needs no power supply of its own. A semi-active device can only resist relative motion, so it can produce \(-c_\text{sky}\dot z_b\) only when that force opposes \(\dot z_b - \dot z_w\). The page’s actuator is fully active: it pushes and pulls in either direction and supplies power when needed.

The page commands \(u_\text{s,cmd} = -c_\text{sky}\dot z_b\), sampled every 1 ms, clipped to \(\pm40\) N and delivered with a lag of 5 ms, with

\[ c_\text{sky} = 2\cdot0.7\sqrt{kM_s} = 47.6\ \mathrm{N\,s/m} , \tag{41} \]

the value that would by itself give the body a damping ratio of 0.7. The actuator is ideal otherwise: it can push and pull, and it supplies whatever power the law demands.

Example 9.1 · what the skyhook buys

Passive, the body’s damping ratio is 0.118 and the peak of (40) is 4.39: a road wave at the bounce frequency is amplified more than fourfold. With the skyhook the damping ratio is \((8 + 47.6)/(2\sqrt{600\times1.927}) = 0.82\) and the peak 1.00. At 10 Hz the passive suspension transmits 0.111, the skyhook 0.100, and a passive damper of the same total, 55.6 N s/m, 0.453. On the page, over a 10 mm bump with speed control, the largest vertical acceleration of the glass falls from 3.74 to 1.48 m/s² (open loop: 3.66 to 1.38).

Why the skyhook does not calm the lean. The same runs show the lean almost unchanged: 5.8 against 6.0 mm with speed control, 25.0 against 24.2 mm in open loop. Vertical acceleration enters the stout through the apparent gravity, \(g + a_{g,z}\), which multiplies the surface displacement \(\eta\) in (12); for a level surface it does nothing, and for a small swing it only changes the swing’s frequency for a moment, by a factor \(\sqrt{1 + a_{g,z}/g}\). Only a vertical shaking at twice the swing frequency, kept up over many swings, can feed the swing that way (parametric resonance, the Faraday waves of a shaken dish); one bump cannot. The skyhook keeps the glass, the drink and the rider level; it is the speed loop that keeps the stout in the glass.

10The stout pushes back, and spills

When the stout surges forward in a braking glass, you feel the glass tug forward in your hand. That tug is Newton’s third law, and the page gives it to the cart.

10.1The force of the stout on the glass

Consider the stout in the glass as a body that can lose mass over the rim. In the room its momentum is \(\mathbf p_\ell = \int\rho(\mathbf V_g + \mathbf u)\,dV = m_\ell\mathbf V_g + \mathbf P\), with \(\mathbf V_g\) the glass’s velocity and \(\mathbf P = \int\rho\,\mathbf u\,dV\) its momentum relative to the glass. Two forces act on it: gravity, \(m_\ell\mathbf g\), and the glass’s push, \(\mathbf F_{g\to\ell}\) (pressure and friction on wall and floor; the air pushes on nothing, by (7)). In a short time \(\Delta t\), a mass \(\Delta m\) leaves over the rim carrying its room momentum \(\Delta m\,\mathbf V_g + \mathbf P_\text{spill}\), where \(\mathbf P_\text{spill}\) is its momentum relative to the glass. Newton’s second law for what was in the glass at the start reads

\[ \Delta\big(m_\ell\mathbf V_g + \mathbf P\big) + \Delta m\,\mathbf V_g + \mathbf P_\text{spill} = \big(\mathbf F_{g\to\ell} + m_\ell\mathbf g\big)\,\Delta t . \tag{42} \]

Since \(\Delta m_\ell = -\Delta m\), the terms \(\mathbf V_g\Delta m_\ell + \Delta m\,\mathbf V_g\) cancel, \(\Delta\mathbf V_g = \mathbf a_g\Delta t\), and by Newton’s third law the stout pushes the glass with \(\mathbf F_{\ell\to g} = -\mathbf F_{g\to\ell}\):

\[ \mathbf F_{\ell\to g} = -m_\ell\,\mathbf a_g + m_\ell\,\mathbf g - \frac{\Delta\mathbf P + \mathbf P_\text{spill}}{\Delta t} . \tag{43} \]

The first two terms are the stout as a frozen load, its inertia and its weight; they are already in \(M_x\) and \(M_s\) in (27)–(28). The last is what the sloshing adds, and it is the force the page hands to the cart:

\[ \mathbf F_\ell = -\frac{\Delta\mathbf P + \mathbf P_\text{spill}}{\Delta t} . \tag{44} \]

The spilled momentum is counted because the glass did not push that stout away at the moment it left; leaving it out would give the cart a kick each time stout went over the rim. The page measures \(\mathbf P = 2m_p\sum_p\mathbf u_p\) from its particles (the factor 2 for the half glass not computed, \(m_p\) the mass of one particle), every fluid step.

10.2How the two are stepped together

One fluid step of length \(\Delta t\) (at most 5 ms) goes as follows (World.advance). The cart is stepped through \(\Delta t\) in equal substeps \(h_c\) of about 0.1 ms (\(\Delta t\) divided by the nearest whole number to \(\Delta t/0.1\ \mathrm{ms}\)), by the symplectic Euler rule (new velocities from the forces at the present state, then new positions from the new velocities), with the stout’s push held fixed. The glass’s mean acceleration over the step, \(\mathbf a_g = \Delta\mathbf V_g/\Delta t\), is handed to the stout, which is stepped once (section 11). The change of its momentum gives the impulse \(\mathbf J = -(\Delta\mathbf P + \mathbf P_\text{spill})\) it gave the glass, and over the next fluid step, of length \(\Delta t'\), the cart receives the force \(\mathbf J/\Delta t'\): (44) when the steps are equal, and the same impulse when they are not. The stout that left reduces \(m_\ell\). The coupling therefore lags by one fluid step. Such an explicit (staggered) coupling can become unstable when the lagged mass is comparable with the structure’s; here the frozen part of the stout is treated without lag inside \(M_x\) and \(M_s\), and the lagged part is the swinging mass of about 85 g against 2.5 kg (Example 4.2). We infer that the lag is harmless here; the energy and spill checks of section 12 found nothing to contradict it.

10.3Spilling

A particle of stout that is above the rim, \(z \gt z_\text{rim}\), and outside the rim’s radius, \(r \gt R_\text{rim}\), has left the glass. Its mass (and its mirror image’s) leaves \(m_\ell\), and its relative momentum goes into \(\mathbf P_\text{spill}\). It becomes a drop in the room, at the glass’s position plus its own and with the glass’s velocity plus its own, and falls freely, \(\ddot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf g\), without air drag, until it lands on the body’s top or on the road, where it stays. check.cjs spill shoves the tulip at 25 m/s² for 0.12 s: 101.3 g leave the glass on the 4 mm grid and 101.3 g are counted as drops (105.3 g and 105.3 g on 3 mm).

11Solving the equations ★

The equations of sections 3 and 10 have no closed-form solution in the tulip, so the page solves them numerically, in three dimensions, while you watch. This section describes the method as the code does it, step by step, for a physicist rather than a programmer.

11.1Splitting a step: Chorin’s projection

Equation (4) has three kinds of term: transport by the flow, forces given in advance (gravity, the jolt, viscosity), and the pressure, which is not given at all. The pressure is whatever it must be to keep the flow free of divergence, (5); it carries no equation of its own. Chorin’s projection method [11] uses this. Over a step \(\Delta t\), first move the liquid and add the known forces, ignoring the pressure, to obtain a provisional velocity \(\mathbf u^*\) that in general has a divergence. Then remove that divergence:

\[ \mathbf u^{n+1} = \mathbf u^* - \frac{\Delta t}{\rho}\nabla p, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u^{n+1} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta t}{\rho}\nabla^2 p = \nabla\cdot\mathbf u^* , \tag{45} \]

a Poisson equation for the pressure, with \(p = 0\) on the free surface and, at the walls, the condition that the corrected flow does not cross them. Chorin describes it as decomposing the provisional field “into the sum of a vector with zero divergence and a vector with zero curl” [11, p. 746]: the pressure gradient is the curl-free part, and subtracting it leaves the part that satisfies (5). Physically, the pressure is the field of impulses the liquid exerts on itself so that no parcel is squeezed.

11.2The staggered (MAC) grid

The glass’s half is covered by a box of cubic cells of side \(\Delta x\): 20 × 10 × 45 cells at \(\Delta x = 4\) mm, 28 × 14 × 60 at 3 mm. Pressures sit at cell centres; each velocity component sits at the centres of the cell faces normal to it, \(u\) on the faces normal to \(x\), and so on (the marker-and-cell or MAC arrangement [4, §2.4]). The divergence of a cell is then the net flow out through its six faces, with no averaging, and the pressure difference across a face is exactly what changes the velocity on it. To discretise (45), integrate it over one liquid cell \(c\) and use Gauss’s theorem on both sides: the flux of \((\Delta t/\rho)\nabla p\) out through the faces equals the flux of \(\mathbf u^*\) out through them. A face \(f\) to the neighbour \(n\) is open over the area \(a_f\Delta x^2\), the pressure gradient across it is \((p_n - p_c)/\Delta x\), and the outward flux of \(\mathbf u^*\) is \(\pm a_f\Delta x^2u_f^*\), with \(+\) on the cell’s high side and \(-\) on its low side:

\[ \frac{\Delta t}{\rho}\sum_f a_f\Delta x^2\,\frac{p_n - p_c}{\Delta x} = \Delta x^2\sum_f \pm a_f\,u_f^* . \]

A face closed by the wall (\(a_f = 0\)) carries neither term: that is the condition of no flow through the wall, imposed without ever writing a pressure condition there. With the unknown \(\psi = p\,\Delta t/\rho\), and the velocity correction of (45) on each open face,

\[ \sum_f a_f\,(\psi_c - \psi_n) = -\Delta x\sum_f \pm a_f\,u^*_f , \tag{46} \] \[ u_f^{\,n+1} = u_f^* - \frac{\psi_n - \psi_c}{\Delta x} , \tag{46a} \]

(46a) written for a face on the cell’s high side; in (46) the sign is \(+\) for a face on the cell’s high side and \(-\) on its low side, and \(a_f\) is the open fraction of the face (section 11.4). With every \(a_f = 1\), (46) is the familiar seven-point Laplacian [4, eqs. (4.19), (4.22)].

11.3Particles that carry the liquid (APIC)

The stout itself is carried by particles: 8 per cell at the start (2 × 2 × 2, slightly jittered), 25 666 in the half glass at 4 mm and 61 407 at 3 mm, each of mass \(m_p = m_\ell/(2N_p)\). Particles carry the velocity from place to place without the smearing a grid would add, and where they are is where the liquid is, which gives the free surface for free. Each step they hand their velocities to the grid and take them back after the projection. The page uses the affine particle-in-cell method, APIC, of Jiang et al. [3]: every particle carries, besides its velocity \(u_p\), the local gradient \(\mathbf c_p\) of each velocity component, so that what it hands to a grid node is not one value but a small linear field:

\[ u_\alpha\sum_p w_{\alpha p} = \sum_p w_{\alpha p}\,(u_p + \mathbf c_p\cdot\mathbf d_{\alpha p}) , \tag{47} \] \[ u_p = \sum_\alpha w_{\alpha p}\,u_\alpha, \qquad \mathbf c_p = \sum_\alpha \nabla w_{\alpha p}\,u_\alpha , \tag{47a} \]

for each component on its own staggered nodes \(\alpha\), with \(\mathbf d_{\alpha p} = \mathbf x_\alpha - \mathbf x_p\) the offset from particle to node and with trilinear weights \(w_{\alpha p}\) (all particles have the same mass, which cancels): (47) hands particles to grid, (47a) grid to particles. This is [3], eqs. (8)–(10), with the identity they give for multilinear weights, \(w_{\alpha p}D_p^{-1}\mathbf d_{\alpha p} = \nabla w_{\alpha p}\), and its MAC-grid form, §6, eqs. (12)–(14). The plain particle-in-cell method hands over only \(u_p\) and loses the rotation of every small eddy at each transfer, which damps the flow heavily; APIC keeps it, and [3] proves that the transfers conserve angular momentum exactly. The particles then move through the corrected grid velocity by the midpoint rule (second-order Runge–Kutta), with the grid velocity extended three cells into the air so that particles at the surface find a value.

11.4The curved wall in a square grid

The wall \(r = R(z)\) cuts through the cubic cells. Following Batty, Bertails and Bridson [5, §2.1], each face is weighted in (46) by its open fraction \(a_f\), the part of its area inside the glass at the face’s height; faces less than 10 % open are closed, which moves the wall by under \(0.1\Delta x\). This is the variational treatment of solid walls, whose weights [5] take as the fluid volume around a face and allow to approximate by \(\Delta x\) times its open area. A particle that ends a step outside the wall is put back onto it along the wall’s normal \(\mathbf n\) of (6) and loses the part of its velocity along \(\mathbf n\) that points out: free slip. The velocities on faces closed by the wall are filled by extending the liquid’s velocity into them, so that a particle sliding along the wall does not read a zero there and is not braked as by a sticky wall one cell thick.

11.5The free surface

The surface is the zero of a smooth function \(\varphi\) built from the particles, after Zhu and Bridson [6, §5, eqs. (7)–(10)]:

\[ \varphi(\mathbf x) = |\mathbf x - \bar{\mathbf x}| - r_p , \qquad \bar{\mathbf x} = \frac{\sum_p W(|\mathbf x - \mathbf x_p|/R_k)\,\mathbf x_p}{\sum_p W(|\mathbf x - \mathbf x_p|/R_k)} , \tag{48} \]

with the kernel \(W(\varsigma) = \max\big(0, (1 - \varsigma^2)^3\big)\),

negative in the liquid. Here \(\bar{\mathbf x}\) is a weighted mean of the nearby particles within \(R_k = 2\Delta x\), and \(r_p\) a particle radius; Zhu and Bridson average the particles’ radii, the page uses one radius, 1.8 particle spacings (\(0.9\Delta x\)). The mirror images of the particles in \(y = 0\), and near the wall their images in the wall, are included so that the surface meets the mirror plane and the wall at right angles. A cell is liquid where \(\varphi \lt 0\) or where it holds particles. Cells cut by the wall hold few particles, because part of them lies in the glass, so one more rule applies to them: a cut cell is liquid when it shares an open face with a liquid cell beside it, or with a liquid cell above it that lies more than half a cell below the surface.

Between a liquid cell and an air cell the surface lies at the fraction \(\lambda = \varphi_c/(\varphi_c - \varphi_n)\) of the way across, found by linear interpolation of \(\varphi\). The ghost-fluid condition [4, §4.5.1] makes the pressure vary linearly from the liquid cell to zero exactly there: the air cell’s pressure is replaced by the ghost value \(\psi_n = -\psi_c(1 - \lambda)/\lambda\), which adds \(a_f/\lambda\) instead of \(a_f\) to the diagonal of (46). The page bounds \(\lambda \ge 0.05\) (the notes [4] use \(10^{-6}\)). This places the boundary condition (7) at the surface to second order rather than at the nearest cell centre.

The particle radius was a measured choice. With 1 spacing, many cells on a tilted surface held a few particles yet read \(\varphi\) just above zero; forced to count as liquid, they got a needless pressure kick each step, which drove a circulation down the high wall and ate the tilt: the steady push of section 3.5 reached only 15 % of its exact centre-of-mass shift. With 1.8 spacings every cell with particles reads as liquid and the tilt holds (section 12).

11.6The pressure solve: conjugate gradients with MIC(0)

Equation (46) is a linear system \(A\boldsymbol\psi = \mathbf b\) with one unknown per liquid cell, about 4 000 at 4 mm. \(A\) is symmetric and positive definite, so the conjugate-gradient method applies: it minimises the error in the norm \(\sqrt{\mathbf e^TA\mathbf e}\) over a growing set of search directions, each conjugate (\(A\)-orthogonal) to the last. Its speed depends on how evenly spread the eigenvalues of \(A\) are, and a Poisson matrix’s are not. A preconditioner \(M \approx A\) that is cheap to invert improves this; the page uses the modified incomplete Cholesky factorisation MIC(0) with tuning constant 0.97 [4, §4.3.4, Fig. 4.2] and a safety threshold 0.25 (a pivot that falls below 0.25 times the diagonal is replaced by the diagonal). The iteration starts from the last step’s pressure and stops when the largest residual is below \(10^{-7}\) of the largest right-hand side. A hard push over 200 steps (the run of tools/fig12/same.cjs) needs 6 462 iterations, 32 a step.

11.7The time step, and the order of one step

A step is at most \(\Delta t = 5\) ms, and short enough that no particle moves more than 0.8 cell:

\[ \Delta t \le \min\Big(5\ \mathrm{ms},\ \frac{0.8\,\Delta x}{\max_p|\mathbf u_p|}\Big), \tag{49} \]

a Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition [4, §3.2.1]; on the 4 mm grid it binds above 0.64 m/s. When it does, the interval to be advanced is cut into equal steps rather than full steps and a short remainder. The cart inside each fluid step is advanced by the symplectic Euler rule in substeps of at most 0.1 ms (first order, and nearly energy-conserving: section 6.4), with the controller sampled every 1 ms and the current and actuator lags integrated exactly. The checks of section 12 all ran at \(\Delta t = 5\) ms; the error due to the time step itself was not measured separately. A swing of the stout takes about 61 steps of 5 ms. Viscosity is applied explicitly, which is stable because \(\nu\Delta t/\Delta x^2 = 3\times10^{-4}\), far below the limit \(1/6\). The splitting of section 11.1 is first-order accurate in \(\Delta t\). One step of the stout, as Fluid.step runs it:

  1. the level set \(\varphi\) and the liquid cells, from the particles (11.5);
  2. particles to grid, (47);
  3. gravity and the jolt, \(\Delta t\,(\mathbf g - \mathbf a_g)\), on every open face; explicit viscosity;
  4. the pressure solve (46) and the velocity correction; the faces closed by the wall and the air near the surface filled by extension;
  5. grid to particles, velocities and gradients, (47);
  6. the particles moved by the midpoint rule; the wall, floor and spill tests of 11.4 and 10.3.

On a 4-core machine one step at 4 mm takes about 15 to 20 ms of computing, so the page runs at roughly a third of real time; the caption shows the rate your computer reaches. Four of the loops (the surface search, the two transfers, the particles’ motion) are shared among your processor’s cores where the browser allows it, each core doing the same arithmetic in the same order for its share, so that the results are identical to the last digit (node tools/fig12/same.cjs prints the fingerprint sha256 7747da757ea9d800; notes/fig12/threads-test.cjs checks it in the browser). The pressure solve cannot be shared this way, because each cell of its triangular sweeps needs the one before.

11.8The live model

The 3D solution runs at about a third of real time, too slow to watch a controller at work. The figure therefore runs, by default, a reduced model built from section 4: the stout as its first three sloshing modes, \(n = 1, 2, 3\) of the family \(m = 1\) that a push along the road excites. Each mode is an oscillator,

\[ \ddot q_n + 2\zeta_n\omega_n\dot q_n + \omega_n^2\Big(1 + \frac{a_z}{g}\Big)q_n = -\beta_n\,a_x, \qquad \omega_n^2 = g k_n\tanh k_nh,\quad k_n = \frac{\xi_{1n}}{R(h)} , \tag{54} \]

with \(\xi_{1n} = 1.8412,\ 5.3314,\ 8.5363\) the first three zeros of \(J_1'\) and \(\beta_n = 2\xi_{1n}\tanh(\xi_{1n}h/R)/(\xi_{1n}^2 - 1)\), as in (17). The tulip is replaced by the cylinder of its radius at the surface, \(R(h) = 39.1\) mm: section 4.6 showed that this cylinder swings within 0.4 % of the 3D solver’s tulip once the grid’s error is taken out. The frequencies are 3.421, 5.821 and 7.365 Hz. Mode 1 is damped by the wall’s boundary layer at the measured rate of section 5, \(\zeta_1 = 0.0053\), and the others by the boundary-layer scaling \(\zeta_n = \zeta_1\sqrt{\omega_1/\omega_n}\). The surface and the push on the glass follow from section 4:

\[ \eta(r, \theta) = h + \sum_n q_n\,\frac{J_1(k_nr)}{J_1(\xi_{1n})}\cos\theta , \qquad P_x = \sum_n\frac{\rho\pi R^3}{\xi_{1n}^2}\,\dot q_n , \tag{55} \]

so the stout pushes the cart by (44) exactly as in the 3D model, and the surface is smooth by construction. Equations (54) are stepped by the symplectic Euler rule in substeps of 0.5 ms, under a hundredth of the fastest period.

The pour. Where the stout at the wall stands above the rim, by \(D(\theta) = h + \sum_nq_n\cos\theta - z_\text{rim} \gt 0\), it pours over the rim like water over a sharp-crested weir. A weir passes, per unit length of crest,

\[ \frac{dV}{dt\,ds} = C_d\,\tfrac23\sqrt{2g}\,D^{3/2} , \qquad C_d = 0.62 . \tag{56} \]

The \(D^{3/2}\) follows from Bernoulli: liquid that falls a height \(z\) below the free surface reaches the speed \(\sqrt{2gz}\), and integrating \(\sqrt{2gz}\,dz\) from 0 to \(D\) gives \(\tfrac23\sqrt{2g}\,D^{3/2}\); \(C_d\) accounts for the contraction of the falling sheet. The value 0.62 is not fitted here: it is the Francis equation of the US Bureau of Reclamation’s Water Measurement Manual [12], \(Q = 3.33\,h^{3/2}L\) in feet and seconds, written in SI (\(3.33/(\tfrac23\sqrt{2\times32.17}) = 0.623\)). The poured volume, integrated round the rim, is subtracted from the glass: the fill \(h\) falls (found from the glass’s volume table), the modes shrink so that their crest loses that volume, and the poured stout’s momentum is counted in \(P_\text{spill}\). It is not drawn as it falls. A weir is a steady flow and a slosh is not, so (56) is an approximation; its test is the comparison below.

11.9The nonlinear live model: a swing that softens and swirls ★

Shake a glass of water at its swinging frequency and two things happen that a linear oscillator cannot do. The swing slows as it grows: a big slosh takes longer to come back than a small one. And past a certain size the water stops swinging back and forth and starts running round the glass, a swirl, though nothing pushed it sideways. The 3D solver shows the first (its swing slows from 3.20 to 3.06 Hz as it grows, Table 5); it cannot show the second, because it computes half the glass with a mirror plane (section 3). This section builds a live model that does both, from the nonlinear modal theory of Narimanov, Moiseev and Lukovsky in the form of Faltinsen, Lukovsky & Timokha [17].

The surface and the energies. Measure lengths in units of the surface radius \(R = R(h)\) and time in \(\sqrt{R/g}\). Write the surface with the modes a swinging first mode drives at second order, over the whole glass:

\[ \begin{aligned} \zeta(r, \theta, t) = {}& R_0(r)\,p_0 + R_1(r)\,\big(p_1\cos\theta + r_1\sin\theta\big) \\ &+ R_2(r)\,\big(p_2\cos2\theta + r_2\sin2\theta\big), \end{aligned} \tag{N1} \]

with \(R_M(r) = \alpha_MJ_M(k_Mr)\), \(k_M\) the first zero of \(J_M'\) (3.8317, 1.8412, 3.0542) and \(\alpha_M\) set by \(\int_0^1 rR_M^2\,dr = 1\). The pair \((p_1, r_1)\) is the first mode along the road and across it; \(p_0\) (axisymmetric) and \((p_2, r_2)\) are what their product makes. The potential energy of the raised surface is exact for an upright wall, \(V = \tfrac12\iint\zeta^2\,dA\). For the kinetic energy write the potential as a sum over the same five modes, \(\Phi = \sum_kP_k\,R_k(r)Z_k(z)T_k(\theta)\) with \(Z_k = \cosh k_k(z + h)/\cosh k_kh\), and integrate it over the liquid up to the moving surface. Two families of integrals result [17, eqs. 2.12–2.14]:

\[ A_k(\boldsymbol\beta) = \iint T_kR_k\!\int_{-h}^{\zeta}\!Z_k\,dz\,dA, \qquad A_{kl}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \int_{Q(t)}\nabla\phi_k\cdot\nabla\phi_l\,dQ , \tag{N2} \]

\(\boldsymbol\beta = (p_0, p_1, r_1, p_2, r_2)\). The surface moves with the liquid (the kinematic condition), which in modal form reads \(J\dot{\boldsymbol\beta} = A\,\mathbf P\) with \(J_{ki} = \partial A_k/\partial\beta_i\) [17, eq. 2.9]. So \(\mathbf P = A^{-1}J\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}\), and the kinetic energy \(T = \tfrac12\mathbf P^TA\mathbf P\) becomes

\[ T = \tfrac12\,\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}^TM(\boldsymbol\beta)\,\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}, \qquad M = J^TA^{-1}J . \tag{N3} \]

The mass matrix depends on the surface because the liquid’s shape does. Lagrange’s equations of \(L = T - V - a_x\iint x\,\zeta\,dA\) (the last term the potential of the fictitious force in the glass’s frame) are the fully nonlinear modal equations of these five modes [17, eq. 2.10]; for potential flow this \(L\) is Luke’s variational principle with the kinematic condition built in (Miles).

Ordering. Expand the \(z\)-integrals in (N2) about the mean surface: \(\int_{-h}^{\zeta}f\,dz = \int_{-h}^0f\,dz + f(0)\zeta + \tfrac12f'(0)\zeta^2 + \dots\) Then \(M = M_0 + M_1(\boldsymbol\beta) + M_2(\boldsymbol\beta) + \dots\), linear and quadratic in the coordinates. Narimanov and Moiseev’s ordering [17, eq. 2.16] says how much of this to keep: if the forcing is of order \(\epsilon\), the swing \(p_1, r_1\) is of order \(\epsilon^{1/3}\) and the modes it drives, \(p_0, p_2, r_2\), of order \(\epsilon^{2/3}\). Keeping in each equation everything up to order \(\epsilon\) gives, for the swing along the road (the equation of \(r_1\) follows by turning the glass a quarter turn),

\[ \begin{aligned} &\ddot p_1 + \sigma_1^2p_1 + d_1\,p_1\big(\ddot p_1p_1 + \ddot r_1r_1 + \dot p_1^2 + \dot r_1^2\big) \\ &\ + d_2\big[r_1(\ddot p_1r_1 - \ddot r_1p_1) + 2\dot r_1(\dot p_1r_1 - \dot r_1p_1)\big] + (\text{products with } p_0, p_2, r_2) = -\kappa_1 P_1\,a_x , \end{aligned} \tag{N4} \]

and for the driven modes, linear oscillators forced by squares of the swing,

\[ \ddot p_2 + \sigma_2^2p_2 + d_7\big(\dot p_1^2 - \dot r_1^2\big) + d_9\big(\ddot p_1p_1 - \ddot r_1r_1\big) = 0 , \tag{N5} \]

with the like equations for \(r_2\) and \(p_0\) [17, eqs. 2.17–2.18; with one radial mode per family this is Lukovsky’s five-mode system, their eq. 2.23]. A swing raises its crests (\(p_2\)) and lifts the middle and the edge of the surface (\(p_0\)) twice per period; through the products in (N4) these, together with the cubic terms in \(d_1\) and \(d_2\), shift the swing’s frequency with its size (in the steady state the shift is set by one combination of them all, [17]’s \(m_1\), eq. 3.13a). Swirl comes from the \(d_2\) terms, which couple the swing along the road to the one across it.

The coefficients, and a check. The coefficients are integrals of Bessel functions, which [17] gives in its supplementary material; that file could not be reached for this note. The page’s coefficients were derived instead from (N2)–(N3) as written above: tools/fig12/nm_coeffs.py evaluates the integrals by quadrature, forms \(M\) to second order, takes Lagrange’s equations symbolically and applies the ordering. The check is [17]’s Table 1, the coefficients for a tank of depth \(h = R\) with one radial mode: \(d_1 = 1.758023\), \(d_2 = -1.083355\). The script gives 1.758023 and \(-1.083355\), every printed digit, and the remaining cubic terms of (N4) have exactly the combinations \(d_1 - 2d_2\), \(d_1 - d_2\) and \(2d_2\) that (N4) requires. For the glass, \(h/R = 125/39.1 = 3.197\): \(\sigma_1^2 = 1.8412\) (so 3.421 Hz, as before), \(d_1 = 1.5633\), \(d_2 = -1.2106\); from \(h/R = 1.5\) on they change by under 2 %, the deep-liquid limit (python3 tools/fig12/nm_coeffs.py 1 1.5 3.197).

The live model. The page runs (N4)–(N5) with these coefficients, the modes \(n = 2, 3\) of (54) along the road added as before, damping \(2\zeta\sigma\dot\beta\) on each coordinate (\(\zeta_1 = 0.0053\) from section 5, the others by the boundary-layer scaling), and heave as \(\sigma^2 \to \sigma^2(1 + a_z/g)\). The equations are linear in the accelerations, so each substep of 0.5 ms solves a 5 × 5 system for them and steps by the symplectic Euler rule (NMFluid). The surface is drawn from (N1) over the whole glass; the pour (56) uses the stout’s height at the wall at each angle, and the crest’s volume above the mean is shrunk by what poured out. A swirl is a broken left–right symmetry, and a real glass always has a little asymmetry: \(r_1\) starts at \(10^{-5}R\) (0.4 µm), a seed. Where it stops. The theory assumes a small swing and no breaking. A crest breaks when its downward acceleration reaches \(g\), at a wall amplitude \(g/\omega_{11}^2 = R/\xi_{11} = 21.2\) mm, a lean of 42 mm (section 8.5). The nonlinear model, like the linear one, does not know this: beyond 21.2 mm at the wall its waves keep their smooth shape, and the numbers above for the 4 and 6 m/s² pushes and the 25 mm bump are the model’s, not the stout’s. There the 3D solver, one click away, is the reference. The tulip is still replaced by the cylinder of its surface radius, and the third-order modes that [17] adds (they move the steady response by 5–15 % against the five-mode system) are left out.

14What the picture shows

The surface drawn is the height of the column of stout over each square of the floor: the volume of the particles standing on it, matched to the volume the glass’s shape opens up to that height, both smoothed over one cell (Fluid.columnHeights). That is the surface the computation holds, averaged over depth; a wave curling over cannot be shown this way. For the picture the column heights are joined by a bicubic Catmull–Rom spline, which passes through every column value and has a continuous slope, so the sheet bends smoothly instead of stepping from cell to cell; within 3.5 cells of the wall, where the columns are cut by the glass and hold too few particles, the height is extended straight out from the columns 3.5 and 7 cells in (a wider baseline than the solver’s own reading, which halves the ripple of the drawn edge at rest). The readout “front − back wall” is the solver’s own reading at the front wall minus that at the back, at the radius \(R(h)\), the quantity of Figure 2 and section 13; it can differ from the drawn edge by a millimetre or two.

The camera. The figure is seen through a pinhole camera that rides with the cart: it looks down at 22° and is turned 18° towards the front, from 2.2 m away. A point at depth \(D\) in front of the camera and at \((X_c, Y_c)\) across and up from its line of sight lands on the screen at \((F X_c/D,\ -F Y_c/D)\) from the picture’s centre, with \(F\) the focal length; near things look larger, and the road converges. The head’s sheet is shaded by one light from its normal \(\mathbf n = (-\partial_x\eta, -\partial_y\eta, 1)/\sqrt{1 + |\nabla\eta|^2}\), so it brightens where it tilts towards the light. The stout’s colour across the glass follows the length \(\ell\) of the light’s path through it, a transmission \(e^{-\kappa\ell}\) (Beer–Lambert): ruby at the thin edges, black through the middle; \(\kappa\) is a drawing choice, not a measured property of stout.

The cart is drawn from the stored state at every frame: the wheel of radius \(R_w\) centred on the axle at \((x, z_w)\), with spokes that turn as it rolls; the spring \(k\) and the damper \(c\) between the axle’s bar and the body; the body, 22 cm by 3 cm, with its bottom at \(z_b\); the glass standing on it. The camera rides with the cart, so the road slides past; its ticks are every 10 cm and the labels give the position \(x\) along the road (the cart starts at \(x = 0.5\) m). The active suspension, the motor and the gearbox are not drawn.

The live charts. In the figure’s sky, at the left, the last 4 s of the stout’s lean (front minus back wall) and of the motor current, with the rim drawn as the limit of the lean; at the right, the first mode’s phase portrait, \(q_1\) against \(\dot q_1/\omega_1\), in which a free swing is a circle, damping makes it a spiral, and the rim is the pair of vertical lines a spilling swing crosses. In both models the lean drawn in red is what the probes read, the black line is twice the Kalman filter’s estimate \(\hat q\) (section 8.5), and the portrait is the estimate, \((\hat q, \hat{\dot q}/\omega)\): what the controller believes, not the solver’s state. In the live model the surface is drawn exactly as (55) gives it, and poured stout is subtracted, not drawn.

The head is drawn, not computed. A cream layer 12 mm thick is laid on the computed surface. Real foam is a different material, lighter and partly elastic, and it damps the swing; it is not in this model. Spilled drops are drawn where the solver moves them, with their mirror images, as short streaks along their velocity. The data panel under the figure holds every number the page computes, written where it is computed, and checks each against its formula and against the picture.

15What the model leaves out

  • The glass’s exact imperial shape: the profile is the US tulip stretched to the Guinness size (section 2).
  • Stout’s own density and viscosity (water’s are used), the foam head, and surface tension, which would raise the swing frequency by under 1 % (section 5).
  • The boundary layer at the wall: the wall is free-slip and the damping is the method’s, not the liquid’s (section 5).
  • Swirl and any motion that breaks the mirror symmetry (section 3.4).
  • Air drag on drops; drops that land stay where they land.
  • The drive: the motor’s numbers are a plausible small drive, not a measured one; no inductance, gear losses or backlash; the speed and body-speed sensors are ideal; the skyhook actuator supplies any power.
  • Pitch and roll of the cart, and the extra rolling on a slope (section 6.3).

The next model would solve the whole glass, not half, so that swirl can appear; resolve the wall layer with a no-slip wall (cells of a fraction of a millimetre there, or a boundary-layer model added to the free-slip wall), which would give the real damping; and carry the foam as a second, lighter layer.
