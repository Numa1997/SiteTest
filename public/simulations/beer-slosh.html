Fig. 12 · lecture note · fluid dynamics

Sloshing in a pint glass

Numa Koudsie · for readers who know Newton’s laws, derivatives and the idea of a normal mode; the parts marked ★ use vector calculus and Bessel functions, and their results are restated without them

Abstract. Anyone who has carried a full pint through a crowded pub knows that the stout does not simply ride along: it rocks, it climbs the wall on one side, and the head goes over the rim. We build the physics of that rocking from Newton’s second law for a fluid. In the frame of the moving glass the stout feels gravity plus the glass’s acceleration; inside it the flow has no rotation, so its velocity is the gradient of a potential; at the surface, gravity pulls the stout back to level. The result is a set of sloshing modes, each an oscillator driven by the glass’s sideways acceleration and pumped by its vertical acceleration. For a straight glass the modes are Bessel functions; for the real Guinness tulip, measured from a photograph and rebuilt in Blender, we compute them by finite elements and check the program against the straight glass. Then we follow the energy: into the thin boundary layers on the wall, into the skin of the surface, and into the friction of the head on the glass, which turns out to be the largest loss. A pint with its head settles in about six seconds; without it, in about eleven.

01Carrying a pint

Pick up a full glass and walk. For a few steps nothing happens; then the surface starts to rock, a little higher on each step, until the stout climbs the side of the glass and the head spills over your fingers. Stop walking and the rocking does not stop at once: it dies away over a few seconds, and the dark liquid settles back under its cream-coloured head.

Three things are already visible in that experience. First, the stout has a rhythm of its own: it rocks at a definite frequency, about three times a second in a pint glass, whatever your hand does. Second, your hand can feed that rhythm: if your steps come at a rate close to it, each step adds to the swing, and the amplitude grows. Third, something takes the energy away again, because the rocking dies when you stand still. A physicist recognises a driven, damped oscillator. This note shows that the stout in a glass is a whole family of such oscillators, called sloshing modes, derives their frequencies from the laws of fluid motion and the shape of the glass, and works out, one by one, the processes that damp them.

We use the glass Guinness serves its draught stout in, the 20-imperial-ounce tulip pint (568 mL), and the stout itself at its serving temperature. Nothing steers the glass yet except your hand or a shaker table; a controller that carries a pint without spilling is the next chapter of this figure.

How claims are marked

A statement with a number in square brackets, such as [3], is a fact taken from that source, listed at the end. A statement marked computed is a fact produced by this page’s own code or by the scripts listed in section 9, which anyone can rerun. Inference marks reasoning from those facts, and opinion marks a judgement. Everything else is derived on the page.

02The glass and the stout

Before any equation we need two things: the shape of the container, and the properties of what is in it.

The shape, from a photograph

Guinness publishes no drawing of its glass, and retail listings give only an overall size: 160 mm tall and 85 mm across at the widest, holding 568 mL, one imperial pint [8]. So we measured the shape from a side-on product photograph [9]. We write \(Z\) for the height above the foot of the glass. In each row of pixels the outermost dark pixels mark the edge of the glass, and half their distance is the outer radius at that height. The rim and the foot appear in the photograph as thin ellipses, because the camera looks slightly down on the glass; their centres, not their lowest points, mark the planes of rim and foot. Setting the distance between those planes to the published 160 mm fixes the scale at 0.393 mm per pixel computed. The outline was then smoothed by a spline allowed to depart from the measured points by 0.2 mm on average, about half a pixel, so that the photograph’s pixel steps do not appear as ripples in the wall.

A silhouette shows only the outside. The inside needs two more numbers. The floor of the cavity shows through the glass in the photograph, about 8 mm above the foot (uncertain by about 3 mm, because the thick base bends the light). The wall thickness cannot be seen at all; we take 2.0 mm, a typical value for a toughened pint glass, as an assumption. Two checks then say the result is the real glass. The widest diameter comes out as 84.1 mm, against the published 85 mm, a difference of 1 %. And the cavity holds 603.7 mL to the brim, so that 568 mL fills it to 151.9 mm, 8.1 mm below the rim computed: a pint glass must hold its pint with room to spare, and this one does. Blender revolved the same profile into a closed mesh of 96 sides and measured 603.3 mL; the 0.4 mL difference is exactly what a 96-sided polygon loses against a circle, a factor \(\tfrac{96}{2\pi}\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{96} = 0.99929\) computed.

03A fluid in a moving glass

On a train that brakes, your coffee leans forward as if gravity had tipped. That is the first idea: in the frame of the glass, the glass’s acceleration acts like an extra gravity pointing the other way.

Put coordinates on the glass: \(x\) and \(y\) horizontal, \(z\) vertical, all fixed to the glass and moving with it. Let the glass move without turning, its own position being \((x_g, y_g, z_g)\) in the room, and write its acceleration \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g = (\ddot x_g, \ddot y_g, \ddot z_g)\); a dot means a time derivative. Take a small parcel of stout of density \(\rho\) and velocity \(\mathbf u\) relative to the glass. In the room its acceleration is that of the glass plus its own, \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g + D\mathbf u/Dt\), where \(D\mathbf u/Dt = \partial\mathbf u/\partial t + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\) is the acceleration of the parcel as it moves along, not of a fixed point. Newton’s law per unit volume, written in the room and then with \(\rho\ddot{\mathbf x}_g\) moved to the right-hand side, reads

\[ \rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \rho\left(\mathbf g - \ddot{\mathbf x}_g\right). \tag{1} \]

Each term is a force per unit volume. On the left is mass times acceleration. \(-\nabla p\) is the push of the pressure \(p\): a parcel is pushed from where the pressure is high to where it is low. \(\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) is the rubbing of neighbouring layers that slide past each other, with \(\mu\) the viscosity. \(\rho\mathbf g\), with \(\mathbf g = (0, 0, -g)\), is the weight, and \(-\rho\ddot{\mathbf x}_g\) is the inertial force of the accelerating frame, the push your coffee feels on the braking train. This is the Navier–Stokes equation. The stout is incompressible: what flows into a small box must flow out, which is written \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0\).

The two body forces combine into the gradient of one potential,

\[ \mathbf g - \ddot{\mathbf x}_g = -\nabla\Psi, \qquad \Psi = (g + \ddot z_g)\,z + \ddot x_g\,x + \ddot y_g\,y , \tag{2} \]

as differentiating \(\Psi\) shows. \(\Psi\) is a potential energy per kilogram, in J/kg = m²/s². So the stout feels an effective gravity \(\mathbf g_\text{eff} = -\nabla\Psi\): tilted when the glass speeds up or slows down sideways, stronger when the glass accelerates upward (starts a lift or stops a descent), weaker when it accelerates downward. A glass carried at constant velocity, up, down or sideways, changes nothing. If \(g + \ddot z_g\) turns negative, the glass is accelerated downward faster than a falling stone and the stout leaves the floor; the page warns when that happens.

Why the interior has no rotation ★

Divide (1) by \(\rho\) and take its curl. The curl of any gradient is zero, so both \(\nabla p\) and \(\nabla\Psi\) drop out. With the vorticity \(\mathbf w = \nabla\times\mathbf u\), the identity \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = \nabla(\tfrac12 u^2) - \mathbf u\times\mathbf w\), and the fact that the curl and the Laplacian commute, we are left with

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf w}{\partial t} = \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf w) + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf w , \tag{3} \]

with \(\nu = \mu/\rho\). Every term on the right contains \(\mathbf w\). A settled pint, \(\mathbf w = 0\), therefore stays free of vorticity however the glass is shaken, except where something outside (3) creates it. (A freshly poured Guinness is not settled: its sinking bubbles drive a circulation [3], [4], which we wait out.) What creates vorticity is the wall: the stout must stick to the glass, while a flow without vorticity slides along it. The conflict is settled in a thin Stokes layer. In Stokes’s exact solution for a fluid beside a wall oscillating at angular frequency \(\omega\), the disturbance falls by a factor \(e\) over the distance

\[ \delta = \sqrt{2\nu/\omega} , \tag{4} \]

the distance vorticity diffuses in a time \(1/\omega\). For the slowest slosh of our pint, \(\omega = 20.4\) rad/s, this is \(\delta = 0.45\) mm, about one percent of the glass’s radius computed.

The flow outside the layers

Key result · irrotational interior

Outside boundary layers about 0.45 mm thick, the stout does not rotate, and its velocity relative to the glass is the gradient of a velocity potential \(\phi\), \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\): the flow runs downhill on the landscape \(\phi\). Incompressibility then reads \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\), Laplace’s equation. Viscosity drops out of the interior entirely, because for \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) the viscous force is \(\mu\nabla(\nabla^2\phi) = 0\); it acts only in the layers, where it takes energy away (section 7).

With \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), every term of (1) is a gradient (the convective term becomes \(\nabla(\tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2)\)), so (1) can be integrated once in space:

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2 + \frac{p}{\rho} + \Psi = C(t), \tag{5} \]

the unsteady Bernoulli equation. The function \(C(t)\) can be absorbed into \(\phi\): only the gradient of \(\phi\) is a velocity, so adding a function of time alone to \(\phi\) changes nothing physical.

The model in one place

The stout is incompressible and settled; the glass translates without turning; the interior is irrotational; the surface stays close to level (linear theory); the head is a layer of mass that rides on the surface and rubs on the wall; the edge of the stout on the glass adds a constant damping rate. The numbers that decide what matters, for the default pint: depth over wave length \(\lambda D = 6.1\) (deep), boundary layer over radius \(\delta/R = 0.011\) (thin), surface tension over gravity \(\sigma\lambda^2/(\rho g) = 0.010\) (small), head mass over sloshing mass \(\kappa_{11} = 0.128\), damping ratio \(\zeta_{11} = 0.024\), and the wave height over the radius, which the page shows live computed. Each is defined where it first appears below.

04Linear sloshing in a straight glass

Laplace’s equation alone has no waves in it. The waves come from the free surface, where gravity pulls a raised patch of stout back down, and the stout below must move to let it.

Boundary conditions

From here on \(z\) is measured upward from the calm surface, so the liquid fills \(-D \le z \le 0\), with \(D\) its depth; heights above the foot keep the letter \(Z\), and the calm surface sits at \(Z_s\). Let the stout surface be raised by \(\eta(x, y, t)\) above its calm level. Three conditions close the problem.

On the wall and the floor the stout cannot pass through the glass: \(\partial\phi/\partial n = 0\), with \(n\) the direction normal to the glass.

At the surface, kinematics: a parcel on the surface stays on the surface, so the surface rises at the vertical speed of the stout there: \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi\) to first order in the small amplitude.

At the surface, dynamics: the pressure just under the surface equals the air pressure (we come back to surface tension and the head in section 5). Putting \(p = p_\text{air}\) and \(z = \eta\) in (5), dropping the square of the small velocity, and moving everything that depends on time alone into \(\phi\),

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + (g + \ddot z_g)\,\eta + \ddot x_g\,x + \ddot y_g\,y = 0 \quad\text{at } z = 0 . \tag{6} \]

Linearisation means evaluating both surface conditions at the calm level \(z = 0\) rather than at \(z = \eta\). The error is of the order of the wave slope, the height of the wave divided by its horizontal scale; the page warns when the wave at the wall exceeds a fifth of the radius.

Check: a steady push tilts the surface. If the glass accelerates sideways at a constant \(\ddot x_g\), the stout comes to rest relative to the glass, \(\phi\) is constant in time, and (6) gives \(\eta = -(\ddot x_g/g)\,x\): a plane surface, low on the side the glass accelerates toward, tilted at \(\tan\alpha = \ddot x_g/g\). That is the coffee on the braking train.

The modes of a cylinder

First let the glass stand still. Eliminating \(\eta\) between the two surface conditions (differentiate (6) in time and put \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi\)) gives one condition on \(\phi\) alone, \(\partial_t^2\phi + g\,\partial_z\phi = 0\) at \(z = 0\). In a cylinder of radius \(R\), with polar coordinates \(r, \theta\), the solution of Laplace’s equation that satisfies it and the wall and floor conditions is a standing wave

\[ \phi = C\,J_m(kr)\cos(m\theta)\,\frac{\cosh k(z + D)}{\cosh kD}\,\cos\omega t . \tag{7} \]

Here \(J_m\) is the Bessel function of order \(m\), which behaves like a cosine whose swings weaken as \(r\) grows; it plays the part the sine plays on a guitar string, adapted to a round boundary. The factor \(\cosh k(z + D)\) makes the motion strongest at the surface and gives zero vertical velocity at the floor. No flow through the side wall requires \(J_m'(kR) = 0\), so \(kR\) must be one of the zeros \(\xi_{mn}\) of \(J_m'\): \(\xi_{11} = 1.8412\), \(\xi_{21} = 3.0542\), \(\xi_{01} = 3.8317\), \(\xi_{12} = 5.3314\) (computed with Wolfram Language and with SciPy, which agree to all digits). Putting (7) into the surface condition, where \(\partial_z\) of the cosh factor is \(k\tanh kD\),

\[ \omega_{mn}^2 = g\,\lambda_{mn}, \qquad \lambda_{mn} = k_{mn}\tanh(k_{mn}D), \qquad k_{mn} = \xi_{mn}/R . \tag{8} \]

Where (7) comes from ★

In polar coordinates Laplace’s equation reads \(\partial_r^2\phi + r^{-1}\partial_r\phi + r^{-2}\partial_\theta^2\phi + \partial_z^2\phi = 0\). Try \(\phi = \Phi(r)\cos(m\theta)Z(z)\) and divide by \(\phi\): the terms in \(z\) alone and in \(r\) alone must each be constant, say \(Z''/Z = k^2\) and \(\Phi'' + \Phi'/r + (k^2 - m^2/r^2)\Phi = 0\). The second is Bessel’s equation; of its two solutions only \(J_m(kr)\) stays finite on the axis (the other, \(Y_m\), is infinite there). \(Z'' = k^2Z\) with \(Z'(-D) = 0\) gives the cosh factor. The time factor \(\cos\omega t\) follows from the surface condition.

The number \(m\) counts the nodal diameters, lines across the surface that stay level: \(m = 1\) is the familiar rocking, with one side up while the other is down; \(m = 2\) has two nodal diameters; \(m = 0\) is a ring wave that heaves the middle against the edge. The number \(n\) orders the modes of one \(m\) by frequency; mode \((m, n)\) has \(n - 1\) nodal circles when \(m \ge 1\), and \(n\) when \(m = 0\). Turn on the shaker on the page and the 3D view shows mode (1,1); the others appear when the stout is jolted.

Example 4.1 · a straight half-litre glass

A cylinder of radius \(R = 40\) mm filled to \(D = 99.5\) mm holds \(\pi R^2D = 0.500\) L. Then \(k_{11} = 46.03\) m⁻¹ and \(\tanh(k_{11}D) = 0.9998\): the glass is “deep”, since \(\tanh\) has saturated. So \(\omega_{11} = \sqrt{9.81\times46.02} = 21.25\) rad/s and \(f_{11} = \omega_{11}/2\pi = 3.382\) Hz, confirmed with Wolfram Language. Once \(kD \gt 3\), \(\tanh kD \gt 0.995\) and the depth changes \(\omega\) by less than 0.3 %; in our pint \(\lambda D = 6.1\) computed.

Key result · deep sloshing

In a deep glass the slowest mode rocks at \(\omega_{11} \approx \sqrt{1.84\,g/R}\), set only by the radius of the surface: like a pendulum of length \(R/1.84\), about 21 mm for a pint. A wider glass sloshes more slowly.

05The real glass, by finite elements

The tulip glass is not a cylinder: it narrows to a waist, swells, and leans inward again near the rim. Separation of variables fails, but the problem keeps its structure, and a computer can solve it on the measured shape.

The glass is still a body of revolution, so each mode still varies as \(\cos m\theta\) around the axis and still oscillates as \(\cos\omega t\). What changes is the shape \(\Phi(r, z)\) in a vertical cut, and the number \(\lambda = \omega^2/g\) that sets the frequency. For each fill the computer finds the numbers \(\lambda_n\) and the shapes \(\Phi_n\) of the modes kept; the method is the next part, and can be skipped.

The eigenvalue problem ★

In the region \(0 \le r \le R_i(Z)\) of the vertical cut, with \(R_i\) the inner radius of the glass, \(\Phi\) satisfies

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial z^2} - \frac{m^2}{r^2}\Phi = 0, \\ \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n} = 0 \ \text{on the glass}, \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} = \lambda\Phi \ \text{at } z = 0 , \end{gathered} \tag{9} \]

where the last condition is \(\partial_t^2\phi + g\partial_z\phi = 0\) of section 4 with \(\phi \propto \cos\omega t\), and \(\Phi = 0\) on the axis when \(m \ge 1\). The eigenvalue appears only in a boundary condition; mathematicians call this a Steklov problem. Multiply the equation by any smooth function \(v\) and by \(r\), integrate over the region and integrate by parts once. The wall and floor terms vanish because \(\partial_n\Phi = 0\), and the surface term is replaced using its condition:

\[ \int\!\!\int\left(\Phi_r v_r + \Phi_z v_z + \frac{m^2}{r^2}\Phi v\right) r\,dr\,dz = \lambda\int_0^{R}\Phi\,v\;r\,dr , \tag{10} \]

with \(R = R_i(Z_s)\) the radius of the calm surface. This weak form is what the finite-element method solves. The cut is divided into triangles; on each, \(\Phi\) is a quadratic polynomial fixed by its values at the three corners and the three mid-edges; requiring (10) for every such function \(v\) gives a matrix equation \(\mathsf K\boldsymbol\Phi = \lambda\,\mathsf M\boldsymbol\Phi\), in which \(\mathsf M\) has entries only at the surface nodes. Eliminating the interior nodes leaves a small symmetric eigenvalue problem on the surface computed.

Checking the program on the cylinder

A program that has not reproduced a known answer has proved nothing. For a cylinder of 40 mm radius and 100 mm depth the exact eigenvalues are \(\lambda = k\tanh kD\) from (8). On a mesh of 16 × 40 cells, each cut into two quadratic triangles, the program gives \(\lambda_{11} = 46.02036\) m⁻¹ against the exact 46.02035, a relative error of \(3\times10^{-7}\); the worst of nine modes, (0,3), is off by \(2.4\times10^{-4}\). Halving the cells reduces the error of mode (1,1) 16-fold, the fourth order expected of quadratic elements computed. The integrals the damping needs (section 7) agree with the exact Bessel integrals, evaluated independently in Wolfram Language, to \(3\times10^{-5}\) for the slowest mode and to \(3\times10^{-3}\) for the other modes kept.

What the tulip shape does

Run on the measured glass for every fill from 300 to 590 mL, the program gives the frequencies of Figure 2. Below the widest point the wall leans outward as it rises; above it, the wall leans inward. With 18 mm of head on both, the outward-leaning glass sloshes more slowly than a straight glass of the same surface radius and depth (3.14 against 3.25 Hz at 300 mL), and the inward-leaning one faster (3.37 against 3.30 Hz at 590 mL) computed. Inference: near the surface, where the sloshing flow lives, an outward-leaning wall gives the stout more room to swing sideways for the same rise at the wall, which acts like a heavier pendulum bob; an inward-leaning wall squeezes the same flow into less room, a stiffer spring.

The head and the surface tension

Two corrections change the surface condition (6). Surface tension makes the surface behave like a stretched drum skin: a curved surface pulls back toward flat with an extra pressure \(-\sigma\nabla_h^2\eta\), where \(\nabla_h^2\) is the Laplacian along the surface. For a mode it multiplies \(\eta\) by \(\lambda^2\) (exactly so in a straight glass, approximately in the tulip), which raises \(\omega^2\) by the fraction \(\sigma\lambda^2/(\rho g)\): 1.0 % for our pint computed. We let the edge of the surface slide freely on the glass. The head is heavier. Treat it as a layer of mass \(M_f\) per area that is lifted and lowered with the surface: Newton’s law for the layer says the stout must push on it with an extra pressure \(M_f\,\ddot\eta\). In (6) this adds \((M_f/\rho)\,\ddot\eta = (M_f\lambda/\rho)\,\partial_t^2\phi\) for a mode, so that \(\partial_t^2\phi\,(1 + M_f\lambda/\rho) + g\lambda\phi = 0\). With both corrections,

\[ \omega_n^2 = \frac{g\lambda_n + \sigma\lambda_n^3/\rho}{1 + \kappa_n}, \qquad \kappa_n = \frac{M_f\lambda_n}{\rho} . \tag{11} \]

The added-mass ratio \(\kappa_n\) compares the head’s mass with the mass of stout that sloshes, a layer of depth \(1/\lambda\). For the default pint, \(\kappa_{11} = 0.128\), and the head lowers \(f_1\) from 3.445 to 3.244 Hz computed. Opinion: this is the crudest piece of the model. A real head also has a little stiffness of its own, and its liquid fraction is not measured; the slider for \(\varepsilon_f\) lets you see how much it matters.

Example 5.1 · the default pint

500 mL of stout fills the tulip to \(Z_s = 137.5\) mm above the foot, where the surface has radius \(R = 39.4\) mm and the stout is \(D = 129.5\) mm deep. The program gives \(\lambda_{11} = 47.27\) m⁻¹, so the bare frequency is \(\sqrt{9.81\times47.27}/2\pi = 3.427\) Hz; surface tension raises it to 3.445 Hz, and the head lowers it to 3.244 Hz computed.

06Driving the modes: hand and shaker

Now let the glass move. The motion enters only through the surface condition (6), and there it does two different things.

Write the surface displacement as a sum over modes, \(\eta = \sum_n a_n(t)\,\psi_n(r)\cos m\theta\) plus the matching \(\sin m\theta\) copies, where \(\psi_n(r) = \Phi_n(r, 0)\) is the mode’s surface shape, scaled so that \(\psi_n = 1\) at the wall: then \(a_n\) is the rise of the stout at the wall, in metres. The potential is \(\phi = \sum_n (\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\,\Phi_n\cos m\theta\), because the kinematic condition requires \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi = \lambda_n\phi\) at the surface for each mode. In (6), write the sideways term as \(\ddot x_g\,x = \ddot x_g\,r\cos\theta\). Put both sums into (6), multiply by \(\psi_j\cos m\theta\, r\) and integrate over the surface. The modes are orthogonal on the surface (a consequence of (10) being symmetric), so one equation is left for each mode. The \(\theta\)-integral of \(r\cos\theta\cos m\theta\) vanishes unless \(m = 1\), so only the \(m = 1\) modes feel the sideways push:

\[ \ddot a_n + 2\gamma_n\dot a_n + \omega_n^2\left(1 + \frac{\ddot z_g}{g}\right)a_n = -\frac{\lambda_n Q_n}{1 + \kappa_n}\,\ddot x_g, \qquad Q_n = \frac{\int_0^R \psi_n\,r^2\,dr}{\int_0^R \psi_n^2\,r\,dr} , \tag{12} \]

with the damping rate \(\gamma_n\) of section 7 added. (For clarity the \(\sigma\) term of (11) is not repeated; the page keeps it.) The \(\sin\) copies obey the same equation with \(\ddot y_g\) in place of \(\ddot x_g\). The minus sign is the coffee on the train: accelerate the glass toward \(+x\) and the stout rises on the \(-x\) side.

Key result · two ways in

Sideways acceleration drives. The term \(\ddot x_g\,x\) in (6) is a tilt of the effective gravity; it pushes on every mode whose shape has a sideways lean, which in a round glass means \(m = 1\) only, through the coupling \(Q_n\). Vertical acceleration pumps. \(\ddot z_g\) multiplies \(a_n\): it changes the strength of gravity, the spring constant of every mode, without pushing any of them. It can only amplify a slosh that is already there.

Checks. For a straight glass the coupling has a closed form: with \(\psi = J_1(kr)/J_1(\xi)\) and the Bessel integrals \(\int_0^R r^2J_1(kr)\,dr = R^2J_2(\xi)/k\) and \(\int_0^R rJ_1^2\,dr = \tfrac{R^2}{2}(1 - 1/\xi^2)J_1^2(\xi)\), using \(J_2(\xi) = J_1(\xi)/\xi\) at a zero of \(J_1'\), one finds \(Q = 2R/(\xi^2 - 1)\). The finite-element program reproduces it to six digits, in the form \(2R/((\xi^2-1)J_1(\xi)) = 0.057528\) m that belongs to the scaling \(\psi = J_1(kr)\) computed. And a steady push must give the tilted plane of section 4: with \(\ddot a = \dot a = 0\), (12) gives \(a_n = -Q_n\ddot x_g/g\) (head aside), and the modes must add up to \(\sum_n Q_n\psi_n(r) = r\). The three \(m = 1\) modes the page keeps reproduce 93 % of the tilt at the wall; the rest lies in faster modes it leaves out computed.

Resonance and the damping ratio

If the glass is shaken sideways as \(x_g = A\sin\Omega t\) at the mode’s own frequency (the page eases the shaking in over 0.3 s, \(x_g = A(1 - e^{-t/0.3\,\mathrm s})\sin\Omega t\), so that it starts without a jolt; the steady state is the same), at \(\Omega = \omega_n\) the spring and inertia terms of (12) cancel, and only the damping is left to balance the push: \(2\gamma_n\omega_n a_n = \lambda_nQ_nA\omega_n^2/(1 + \kappa_n)\). The damping ratio \(\zeta_n = \gamma_n/\omega_n\), shown on the page as \(\zeta_1\), measures how much of its energy a mode loses per radian of swing.

Example 6.1 · a fifth of a millimetre

Shake the default pint sideways by \(A = 0.2\) mm at \(f_1 = 3.24\) Hz. The glass’s acceleration is only \(A\omega^2 = 0.083\) m/s², under a hundredth of \(g\); yet the steady wave at the wall is 5.6 mm computed, 28 times the shaking. That is what the button Sideways at f₁ does.

Pumping: Faraday’s waves ★

Shake the glass up and down, \(z_g = A\sin\Omega t\), and (12) for one mode becomes \(\ddot a + 2\gamma\dot a + \omega^2(1 - \epsilon\sin\Omega t)\,a = 0\) with \(\epsilon = A\Omega^2/g\): a pendulum whose gravity is modulated, the Mathieu equation. Its strongest instability lies at \(\Omega = 2\omega\), twice the mode’s frequency. To see it, write \(a = P(t)\cos\omega t + S(t)\sin\omega t\) with slowly varying \(P, S\) and the usual condition \(\dot P\cos\omega t + \dot S\sin\omega t = 0\). The equation then gives \(\dot P = -F\sin\omega t/\omega\) and \(\dot S = F\cos\omega t/\omega\), with \(F = -2\gamma\dot a + \epsilon\omega^2\sin(2\omega t)\,a\). Averaged over one period, using \(\langle\sin^2\omega t\cos^2\omega t\rangle = \tfrac18\),

\[ \dot P = -\left(\gamma + \frac{\epsilon\omega}{4}\right)P, \qquad \dot S = \left(\frac{\epsilon\omega}{4} - \gamma\right)S . \]

The \(\sin\) part grows when the gain beats the damping,

\[ \frac{\epsilon\,\omega}{4} \gt \gamma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A \gt \frac{4\zeta g}{\Omega^2} . \tag{13} \]

These standing waves at half the drive frequency are called Faraday waves. For the default pint the threshold is \(A = 0.58\) mm at \(\Omega = 2\omega_1\); the button Up and down at 2f₁ shakes 2 mm, \(\epsilon = 0.34\), for which (13) predicts a net growth rate of 1.2 per second. The averaging assumes \(\epsilon \ll 1\); run on the page’s own integrator, the growth comes out at 1.21 per second, so it holds here computed. Before it grows, a micrometre of initial disorder first falls about tenfold: a seed at rest is all \(\cos\omega t\), the decaying part \(P\) for this phase of the drive, and the growing part \(S\) is fed only by the small terms the averaging drops. (A linear model gives no seed by itself: the page starts every mode at about a micrometre, standing for the disorder a real surface always has.)

The hand

Your pointer does not move the glass directly. The glass follows it like a mass on a critically damped spring of natural frequency \(\Omega_h\), \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g = \Omega_h^2(\mathbf x_\text{hand} - \mathbf x_g) - 2\Omega_h\dot{\mathbf x}_g\), so a sudden jerk of the pointer becomes a finite acceleration. The page sets \(\Omega_h = 22\) rad/s, close to the slosh’s own 20.4 rad/s: a motion of the pointer at the slosh frequency reaches the glass at \(\Omega_h^2/(\Omega_h^2 + \omega^2) = 54\) % of its size computed. Opinion: this is a cartoon of a human arm, not a model of one; its one honest content is that a stiff hand (large \(\Omega_h\)) transmits sharp accelerations and a soft hand filters them. Walking jolts the glass at every heel strike, a sharp and periodic shock rather than a smooth sine, so it contains the step frequency and its multiples. People walk at about 1 to 2.5 steps per second, and a coffee cup sloshes at 2.6 to 4.3 Hz [2]; the second multiple of a step falls right among the slosh frequencies of a pint.

07Where the energy goes

A sloshing pint stops within seconds; an ideal fluid would rock for ever. Each loss below is computed on its own and has its own switch on the page.

The energy method

The energy of a mode is its kinetic energy at the moment the surface passes level, \(E = \tfrac12\rho\int|\nabla\phi|^2\,dV\). If a process dissipates the average power \(\bar P\), the energy decays as \(e^{-2\gamma t}\) and the amplitude as \(e^{-\gamma t}\), with

\[ \gamma = \frac{\bar P}{2E} . \tag{14} \]

Each loss is then a matter of computing \(\bar P\) with the flow of the lossless mode. This is accurate as long as the losses are small, \(\zeta = \gamma/\omega \ll 1\); here \(\zeta\) is 2 to 4 %.

The boundary layers on wall and floor ★

Just outside the Stokes layer the flow slides along the glass with the velocity \(U\cos\omega t\) given by the potential. Inside, at distance \(s\) from the glass, the solution of the diffusion equation that sticks to the glass is \(u = U[\cos\omega t - e^{-s/\delta}\cos(\omega t - s/\delta)]\). The power per unit area turned into heat by the rubbing of neighbouring layers is \(\mu\int(\partial u/\partial s)^2 ds\). The square of the gradient averages over a period to \((U^2/\delta^2)\,e^{-2s/\delta}\), and the integral to \(U^2/(2\delta)\), so

\[ \bar P_w = \frac{\rho}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\int_\text{wall + floor}|\nabla_t\phi|^2\,dS , \tag{15} \]

with \(\nabla_t\phi\) the slip velocity amplitude along the glass. Dividing by \(2E\) and using Green’s identity \(\int|\nabla\Phi|^2dV = \lambda\int\psi^2 dS\) for the energy,

\[ \gamma_w = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\;\frac{I_w + I_b}{E}, \qquad I_w = \int_\text{wall}\left(\Phi_\ell^2 + \frac{m^2\Phi^2}{r^2}\right)r\,d\ell , \tag{16} \]

with \(\Phi_\ell\) the derivative along the wall’s profile and \(I_b\) the same integral over the floor. Here \(E\) stands for \(\iint|\nabla\Phi|^2\,r\,dr\,dz\), the energy (14) without the factors \(\tfrac12\rho\), the amplitude and the angular integral, which cancel in the ratio. The program evaluates \(I_w\), \(I_b\) and \(E\) for each mode of the tulip glass.

Check. For the deep cylinder and \(m = 1\), with \(\Phi = J_1(kr)e^{kz}\), the integrals are elementary: \(I_w/E = (1 + 1/\xi^2)/[R(1 - 1/\xi^2)] = 1.837/R\). The logarithmic decrement, the fall of \(\ln a\) per period, is \(\Delta = 2\pi\gamma/\omega\); with \(\omega = \sqrt{\xi g/R}\) this gives \(\Delta = \pi\cdot1.837\,\nu^{1/2}/(\sqrt2\,\xi^{1/4})\,R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\), that is

\[ \Delta_{11} = 3.50\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4} \quad\text{(clean walls, theory)}, \qquad \Delta_{11} = 4.98\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\quad\text{(experiments in tanks [6])} . \tag{17} \]

The measured coefficient is 1.42 times the clean-wall theory. That is no contradiction: the abstract of Miles’s paper on damping in closed basins reports that observed decay rates exceed wall damping alone by factors of two to three, and names surface films and the contact line as the causes [5]. Those are the next two terms.

The skin of the surface

Beer is full of proteins that gather at the surface, and under a head the stout touches the underside of a foam. Either way, the surface resists being stretched. If it cannot stretch at all, the surface is a wall for horizontal motion, and its own Stokes layer dissipates by the same formula (15), now integrated over the surface: \(\gamma_s = c_s\,\tfrac12\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,I_s/E\), with \(I_s\) the integral of (16) taken over the surface instead of the wall, \(c_s = 0\) for a clean surface and \(c_s = 1\) for one that cannot stretch. The page’s default is \(c_s = 1\); opinion: a stout under a head is closer to a skin than to a clean surface. With it, the skin takes about as much as the wall.

The interior ★

Even an irrotational flow dissipates, because its parcels are stretched. The power lost per volume is \(2\mu\sum_{ij}e_{ij}^2\), with the rate of strain \(e_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\phi\). Because \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\), its integral becomes a surface integral, \(\mu\oint\partial_n|\nabla\phi|^2dS\). For a deep mode, \(\phi \propto e^{\lambda z}\), so \(|\nabla\phi|^2\) grows toward the surface as \(e^{2\lambda z}\) and its normal derivative there is \(2\lambda|\nabla\phi|^2\). Dividing the period-averaged loss by \(2E\) gives

\[ \gamma_b = 2\nu\lambda^2 , \]

the damping of waves on deep open water. In a round glass the exact value for the slowest mode is \(1.88\nu k^2\), 6 % lower; on the total damping that is 0.1 %. For the slowest mode \(\gamma_b\) is 0.009 per second, 2 % of the total: negligible there, but it grows as \(\lambda^2\), so short waves feel it most computed.

The contact line

Where the surface meets the glass, the edge of the liquid has to creep up and down the wall. A wetting liquid loses somewhat more energy than viscous theory predicts, and non-wetting combinations lose several times more [7]; the loss depends on the amplitude, rising as the waves get smaller until the edge sticks [7]. No first-principles formula for a stout under a head exists. The page uses a constant rate \(\gamma_c\), default 0.05 per second, at the low end of the 0.05 to 0.25 per second measured in a 60 cm water tank [7]. Opinion: a rate measured in another tank, at another frequency, transfers only as an order of magnitude; and under a head the edge of the stout is hidden in foam, so the head term below may already contain part of this loss. Set \(\gamma_c = 0\) to remove any double count.

The head

Sauret and co-workers measured how a layer of foam damps sloshing in a narrow tank [1]. The foam adds a damping rate that grows with its thickness and saturates once the foam is about as thick as the depth the wave reaches, \(1/k\): they found that the bubbles move less the higher they sit above the liquid, fading over that depth. They traced the loss to the foam’s films sliding on the glass walls, so it scales as the wall area per volume of foam: as \(1/w\) for their tank of width \(w\), with \(w\gamma_f = 1.6 \pm 0.3\) cm/s measured [1]. For a round glass the wall per volume is \(2/R\) instead of \(2/w\), so we take

\[ \gamma_f = c_f\,\frac{u_f}{R}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda h_f}\right), \qquad u_f = 1.6\text{ cm/s} , \tag{18} \]

with the factor \(c_f\), default 1, on a slider. This fits with section 5 as follows. Inference: the head is lifted bodily by the surface beneath it (a foam resists being sheared like a soft solid, so its vertical motion is passed up through it), which is what loads the surface; but it slides against the wall only where the sideways flow beneath drags it, in a layer of depth about \(1/\lambda\), which is what dissipates. The transfer from a slot to a round glass, and from 3 mm bubbles in dish soap to the much finer bubbles of a stout’s head, is the least certain step on this page; it could be off by a factor of three either way.

Key result · how long a pint rocks

With its head, the default pint’s slowest slosh decays as \(e^{-\gamma t}\) with \(\gamma = 0.50\) s⁻¹: its amplitude falls to 1/e in 2.0 s and to 5 % in 6.0 s. Without the head the rate is 0.27 s⁻¹, and the same fall takes 3.7 s and 11.1 s. With the walls and the interior alone it would be 8.9 s to 1/e computed. Inference: the walls set the floor of the damping, and everything else in a real pint at least doubles it.

No measured decay time of sloshing in a beer glass was found in the sources searched; the walking-with-coffee study gives a computed, not measured, decrement for a cup [2]. A real test of this note is therefore within anyone’s reach: film a pint after a tap on the glass and count the swings.

08The velocity field in the cut

The figure under the glass is a vertical cut through it along the direction in which the stout rocks. The arrows are the velocity \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) of the stout relative to the glass.

Each mode contributes \((\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\nabla(\Phi_n\cos m\theta)\): the arrows are largest when the surface passes level and vanish at the extremes of the swing, when the stout is momentarily at rest and all its energy sits in the raised surface. In the cut, the component \(\partial_r\Phi\) points along the cut and \(\partial_z\Phi\) up; the component across the cut, \(-m\Phi\sin(m\theta)/r\), is drawn as a ring whose size is its speed. For pure rocking along the cut it is zero; rings appear when modes rock across the cut too, when the glass is pushed in a new direction, or when \(m \ge 2\) modes are excited.

The fading with depth follows from (7): \(\cosh k(z + D)/\cosh kD \approx e^{kz}\) when \(kD \gg 1\). Laplace’s equation trades variation along the surface for variation with depth: a pattern that changes over a distance \(R\) across the glass must also die out over a distance of order \(R/\xi\) downward. That is why a pint sloshes only in its top two or three centimetres, and why the shape of the lower glass hardly changes the frequency.

09On the screen, and what is left out

Everything the page draws comes from the equations above, solved in two stages: the modes once, offline, and their motion live, while you watch.

The numerical method

Offline, the finite-element program of section 5 solves (9) for 30 fills from 300 to 590 mL and keeps eight radial shapes: \(m = 0\) with \(n = 1, 2\); \(m = 1\) with \(n = 1, 2, 3\); \(m = 2\) with \(n = 1, 2\); and \(m = 3\) with \(n = 1\). Sideways motion drives only the \(m = 1\) modes, but vertical shaking pumps all of them (section 6), and a jolted surface shows the others; keeping them costs little. With the \(\sin\) copies of \(m \ge 1\) that makes 14 oscillators. For each it stores \(\lambda_n\), \(Q_n\), the damping integrals, the surface shape \(\psi_n\) at 25 radii, and, for six fills (360, 420, 480, 520, 560 and 590 mL), \(\Phi\), \(\partial_r\Phi\) and \(\partial_z\Phi\) on a 12 × 20 grid in the cut. The page interpolates between fills; for the straight glass it computes the same quantities from Bessel functions.

Live, the page integrates the hand’s follower and the 14 equations (12) with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method at a fixed step \(\Delta t = 1\) ms. We chose it because the vertical shaking makes the coefficients of (12) change in time, so no exact step formula applies, and because one method for the hand, the shaker and the modes keeps the code short. The fastest mode kept, (1,3) at about 6.4 Hz, has \(\omega\Delta t = 0.04\), far inside the method’s stability limit (\(|\omega\Delta t| \lt 2.8\)), with a phase error per step of order \((\omega\Delta t)^5/120\), about \(10^{-9}\). Measured: over 8 s of resonant shaking the page’s amplitudes differ from an independent high-accuracy integration by \(5\times10^{-8}\) of the largest amplitude computed. Each frame runs as many steps as real time has passed, but at most 50 ms worth: a slow computer shows the stout in slow motion rather than skipping ahead. If a wave ever exceeds twice the surface radius, far outside the model, the page calms the stout back to its micrometre seed and says so.

Equation to screen

The readout \(f_1\) is \(\omega_{11}/2\pi\) from (11); \(\zeta_1\) is \(\gamma_{11}/\omega_{11}\) of section 6; “Falls to 1/e in” is \(1/\gamma_{11}\); “Head top to rim, now” is the rim height minus the highest point of the head, \(Z_s + \eta_\text{max} + h_f\); the line under “Now” compares the highest wave at the wall, \(\eta_\text{max}\), with the radius. The trace plots \(\eta_\text{max}\) and the head’s top against the rim. In the cut, the key gives the arrow length of 50 mm/s.

Where the drawing differs from the model

The 3D view draws the glass from the measured profile and the stout’s surface from \(\eta(r, \theta)\) at 16 radii and 72 angles. The head is drawn as a layer of constant thickness on top of the surface, which is how the model treats its mass; a real head bends. The cut uses the velocity fields of the nearest of the six stored fills: its grid points are placed on the present fill’s depth and wall, but the velocities are those of the stored fill. Arrows longer than 34 px are cut short and drawn pink. While the arrows are shown, the stout is drawn see-through, which real stout is not.

Limits of the model

The model is linear: it holds while the wave at the wall is small compared with the radius. The page’s warning at a fifth of the radius is our choice of a safe bound, not a measured threshold. A strongly driven slosh in a round glass can also turn into a swirl, the surface rotating around the glass, an effect of the nonlinear terms that this page leaves out. The glass is assumed to translate without turning; a real hand also tilts the glass, which adds forces that are not the gradient of a potential (section 3) and is left out. The contact line is treated as a constant damping and the head as a lifted mass with wall friction. The next model would keep the quadratic terms of the surface conditions (giving swirl and the bending of the resonance curve), let the glass tilt, give the head an elasticity of its own, and measure its damping in a real glass.
