Fig. 12 · lecture note · fluid dynamics and control

A pint of stout on a suspension

Numa Koudsie · for readers who know Newton’s laws, derivatives and the idea of a normal mode; the parts marked ★ use vector calculus, Bessel functions and matrices, and their results are restated without them

Abstract. A pint carried across a pub, or driven over a speed bump on a trolley, does not simply ride along: the stout rocks, climbs the glass on one side, and the head goes over the rim. We build the physics of that rocking from Newton’s second law for a fluid. In the frame of the glass the stout feels gravity plus the glass’s acceleration; inside it the flow has no rotation, so its velocity is the gradient of a potential; at the surface, gravity pulls the stout back to level. The result is a set of sloshing modes, each an oscillator driven by the glass’s sideways acceleration and by its tilt, and pumped by its vertical acceleration. For a straight glass the modes are Bessel functions; for the real Guinness glass, drawn from its engineering elevation and sculpted on two sides like the real thing, we compute them by finite elements in three dimensions and check the program against the straight glass. We follow the energy into the boundary layers, the skin of the surface and the friction of the head, which is the largest loss. Then the glass is put on a cart with two sprung wheels and driven over a bump: on springs and dampers alone the body pitches at nearly the stout’s own frequency, and the pint is lost. Two moving-coil actuators under a controller designed from the whole model, cart and stout together, keep the wave at the rim to a few millimetres. Every number on the page is computed, and the ones that are guesses are marked as such.

01Carrying a pint

Pick up a full glass and walk. For a few steps nothing happens; then the surface starts to rock, a little higher on each step, until the stout climbs the side of the glass and the head spills over your fingers. Stop walking and the rocking does not stop at once: it dies away over a few seconds, and the dark liquid settles back under its cream-coloured head.

Three things are already visible in that experience. First, the stout has a rhythm of its own: it rocks at a definite frequency, about three times a second in a pint glass, whatever your hand does. Second, your hand can feed that rhythm: if your steps come at a rate close to it, each step adds to the swing, and the amplitude grows. Third, something takes the energy away again, because the rocking dies when you stand still. A physicist recognises a driven, damped oscillator. This note shows that the stout in a glass is a whole family of such oscillators, called sloshing modes, derives their frequencies from the laws of fluid motion and the shape of the glass, and works out, one by one, the processes that damp them.

We use the glass Guinness serves its draught stout in, the 20-imperial-ounce “gravity” glass of 2010 (568 mL), and the stout itself at its serving temperature. The glass stands on a cart: a body on two sprung wheels, the simplest picture of a car or a trolley, which drives over a bump, a kerb or cobbles while you watch the stout. In sections 10 and 11 the cart gets two actuators and a controller, and the question becomes the one every waiter answers with their wrists: how do you keep the pint level when the ground does not stay still?

02The glass and the stout

Before any equation we need two things: the shape of the container, and the properties of what is in it.

The shape, from a drawing

Guinness publishes no drawing of its glass; retail listings give an overall size, 160 mm tall and 85 mm across at the widest, holding 568 mL, one imperial pint [8]. A dimensioned elevation of the same tulip shape exists, however, drawn for the 473 mL version sold in America: 149 mm tall and 76 mm across [9]. Its outline is a vector drawing, so the half-width of the glass can be read off at every height with no pixel noise. We write \(Z\) for the height above the foot of the glass. Scaling the drawing to the imperial glass takes two slightly different factors, 160/149 = 1.074 in height and 85/81.5 = 1.043 in width, so our glass is the drawing stretched by 3 % more in height than in width: a modelling choice, stated here once.

The outline alone gives the outside. The inside needs two more numbers. The floor of the cavity sits about 8 mm above the foot, as the thick base of the real glass shows; the wall we take 2.0 mm thick, a typical value for a toughened pint glass, measured normal to the wall. The body of revolution this gives has its waist 61.5 mm across at \(Z = 40\) mm, its widest point 85.0 mm at \(Z = 124.5\) mm, and a rim of 80.7 mm; it holds 576 mL to the brim computed. Inference: that is the right size. British pint glasses are brim measure: the 568 mL fill the glass to its rim, and the head stands above it as a dome. Our cavity takes the pint to 158.2 mm, 1.8 mm below the rim, with the 2.0 mm wall as the only free number: a consistency check the earlier photograph-based profile of this page failed by 6 %.

The sculpted panels

The glass is not quite a body of revolution. When Diageo redesigned it in 2010, the lower body was “embossed with curves inspired by the harp logo” [13]; in photographs of the clear glass [14] a crease sweeps from the centre of the foot up to each side at about half height, a V on the front and another on the back, and the wall inside each V is set back a little. So two opposite sides are deeper than the other two, and the glass keeps a two-fold symmetry: turn it by 180° about its axis and it looks the same, but a quarter turn changes it. We model the recess as a smooth panel of depth \(A = 2.0\) mm inside the V, blending into the round body between 60 and 100 mm up; the V’s bottom sits 16 mm above the foot and its arms reach the sides at 80 mm. Opinion: the depth is read from the photographs’ shading, not measured; it could be half or double. At 50 mm up the cavity is 27.0 mm in radius at the front and 29.0 mm at the sides computed. The panels remove 14 mL from the round cavity’s 590 mL, which is how the brim volume comes to 576 mL.

03A fluid in a moving glass

On a train that brakes, your coffee leans forward as if gravity had tipped. That is the first idea: in the frame of the glass, the glass’s acceleration acts like an extra gravity pointing the other way.

Put coordinates on the glass: \(x\) and \(y\) horizontal, \(z\) vertical, all fixed to the glass and moving with it. Let the glass move without turning for now (it turns in section 6), its own position being \((x_g, y_g, z_g)\) in the room, and write its acceleration \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g = (\ddot x_g, \ddot y_g, \ddot z_g)\); a dot means a time derivative. Take a small parcel of stout of density \(\rho\) and velocity \(\mathbf u\) relative to the glass. In the room its acceleration is that of the glass plus its own, \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g + D\mathbf u/Dt\), where \(D\mathbf u/Dt = \partial\mathbf u/\partial t + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\) is the acceleration of the parcel as it moves along, not of the point it happens to pass. Newton’s second law per unit volume, with the forces a fluid can exert, is the Navier–Stokes equation; moving the frame’s acceleration to the right-hand side,

\[ \rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \rho\left(\mathbf g - \ddot{\mathbf x}_g\right). \tag{1} \]

Each term is a force per unit volume. On the left is mass times acceleration. \(-\nabla p\) is the push of the pressure \(p\): a parcel is pushed from where the pressure is high to where it is low. \(\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) is the rubbing of neighbouring layers that slide past each other, with \(\mu\) the viscosity. \(\rho\mathbf g\), with \(\mathbf g = (0, 0, -g)\), is the weight, and \(-\rho\ddot{\mathbf x}_g\) is the inertial force of the accelerating frame, the push your coffee feels on the braking train. (Notation: \(\partial/\partial t\) is the rate of change at a fixed point, holding the other variables still; \(\nabla\), “nabla”, collects the rates of change in the three directions, \(\nabla p = (\partial_xp, \partial_yp, \partial_zp)\); \(\nabla^2 = \partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2\) is the Laplacian, the operator that compares a quantity with the average over its neighbours.)

The two body forces combine into the gradient of one potential,

\[ \mathbf g - \ddot{\mathbf x}_g = -\nabla\Psi, \qquad \Psi = (g + \ddot z_g)\,z + \ddot x_g\,x + \ddot y_g\,y , \tag{2} \]

as differentiating \(\Psi\) shows. \(\Psi\) is a potential energy per kilogram, in J/kg = m²/s². So the stout feels an effective gravity \(\mathbf g_\text{eff} = -\nabla\Psi\): tilted when the glass speeds up or slows down sideways, stronger when the glass accelerates upward (starts a lift, or hits the far side of a dip), weaker when it accelerates downward. A glass carried at constant velocity, up, down or sideways, changes nothing. If \(g + \ddot z_g\) turns negative, the glass is accelerated downward faster than a falling stone and the stout leaves the floor; the page warns when that happens.

Why the interior has no rotation ★

Divide (1) by \(\rho\) and take its curl. The curl of any gradient is zero, so both \(\nabla p\) and \(\nabla\Psi\) drop out. With the vorticity \(\mathbf w = \nabla\times\mathbf u\), the identity \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = \nabla(\tfrac12 u^2) - \mathbf u\times\mathbf w\), and the fact that the curl and the Laplacian commute, we are left with

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf w}{\partial t} = \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf w) + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf w , \tag{3} \]

with \(\nu = \mu/\rho\). Every term on the right contains \(\mathbf w\). A settled pint, \(\mathbf w = 0\), therefore stays free of vorticity however the glass is shaken, except where something outside (3) creates it. (A freshly poured Guinness is not settled: its sinking bubbles drive a circulation [3], [4], which we wait out.) What creates vorticity is the wall: the stout must stick to the glass, while a flow without vorticity slides along it. The conflict is settled in a thin Stokes layer. In Stokes’s exact solution for a fluid beside a wall oscillating at angular frequency \(\omega\), the disturbance falls by a factor \(e\) over the distance

\[ \delta = \sqrt{2\nu/\omega} , \tag{4} \]

the distance vorticity diffuses in a time \(1/\omega\). For the slowest slosh of our pint, \(\omega = 20.3\) rad/s, this is \(\delta = 0.45\) mm, about one percent of the glass’s radius computed.

The flow outside the layers

Key result · irrotational interior

Outside boundary layers about 0.45 mm thick, the stout does not rotate, and its velocity relative to the glass is the gradient of a velocity potential \(\phi\), \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\): the flow runs uphill on the landscape \(\phi\), steepest where the landscape is steepest. Incompressibility then reads \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\), Laplace’s equation. Viscosity drops out of the interior entirely, because for \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) the viscous force is \(\mu\nabla(\nabla^2\phi) = 0\); it acts only in the layers, where it takes energy away (section 7).

With \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), every term of (1) is a gradient (the convective term becomes \(\nabla(\tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2)\)), so (1) can be integrated once in space:

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2 + \frac{p}{\rho} + \Psi = C(t), \tag{5} \]

the unsteady Bernoulli equation. The function \(C(t)\) can be absorbed into \(\phi\): only the gradient of \(\phi\) is a velocity, so adding a function of time alone to \(\phi\) changes nothing physical.

The model in one place

The stout is incompressible and settled; the interior is irrotational; the surface stays close to level (linear theory); the head is a layer of mass that rides on the surface and rubs on the wall; the edge of the stout on the glass adds a constant damping rate; the glass translates with the cart and tilts with it, by small angles. The numbers that decide what matters, for the default pint: the depth times the slowest wave’s wavenumber, \(\lambda D = 6.4\) (deep; \(\lambda\), defined in section 4, is the inverse of the depth over which a wave fades), boundary layer over radius \(\delta/R = 0.011\) (thin), surface tension over gravity \(\sigma\lambda^2/\rho g = 0.010\) (small), damping ratio \(\zeta = 0.024\) (light), the head’s added mass over the sloshing mass \(\kappa = 0.13\) (not small), and the cart’s pitch frequency over the slosh frequency, 1.05 (nearly resonant, section 10) computed.

04Linear sloshing in a straight glass

Laplace’s equation alone has no waves in it. The waves come from the free surface, where gravity pulls a raised patch of stout back down, and the stout below must move to let it.

Boundary conditions

From here on \(z\) is measured upward from the calm surface, so the liquid fills \(-D \le z \le 0\), with \(D\) its depth; heights above the foot keep the letter \(Z\), and the calm surface sits at \(Z_s\). Let the stout surface be raised by \(\eta(x, y, t)\) above its calm level. Three conditions close the problem.

On the wall and the floor the stout cannot pass through the glass: \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\phi = 0\), with \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) the unit vector normal to the glass.

At the surface, kinematics: a parcel on the surface stays on the surface, so the surface rises at the vertical speed of the stout there: \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi\) to first order in the small amplitude.

At the surface, dynamics: the pressure just under the surface equals the air pressure (we come back to surface tension and the head in section 5). Putting \(p = p_\text{air}\) and \(z = \eta\) in (5), dropping the square of the small velocity, and moving everything that depends on time alone into \(\phi\),

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + (g + \ddot z_g)\,\eta + \ddot x_g\,x + \ddot y_g\,y = 0 \quad\text{at } z = 0 . \tag{6} \]

Linearisation means evaluating both surface conditions at the calm level \(z = 0\) rather than at \(z = \eta\). The error is of the order of the wave slope, the height of the wave divided by its horizontal scale; the page warns when the wave at the rim exceeds a fifth of the radius.

Check: a steady push tilts the surface. If the glass accelerates sideways at a constant \(\ddot x_g\), the stout comes to rest relative to the glass, \(\phi\) is constant in time, and (6) gives \(\eta = -(\ddot x_g/g)\,x\): a plane surface, low on the side the glass accelerates toward, tilted at \(\tan\alpha = \ddot x_g/g\). That is the coffee on the braking train.

The modes of a cylinder

First let the glass stand still. Eliminating \(\eta\) between the two surface conditions (differentiate (6) in time and put \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi\)) gives one condition on \(\phi\) alone, \(\partial_t^2\phi + g\,\partial_z\phi = 0\) at \(z = 0\). In a cylinder of radius \(R\), with polar coordinates \(r, \theta\), the solution of Laplace’s equation that satisfies it and the wall and floor conditions is a standing wave

\[ \phi = \phi_0\,J_m(kr)\cos(m\theta)\,\frac{\cosh k(z + D)}{\cosh kD}\,\cos\omega t . \tag{7} \]

Here \(J_m\) is the Bessel function of order \(m\), which behaves like a cosine whose swings weaken as \(r\) grows; it plays the part the sine plays on a guitar string, adapted to a round boundary. The factor \(\cosh k(z + D)\) makes the motion strongest at the surface and gives zero vertical velocity at the floor. No flow through the side wall requires \(J_m'(kR) = 0\), so \(kR\) must be one of the zeros \(\xi_{mn}\) of \(J_m'\): \(\xi_{11} = 1.8412\), \(\xi_{21} = 3.0542\), \(\xi_{01} = 3.8317\), \(\xi_{12} = 5.3314\) (computed with Wolfram Language and with SciPy, which agree to all digits). Putting (7) into the surface condition, where \(\partial_z\) of the cosh factor gives \(k\tanh kD\), fixes the frequency:

\[ \omega_{mn}^2 = g\,\lambda_{mn}, \qquad \lambda_{mn} = k_{mn}\tanh(k_{mn}D), \qquad k_{mn} = \xi_{mn}/R . \tag{8} \]

Where (7) comes from ★

In polar coordinates Laplace’s equation reads \(\partial_r^2\phi + r^{-1}\partial_r\phi + r^{-2}\partial_\theta^2\phi + \partial_z^2\phi = 0\). Try \(\phi = \Phi(r)\cos(m\theta)G(z)\) and divide by \(\phi\): the terms in \(z\) alone and in \(r\) alone must each be constant, say \(G''/G = k^2\) and \(\Phi'' + \Phi'/r + (k^2 - m^2/r^2)\Phi = 0\). The second is Bessel’s equation; of its two solutions only \(J_m(kr)\) stays finite on the axis (the other, \(Y_m\), is infinite there). \(G'' = k^2G\) with \(G'(-D) = 0\) gives the cosh factor. The time factor \(\cos\omega t\) follows from the surface condition.

The number \(m\) counts the nodal diameters, lines across the surface that stay level: \(m = 1\) is the familiar rocking, with one side up while the other is down; \(m = 2\) has two nodal diameters; \(m = 0\) is a ring wave that heaves the middle against the edge. The number \(n\) orders the modes of one \(m\) by frequency; mode \((m, n)\) has \(n - 1\) nodal circles when \(m \ge 1\), and \(n\) when \(m = 0\). Every mode with \(m \ge 1\) comes twice, as a \(\cos m\theta\) and a \(\sin m\theta\) copy, rocking along \(x\) or along \(y\); in a round glass the two have the same frequency. Shove the cart along the road and the 3D view shows the \(\cos\) copy of mode (1,1); shove it sideways and the \(\sin\) copy appears.

Example 4.1 · a straight half-litre glass

A cylinder of radius \(R = 40\) mm filled to \(D = 99.5\) mm holds \(\pi R^2D = 0.500\) L. Then \(k_{11} = 46.03\) m⁻¹ and \(\tanh(k_{11}D) = 0.9998\): the glass is “deep”, since \(\tanh\) has saturated. So \(\omega_{11} = \sqrt{g\,k_{11}\tanh(k_{11}D)} = \sqrt{9.81\times46.02} = 21.25\) rad/s and \(f_{11} = \omega_{11}/2\pi = 3.382\) Hz, confirmed with Wolfram Language. Once \(kD \gt 3\), \(\tanh kD \gt 0.995\) and the depth changes \(\omega\) by less than 0.3 %; in our pint \(\lambda D = 6.4\) computed.

Key result · deep sloshing

In a deep glass the slowest mode rocks at \(\omega_{11} \approx \sqrt{1.84\,g/R}\), set only by the radius of the surface: like a pendulum of length \(R/1.84\), about 21 mm for a pint. A wider glass sloshes more slowly.

05The real glass, in three dimensions

The Guinness glass is not a cylinder: it narrows to a waist, swells, leans inward again near the rim, and carries its two sculpted panels. Separation of variables fails twice over, but the problem keeps its structure, and a computer can solve it on the real shape.

For a body of revolution each mode would still vary as \(\cos m\theta\) around the axis, and a two-dimensional computation in a vertical cut would do. The panels break that: a mode of the real glass is a function \(\Phi(x, y, z)\) of all three coordinates, and nothing but its symmetry under a half turn survives. So we solve the problem in three dimensions. The modes still oscillate as \(\cos\omega t\); what the computer finds, for each fill, are the numbers \(\lambda = \omega^2/g\) and the shapes \(\Phi\) of the modes kept. The method is the next part, and can be skipped.

The eigenvalue problem ★

In the cavity \(\Omega\) below the calm surface \(S\),

\[ \nabla^2\Phi = 0 \ \text{in } \Omega, \qquad \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\Phi = 0 \ \text{on the glass}, \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} = \lambda\Phi \ \text{on } S , \tag{9} \]

where the last condition is \(\partial_t^2\phi + g\partial_z\phi = 0\) of section 4 with \(\phi \propto \cos\omega t\). The eigenvalue appears only in a boundary condition; mathematicians call this a Steklov problem. Multiply the equation by any smooth function \(v\), integrate over the cavity and integrate by parts once. The wall and floor terms vanish because \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\Phi = 0\), and the surface term is replaced using its condition:

\[ \int_\Omega\nabla\Phi\cdot\nabla v\,dV = \lambda\int_S\Phi\,v\,dS . \tag{10} \]

This weak form is what the finite-element method solves. The cavity is divided into curved bricks, mapped from a grid in (radius fraction, angle, height) so that the outer bricks follow the real wall, panels included; on each brick \(\Phi\) is a product of three quadratics, fixed by its values at 27 points. Requiring (10) for every such function \(v\) gives a matrix equation \(\mathsf K\boldsymbol\Phi = \lambda\,\mathsf M\boldsymbol\Phi\), in which \(\mathsf M\) has entries only at the surface points. Eliminating the interior points leaves a dense symmetric eigenvalue problem on the surface, about 900 unknowns for 33 000 points in all computed. Each mode’s shape \(\psi(s, \theta) = \Phi|_S\) is then written as a short Fourier series in the angle, \(\psi = \sum_k[c_k(s)\cos k\theta + s_k(s)\sin k\theta]\), which is exact for a body of revolution (one term) and keeps three terms, \(k = m, m \pm 2\), for the sculpted glass: the panels can only mix an angular pattern with the ones two steps away, by their two-fold symmetry. We scale every mode so that its largest value on the rim is 1: the amplitude of a mode is then the wave height at the rim where that mode is largest, in metres.

Checking the program

A program that has not reproduced a known answer has proved nothing. For a cylinder of 40 mm radius and 100 mm depth the exact eigenvalues are \(\lambda = k\tanh kD\) from (8). On the mesh the page uses (8 bricks in radius, 28 around, 18 up), the program gives \(\lambda_{11}\) within \(6\times10^{-6}\) of the exact value, and every mode the page keeps within \(1.2\times10^{-4}\); the coupling \(Q_{11}\) of section 6 within \(2\times10^{-6}\) of its closed form, and the wall integral of section 7 within \(5\times10^{-6}\) of the exact Bessel integral computed. On the glass with its panels switched off, the three-dimensional program and the earlier two-dimensional one for bodies of revolution agree on every kept mode within \(1.6\times10^{-4}\), two programs with different meshes and different element shapes computed. What the shape does

Run on the glass for every fill from 300 to 576 mL, the program gives the frequencies of Figure 2. Below the widest point the wall leans outward as it rises; above it, the wall leans inward. With 18 mm of head on both, the outward-leaning glass sloshes more slowly than a straight glass of the same surface radius and depth (3.06 against 3.22 Hz at 300 mL), and the inward-leaning one faster (3.32 against 3.27 Hz at 576 mL) computed. Inference: near the surface, where the sloshing flow lives, an outward-leaning wall gives the stout more room to swing sideways for the same rise at the wall, which acts like a wider glass; an inward-leaning wall does the opposite.

And the panels? At the default fill they do nothing measurable: the \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) copies of mode (1,1), rocking across the panels and along them, differ in frequency by less than 0.01 %, and each mode’s rim pattern is a pure \(\cos m\theta\) or \(\sin m\theta\) to four digits computed. At 300 mL, with the surface 105 mm up and the panels’ fade just below it, the split reaches 0.1 %. Inference: this is section 8 at work before we reach it: a slosh lives in the top two centimetres of the stout, and the panels end ten centimetres up. The sculpting is real, it is in the glass the page draws and in the cavity the program meshes, and it is also, for the frequencies, irrelevant: a fact worth more than a guess either way.

The head and the surface tension

Two corrections change the surface condition (6). Surface tension makes the surface behave like a stretched drum skin: a curved surface pulls back toward flat with an extra pressure \(-\sigma\nabla_h^2\eta\), where \(\nabla_h^2\) is the Laplacian along the surface. For a mode it multiplies \(\eta\) by \(\lambda^2\) (exactly so in a deep straight glass, approximately in the real one), which raises \(\omega^2\) by the fraction \(\sigma\lambda^2/(\rho g)\): 1.0 % for our pint computed. We let the edge of the surface slide freely on the glass. The head is heavier. Treat it as a layer of mass \(M_f\) per area that is lifted and lowered with the surface beneath it: it adds the inertia \(M_f\partial_t^2\eta\) to the pressure balance at the surface, and nothing to the restoring force, because a foam weighs the same wherever the surface takes it. For a mode this adds \(M_f\lambda\) to the inertia \(\rho\) of the sloshing layer, and

\[ \omega_n^2 = \frac{g\lambda_n + \sigma\lambda_n^3/\rho}{1 + \kappa_n}, \qquad \kappa_n = \frac{M_f\lambda_n}{\rho} . \tag{11} \]

The added-mass ratio \(\kappa_n\) compares the head’s mass with the mass of stout that sloshes, a layer of depth \(1/\lambda\). For the default pint, \(\kappa_{11} = 0.127\), and the head lowers \(f_1\) from 3.432 to 3.233 Hz computed. Opinion: this is the crudest piece of the model. A real head also has a little stiffness of its own, and its liquid fraction is not measured; the slider for \(\varepsilon_f\) lets you see how much it matters.

Example 5.1 · the default pint

500 mL of stout fills the glass to \(Z_s = 144.1\) mm above the foot, where the surface has radius \(R = 39.8\) mm and the stout is \(D = 136.1\) mm deep. The program gives \(\lambda_{11} = 46.94\) m⁻¹, so the bare frequency is \(\sqrt{9.81\times46.94}/2\pi = 3.415\) Hz; surface tension raises it to 3.432 Hz, and the head lowers it to 3.233 Hz computed.

06Driving the modes: push, tilt, pump

Now let the glass move with the cart: it is pushed along, it tilts when the body pitches, and it is lifted and dropped by the road. The motion enters only through the surface condition, and there it does three different things.

The glass turns ★

Section 3 took the glass to translate without turning. On the cart it also tilts, by the body’s pitch angle \(\psi\), small and positive when the front goes down. Turning changes the problem in a way the frame-of-the-glass argument does not handle gracefully, because a rotating frame adds forces that are not the gradient of a potential. So we work in the room instead, where the stout’s flow is irrotational and has a potential \(\Phi\) whether or not the glass turns, and let the boundary move. Fix coordinates \((x', y', z')\) to the glass with their origin at \(O\), the centre of its foot, which the cart carries to \((x_O, z_O)\) in the room. A point \(z'\) up the glass’s axis sits at \(x_O + \psi z'\) in the room when the glass is tilted by \(\psi\): the tilt sweeps the top of the glass sideways.

Split the potential into the part the glass would impose on a frozen liquid and the sloshing part: \(\Phi = \dot x_O\,x' + \dot z_O\,z' + \dot\psi\,\chi(x', y', z') + \phi_s\). The first two terms are the translation. The third is the flow of a liquid that turns with its container, and \(\chi\) is fixed by Laplace’s equation inside the cavity with the condition that the liquid moves with the glass at every boundary, \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\chi = \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf y}\times\mathbf r')\) on the wall, the floor, and the mean surface too, as if a lid were turning with the glass: the Stokes–Joukowski potential of the cavity, which the finite-element program of section 5 computes alongside the modes. Why a lid: because then the sloshing part \(\phi_s\) alone has to carry the free surface, with \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\phi_s = 0\) on wall and floor, exactly the conditions the modes were built for. A liquid that is swung round does not simply turn with its glass; near the surface it lags, and \(\chi\) says by how much.

Two things change in the surface conditions. The surface’s height in the room is \(z_O + Z_s + \eta - \psi x'\), so its vertical velocity has the extra term \(-\dot\psi x'\); but \(\partial_z\Phi\) on the surface contains \(\dot\psi\,\partial_z\chi = -\dot\psi x'\) from the lid condition, and the two cancel: the kinematic condition keeps its old form, \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi_s\). In the pressure condition (5), the weight’s potential \(gZ\) is evaluated on the tilted surface, which gives \(-g\psi x'\), and \(\partial_t\Phi\) brings \(\ddot x_O x'\), \(\ddot z_O\eta\) and \(\ddot\psi\chi\). The dynamic condition becomes

\[ \frac{\partial\phi_s}{\partial t} + (g + \ddot z_O)\,\eta = -\left(\ddot x_O - g\psi\right)x' - \ddot\psi\,\chi \quad\text{on } S , \]

which is (6) with two new terms: the tilt \(g\psi\), which is a sideways gravity in the glass’s own frame, and the turn \(\ddot\psi\chi\), the liquid’s inertia against being swung. The products of small quantities that the frame’s own velocity would add are second order and dropped, as everywhere in this note.

One equation per mode

Write the surface displacement as a sum over the modes of section 5, \(\eta = \sum_n a_n(t)\,\psi_n\), with \(\psi_n\) scaled to 1 at its largest point on the rim, so that \(a_n\) is a wave height in metres. The potential is \(\phi_s = \sum_n(\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\Phi_n\), because the kinematic condition requires \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi_s = \lambda_n\phi_s\) at the surface for each mode. Put both sums into the dynamic condition, multiply by \(\psi_j\) and integrate over the surface. The modes are orthogonal there (a consequence of the symmetry of (10)), so one equation is left for each mode; with the head and the surface tension of (11) and the damping rate \(\gamma_n\) of section 7,

\[ \ddot a_n + 2\gamma_n\dot a_n + \omega_n^2\left(1 + \frac{\ddot z_O}{g}\right)a_n = -\frac{\lambda_n}{1 + \kappa_n}\left[\left(\ddot x_O - g\psi\right)Q_n + \ddot\psi\,Q_{rn}\right], \qquad Q_n = \frac{\int_S\psi_n\,x\,dS}{\int_S\psi_n^2\,dS}, \quad Q_{rn} = \frac{\int_S\psi_n\,\chi\,dS}{\int_S\psi_n^2\,dS} . \tag{12} \]

(For clarity the \(\sigma\) term of (11) is not repeated; the page keeps it, and the vertical acceleration does not scale it: \(\ddot z_O\) changes gravity, not surface tension.) The modes that rock along \(y\) obey the same equation with \(\ddot y_O\) and the roll of the glass in place of \(\ddot x_O\) and the pitch; the cart does not roll, so they are driven by a sideways push alone. The minus sign is the coffee on the train: accelerate the glass toward \(+x\) and the stout rises on the \(-x\) side.

Key result · three ways in

A sideways acceleration drives. The term \(\ddot x_O x\) is a tilt of the effective gravity; it pushes on every mode whose shape leans sideways, which means \(m = 1\), through the coupling \(Q_n\). A tilt drives in the same way. A glass tilted by \(\psi\) feels gravity pointing sideways by \(g\psi\) in its own frame: one degree of tilt is 0.17 m/s², the same as a modest push, and it drives the same modes. A turn also drives through \(Q_{rn}\), the inertia of the liquid against being swung about the foot. A vertical acceleration pumps. \(\ddot z_O\) multiplies \(a_n\): it changes the strength of gravity, the spring constant of every mode, without pushing any of them. It can only amplify a slosh that is already there.

Checks. For a straight glass the coupling has a closed form: with \(\psi = J_1(kr)\cos\theta/J_1(\xi)\) and the Bessel integrals \(\int_0^R r^2J_1(kr)\,dr = R^2J_2(\xi)/k\) and \(\int_0^R rJ_1^2\,dr = \tfrac{R^2}{2}(1 - 1/\xi^2)J_1^2(\xi)\), using \(J_2(\xi) = J_1(\xi)/\xi\) at a zero of \(J_1'\), one finds \(Q = 2R/(\xi^2 - 1)\). For \(R = 40\) mm this is 33.473 mm, and the three-dimensional program reproduces it to six digits computed. A steady push must give the tilted plane of section 4: with \(\ddot a = \dot a = 0\), (12) gives \(a_n = -Q_n\ddot x_O/g\) (head aside), and the modes must add up to \(\sum_n Q_n\psi_n = x\). The two \(m = 1\) modes along \(x\) that the page keeps reproduce 90 % of the tilt at the rim; the rest lies in faster modes it leaves out computed. And a check on the turn: if the liquid were frozen, a point on the surface at height \(Z_s\) above the foot would be swung sideways by \(\ddot\psi Z_s\), so \(Q_{rn}\) would equal \(Q_nZ_s\). For the slowest mode of the default pint \(Q_{r}/Q = 103\) mm against \(Z_s = 144\) mm computed: the stout is swung only 71 % as hard as a frozen block, because the liquid near the surface lags the turning glass.

Resonance and the damping ratio

If the glass were shaken sideways as \(x_O = A\sin\Omega t\) at a mode’s own frequency, at \(\Omega = \omega_n\) the spring and inertia terms of (12) cancel, and only the damping is left to balance the push: \(2\gamma_n\omega_n a_n = \lambda_nQ_nA\omega_n^2/(1 + \kappa_n)\). The damping ratio \(\zeta_n = \gamma_n/\omega_n\), shown on the page as \(\zeta_1\), is the fraction of its amplitude a mode loses per radian of swing (its energy loses twice that fraction). Shake the default pint by \(A = 0.2\) mm at \(f_1 = 3.23\) Hz, an acceleration of only \(A\omega^2 = 0.083\) m/s², and the steady wave at the rim is 5.6 mm, 28 times the shaking computed. On the cart the road does the shaking: cobbles 0.10 m apart at a speed \(v\) hit the wheels \(v/0.1\) m times a second, 15 Hz at walking pace, well above the slosh, but the body’s pitch, at 3.4 Hz, sits almost on it (section 10).

Pumping: Faraday’s waves ★

Shake the glass up and down, \(z_O = A\sin\Omega t\), and (12) for one mode becomes \(\ddot a + 2\gamma\dot a + \omega^2(1 - \epsilon\sin\Omega t)\,a = 0\) with \(\epsilon = A\Omega^2/g\): a pendulum whose gravity is modulated, the Mathieu equation. Its strongest instability lies at \(\Omega = 2\omega\), twice the mode’s frequency. To see it, write \(a = b_1(t)\cos\omega t + b_2(t)\sin\omega t\) with slowly varying \(b_1, b_2\) and the usual condition \(\dot b_1\cos\omega t + \dot b_2\sin\omega t = 0\). The equation then gives \(\dot b_1 = -f\sin\omega t/\omega\) and \(\dot b_2 = f\cos\omega t/\omega\), with \(f = -2\gamma\dot a + \epsilon\omega^2\sin(2\omega t)\,a\). Averaged over one period, using \(\langle\sin^2\omega t\cos^2\omega t\rangle = \tfrac18\),

\[ \dot b_1 = -\left(\gamma + \frac{\epsilon\omega}{4}\right)b_1, \qquad \dot b_2 = \left(\frac{\epsilon\omega}{4} - \gamma\right)b_2 . \]

The \(\sin\) part grows when the gain beats the damping:

\[ \frac{\epsilon\,\omega}{4} \gt \gamma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A \gt \frac{4\zeta g}{\Omega^2} . \tag{13} \]

These standing waves at half the drive frequency are called Faraday waves. For the default pint the threshold is \(A = 0.58\) mm at \(\Omega = 2\omega_1\) computed. On the cart the vertical shaking comes from the road through the suspension: the pumping is there in the equations, with \(\ddot z_O\) taken from the glass’s motion, but a cart that shakes its pint hard enough to pump it also drives the slosh directly through the tilt, and the direct drive wins. (A linear model gives no seed by itself: the page starts every mode at about a micrometre, standing for the disorder a real surface always has. And when \(g + \ddot z_O\) would fall below \(0.2g\), the page holds it there: the stout would be leaving the floor, and nothing linear describes that.)

The push

Your pointer does not move the cart directly. Dragging the body applies a force along the chosen axis, proportional to the drag and capped at 80 N, as if you leaned on it; the buttons give a shove of 40 N for 0.3 s along the road or sideways, or press down on the body with 120 N. A push along the road accelerates the whole cart, 28.9 kg of it, and the stout feels \(\ddot x_O\); a press on the body compresses the springs, and when you let go the body bobs and pitches, which tilts the glass. Which of the three ways in each push uses is something the cut and the trace let you see.

07Where the energy goes

A sloshing pint stops within seconds; an ideal fluid would rock for ever. Each loss below is computed on its own and has its own switch on the page.

The energy method

The energy of a mode is its kinetic energy at the moment the surface passes level, \(E = \tfrac12\rho\int|\nabla\phi|^2\,dV\). If a process dissipates the average power \(\bar P\), the energy decays as \(e^{-2\gamma t}\) and the amplitude as \(e^{-\gamma t}\), with

\[ \gamma = \frac{\bar P}{2E} . \tag{14} \]

Each loss is then a matter of computing \(\bar P\) with the flow of the lossless mode. This is accurate as long as the losses are small, \(\zeta = \gamma/\omega \ll 1\); here \(\zeta\) is 2 to 4 %.

The boundary layers on wall and floor ★

Just outside the Stokes layer the flow slides along the glass with the velocity \(U\cos\omega t\) given by the potential. Inside, at distance \(s\) from the glass, the solution of the diffusion equation that sticks to the glass is \(u = U[\cos\omega t - e^{-s/\delta}\cos(\omega t - s/\delta)]\). The power per unit area turned into heat by the rubbing of neighbouring layers is \(\mu\int(\partial u/\partial s)^2 ds\). The square of the gradient averages over a period to \((U^2/\delta^2)\,e^{-2s/\delta}\), and the integral to \(U^2/(2\delta)\), so

\[ \bar P_w = \frac{\rho}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\int_\text{wall + floor}|\nabla_t\phi|^2\,dS , \tag{15} \]

with \(\nabla_t\phi\) the slip velocity amplitude along the glass. Dividing by \(2E\) and using Green’s identity \(\int|\nabla\Phi|^2dV = \lambda\int\psi^2 dS\) for the energy,

\[ \gamma_w = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\;\frac{I_w + I_b}{E_\Phi}, \qquad I_w = \int_\text{wall}|\nabla_t\Phi|^2\,dS , \tag{16} \]

with \(I_b\) the same integral over the floor. Here \(E_\Phi = \int|\nabla\Phi|^2\,dV\) is the energy (14) without the factors \(\tfrac12\rho\) and the amplitude, which cancel in the ratio. The program evaluates \(I_w\), \(I_b\) and \(E_\Phi\) for each mode of the real glass, on its real wall.

Check. For the deep cylinder and \(m = 1\), with \(\Phi = J_1(kr)\cos\theta\,e^{kz}\), the integrals are elementary: \(I_w/E_\Phi = (1 + 1/\xi^2)/[R(1 - 1/\xi^2)] = 1.837/R\). The logarithmic decrement, the fall of \(\ln a\) per period, is \(\Delta = 2\pi\gamma/\omega\); with \(\omega = \sqrt{\xi g/R}\) this gives \(\Delta = \pi\cdot1.837\,\nu^{1/2}/(\sqrt2\,\xi^{1/4})\,R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\), that is

\[ \Delta_{11} = 3.50\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4} \quad\text{(clean walls, theory)}, \qquad \Delta_{11} = 4.98\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\quad\text{(experiments in tanks [6])} . \tag{17} \]

The measured coefficient is 1.42 times the clean-wall theory. That is no contradiction: the abstract of Miles’s paper on damping in closed basins reports that observed decay rates exceed wall damping alone by factors of two to three, and names surface films and the contact line as the causes [5]. Those are the next two terms.

The skin of the surface

Beer is full of proteins that gather at the surface, and under a head the stout touches the underside of a foam. Either way, the surface resists being stretched. If it cannot stretch at all, the surface is a wall for horizontal motion, and its own Stokes layer dissipates by the same formula (15), now integrated over the surface: \(\gamma_s = c_s\,\tfrac12\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,I_s/E_\Phi\), with \(I_s\) the integral of (16) taken over the surface instead of the wall, \(c_s = 0\) for a clean surface and \(c_s = 1\) for one that cannot stretch. The page’s default is \(c_s = 1\); opinion: a stout under a head is closer to a skin than to a clean surface. With it, the skin takes about as much as the wall.

The interior ★

Even an irrotational flow dissipates, because its parcels are stretched. The power lost per volume is \(2\mu\sum_{ij}e_{ij}^2\), with the rate of strain \(e_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\phi\). Because \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\), its integral becomes a surface integral, \(\mu\oint\partial_n|\nabla\phi|^2dS\). For a deep mode, \(\phi \propto e^{\lambda z}\), so \(|\nabla\phi|^2\) grows toward the surface as \(e^{2\lambda z}\) and its normal derivative there is \(2\lambda|\nabla\phi|^2\). Dividing the period-averaged loss by \(2E\) gives

\[ \gamma_v = 2\nu\lambda^2 , \]

the damping of waves on deep open water. In a round glass the exact value for the slowest mode is \(1.88\nu k^2\), 6 % lower; on the total damping that is 0.1 %. For the slowest mode \(\gamma_v\) is 0.009 per second, 2 % of the total: negligible there, but it grows as \(\lambda^2\), so short waves feel it most computed.

The contact line

Where the surface meets the glass, the edge of the liquid has to creep up and down the wall. A wetting liquid loses somewhat more energy than viscous theory predicts, and non-wetting combinations lose several times more [7]; the loss depends on the amplitude, rising as the waves get smaller until the edge sticks [7]. No first-principles formula for a stout under a head exists. The page uses a constant rate \(\gamma_c\), default 0.05 per second, at the low end of the 0.05 to 0.25 per second measured in a glass tank of water 60 cm long and 6 cm wide [7]. Opinion: a rate measured in another tank, at another frequency, transfers only as an order of magnitude; and under a head the edge of the stout is hidden in foam, so the head term below may already contain part of this loss. Set \(\gamma_c = 0\) to remove any double count.

The head

Sauret and co-workers measured how a layer of foam damps sloshing in a narrow tank [1]. The foam adds a damping rate that grows with its thickness and saturates once the foam is about as thick as the depth the wave reaches, \(1/k\): they found that the bubbles move less the higher they sit above the liquid, fading over that depth. They traced the loss to the foam’s films sliding on the glass walls, so it scales as the wall area per volume of foam: as \(1/w\) for their tank of width \(w\), with \(w\gamma_f = 1.6 \pm 0.3\) cm/s measured [1]. For a round glass the wall per volume is \(2/R\) instead of \(2/w\), so we take

\[ \gamma_f = c_f\,\frac{u_f}{R}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda h_f}\right), \qquad u_f = 1.6\text{ cm/s} , \tag{18} \]

with the factor \(c_f\), default 1, on a slider. This fits with section 5 as follows. Inference: the head is lifted bodily by the surface beneath it (a foam resists being sheared like a soft solid, so its vertical motion is passed up through it), which is what loads the surface; but it slides against the wall only where the sideways flow beneath drags it, in a layer of depth about \(1/\lambda\), which is what dissipates. The transfer from a slot to a round glass, and from 3 mm bubbles in dish soap to the much finer bubbles of a stout’s head, is the least certain step on this page; it could be off by a factor of three either way.

Key result · how long a pint rocks

With its head, the default pint’s slowest slosh decays as \(e^{-\gamma t}\) with \(\gamma = 0.49\) s⁻¹: its amplitude falls to 1/e in 2.0 s and to 5 % in 6.1 s. Without the head the rate is 0.27 s⁻¹, and the same fall takes 3.7 s and 11.2 s. With the walls and the interior alone it would be 9.0 s to 1/e computed. Inference: the walls set the floor of the damping, and everything else in a real pint at least doubles it.

No measured decay time of sloshing in a beer glass was found in the sources searched; the walking-with-coffee study gives a computed, not measured, decrement for a cup [2]. A real test of this note is therefore within anyone’s reach: film a pint after a tap on the glass and count the swings.

08The velocity field in the cut

The figure under the cart is a vertical cut through the glass along the direction in which the stout rocks. The arrows are the velocity \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) of the stout relative to the glass.

Each mode contributes \((\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\nabla\Phi_n\): the arrows are largest when the surface passes level and vanish at the extremes of the swing, when the stout is momentarily at rest and all its energy sits in the raised surface. The program stores each \(\Phi_n\) and its gradient in the same angular Fourier form as the surface shapes, so the page can evaluate the field in any vertical plane through the axis. In the cut, \(\partial_r\Phi\) points along the cut and \(\partial_z\Phi\) up; the component across the cut, \(r^{-1}\partial_\theta\Phi\), is drawn as a ring whose size is its speed. For pure rocking along the cut it is zero; rings appear when modes rock across the cut too, when the cart is pushed sideways, or when \(m \ge 2\) modes are excited.

The fading with depth follows from (7): \(\cosh k(z + D)/\cosh kD \approx e^{kz}\) when \(kD \gg 1\). Laplace’s equation trades variation along the surface for variation with depth: a pattern that changes over a distance \(R\) across the glass must also die out over a distance of order \(R/\xi\) downward. That is why a pint sloshes only in its top two or three centimetres, why the shape of the lower glass hardly changes the frequency, and why the sculpted panels of section 2, ten centimetres down, do not show in Figure 2.

09On the screen, and what is left out

Everything the page draws comes from the equations above, solved in two stages: the modes once, offline, and the whole motion live, while you watch.

The numerical method

Offline, the finite-element program of section 5 solves (9) for 15 fills from 300 to 576 mL and keeps the 14 slowest modes with \(m \le 3\): for the default pint, the pairs (1,1), (2,1), (3,1), (1,2), (2,2), (3,2) along \(x\) and along \(y\), and the ring modes (0,1) and (0,2). The cart drives only the \(m = 1\) modes, but the road’s vertical shaking pumps all of them (section 6), and a jolted surface shows the others; keeping them costs little. For each mode it stores \(\lambda_n\), \(N_n = \int\psi_n^2dS\), the couplings \(Q_n\) and \(Q_{rn}\) for \(x\), \(y\) and the pitch, the damping integrals, and the surface shape as its angular Fourier components at 17 radii; for two fills, 500 and 568 mL, also \(\Phi\) and its gradient on a grid of 17 levels in the cut. The page interpolates between fills; for the straight glass it computes the same quantities from Bessel functions.

Live, the page integrates one linear system for everything that moves: the cart’s six coordinates and the 14 modes together, \(\mathsf M\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf C\dot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf K\mathbf q = \mathbf F\) (section 10), with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method at a fixed step \(\Delta t = 1\) ms, the controller’s force held between its samples. We chose it because the road makes the forcing, and the vertical acceleration the coefficients, change in time, so no exact step formula applies, and because one method for cart and stout keeps the code short. The fastest motion kept, the wheels’ hop at about 28 Hz, has \(\omega\Delta t = 0.18\), well inside the method’s stability limit (\(|\omega\Delta t| \lt 2.8\)), with a phase error per step of order \((\omega\Delta t)^5/120\), about \(2\times10^{-6}\). Measured: the page’s own integrator and an independent one in Python, given the same cart, the same road and the same gains, agree on the peak wave of every run of section 11 to the digits quoted computed. Each frame runs as many steps as real time has passed, but at most 50 ms of it, so a slow computer slows the clock rather than skipping ahead. The vertical acceleration that pumps the modes is taken from the previous step; one millisecond of lag.

Limits of the model

The model is linear: it holds while the wave at the rim is small compared with the radius. The page’s warning at a fifth of the radius is our choice, not a measured threshold, and a generous one: there the surface slope is already about 0.37, and the results are qualitative well before it. The passive cart on the speed bump of section 11 drives the model far past that; its 61 mm “wave” means “the pint is lost”, not a height. A strongly driven slosh in a round glass can also turn into a swirl, the surface rotating around the glass, an effect of the nonlinear terms that this page leaves out (well known from experiments in round tanks; we have not opened a source for it here). The contact line is treated as a constant damping and the head as a lifted mass with wall friction; the dome the head can hold above the rim is a guess. The cart has no roll, its wheels never leave the road, and its tyres are linear springs. The next model would keep the quadratic terms of the surface conditions, which give the swirl and the dependence of frequency on amplitude, and a nonlinear tyre that can lift off.

10The cart and the road

A trolley with a pint on it is a mass on springs over a road: the body bobs and pitches, the wheels follow the ground, and the glass, bolted to the tray, does whatever the body does. This section writes that down, with the stout coupled in, and asks what the body does to the pint before anything controls it.

The machine

The cart is the half-car of vehicle dynamics: a body of mass \(M_b = 25\) kg and pitch inertia \(J_b = 2.0\) kg·m² about its centre of mass, carried by two struts \(l_f = l_r = 0.40\) m ahead of and behind that centre, each a spring \(k = 3000\) N/m and a damper \(c = 120\) N·s/m, standing on two wheels of \(m_w = 1.5\) kg whose tyres are springs of \(k_t = 50\) kN/m (with a little damping, \(c_t = 20\) N·s/m) resting on the road. The glass, 0.42 kg empty, stands \(d_g = 0.10\) m ahead of the centre of mass and \(h_g = 0.25\) m above it, on the tray. All of these are our choice for the page; the sliders change the three that matter most. The body has two freedoms, its heave \(z_b\) and its pitch \(\psi\) (front down positive, as in section 6); each wheel one, \(z_f\) and \(z_r\); the cart as a whole moves along the road and sideways with \(x_c\) and \(y_c\) when it is pushed. The road under each wheel, \(z_{rf}(t)\) and \(z_{rr}(t)\), is the profile \(z_r(s)\) of the road at the wheel’s position, \(s = vt + x_c \pm l\), when the cart travels at speed \(v\).

The equations, from a Lagrangian ★

With six coordinates and fourteen modes, Newton’s law force by force would be long and error-prone. Lagrange’s method needs only the energies. The kinetic energy of the frame, the wheels and the empty glass is the usual \(\tfrac12\) mass × velocity² and \(\tfrac12J\dot\psi^2\); the glass is a point mass at its position on the body. The stout’s kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12\rho\int|\nabla\Phi|^2dV\) with the potential of section 6, \(\Phi = \dot x_O x' + \dot z_O z' + \dot\psi\chi + \sum_n(\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\Phi_n\). Expanding the square gives every coupling at once. The pure terms are \(\tfrac12 m_l(\dot x_O^2 + \dot z_O^2)\), the frozen liquid translating; \(\tfrac12\rho\,\dot\psi^2\!\int|\nabla\chi|^2dV = \tfrac12 J_{sj}\dot\psi^2\), the liquid turning, with the Stokes–Joukowski inertia \(J_{sj}\) in place of a rigid body’s; and \(\tfrac12(\rho N_n/\lambda_n)(1 + \kappa_n)\dot a_n^2\) for each mode, the head’s mass included. The cross terms are the physics: \(\rho\dot x_O\dot a_n\!\int\!\nabla x'\!\cdot\!\nabla\Phi_n\,dV = \rho\,Q_nN_n\,\dot x_O\dot a_n\) by Green’s identity, the same \(Q_n\) as in (12), and likewise \(\rho\,Q_{rn}N_n\,\dot\psi\dot a_n\) for the turn and \(m_l z_{cm}\dot x_O\dot\psi\) between the translation and the turn, with \(z_{cm}\) the height of the stout’s centre of mass above the foot. The potential energy holds the springs, \(\tfrac12k(z_b - l_f\psi - z_f)^2 + \tfrac12k(z_b + l_r\psi - z_r)^2\), the tyres, \(\tfrac12k_t(z_f - z_{rf})^2 + \tfrac12k_t(z_r - z_{rr})^2\), the waves, \(\tfrac12\rho g N_n(1 + \sigma\lambda_n^2/\rho g)a_n^2\), the tilt, \(-\rho g\psi\sum_nQ_nN_na_n\), which is the \(g\psi\) term of (12) seen from the other side, and the weight of what stands above the pitch axis, \(-\tfrac12 g\,(m_g h_g' + m_l(h_g + z_{cm}))\,\psi^2\), an inverted pendulum that the springs easily outweigh. A Rayleigh function holds the dampers, \(\tfrac12c\,\dot d_f^2 + \tfrac12c\,\dot d_r^2\) with \(d\) the strut deflections, the tyres’ damping, the rolling resistance \(\tfrac12c_x\dot x_c^2\), and the modes’ \(\rho N_n(1 + \kappa_n)\gamma_n\dot a_n^2\). Lagrange’s equations then give one linear system,

\[ \mathsf M\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf C\dot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf K\mathbf q = \mathbf F, \qquad \mathbf q = (x_c, y_c, z_b, \psi, z_f, z_r, a_1, \dots, a_{14}) , \tag{19} \]

whose matrices are the second derivatives of the three energies. \(\mathbf F\) holds the road, \(k_t z_{rf} + c_t\dot z_{rf}\) on the front wheel and the same at the rear, the actuators of section 11, \(u_f\) and \(u_r\) pushing up on the body and down on their wheel, and your push. The row of (19) for a mode, divided by \(\rho N_n/\lambda_n\), is exactly (12): the Lagrangian route and the boundary-value route of section 6 give the same equation, which is one check. The others: the page assembles \(\mathsf M\), \(\mathsf C\) and \(\mathsf K\) by hand, term by term; an independent script writes the energies out in SymPy and takes the second derivatives, and the two agree to the last digit; \(\mathsf M\) is symmetric and positive definite, as a kinetic energy must be computed (python3 tools/fig12/check_plant.py).

Key result · the stout pushes back

The couplings \(\rho Q_nN_n\) in \(\mathsf M\) work both ways. The cart’s acceleration drives the stout, and the stout’s acceleration \(\ddot a_n\) pushes back on the cart with the force \(-\rho Q_nN_n\ddot a_n\) and on the body with the moment \(-\rho Q_{rn}N_n\ddot a_n\): the same numbers, by the symmetry of a kinetic energy. The part of the stout that swings rather than rides is the sloshing mass \(m_n = \rho\lambda_nQ_n^2N_n/(1 + \kappa_n)\): 81 g for the slowest mode of the default pint, 2.3 g for the next, out of 504 g of stout computed. On a 27 kg cart that push is small; on the tray of a waiter it is what they feel.

What the cart does by itself

Hold the wheels still and the body is a mass of \(M_s = M_b + m_g + m_l = 25.9\) kg on two springs: it heaves at \(f_z = \sqrt{2k/M_s}/2\pi = 2.42\) Hz and pitches at \(f_\psi = \sqrt{k(l_f^2 + l_r^2)/J}/2\pi = 3.39\) Hz, with the damping ratio 0.30 in heave; the wheels hop on their tyres at about 30 Hz computed. The pitch frequency sits 5 % from the stout’s 3.23 Hz. Inference: that is the whole trouble. A bump pitches the body, the pitch tilts the glass, and the tilt drives the slosh at nearly its own frequency, so each nod of the body adds to the wave. The free motions of the coupled system, cart and stout together, come out at 2.28 Hz (mostly heave), 3.09 Hz (mostly pitch, damping ratio 0.40) and 3.235 Hz (mostly slosh, damping ratio 0.028) computed: the coupling pushes the pitch and the slosh apart a little, and lends the slosh almost nothing of the suspension’s damping, because 81 g of sloshing stout cannot move 26 kg of body.

The road

Four profiles, each starting 1 m down the road. A speed bump, \(z_r = \tfrac12 h_r[1 - \cos(2\pi s/0.5\,\mathrm m)]\) over half a metre, 30 mm high by default; a kerb, a step of 20 mm smoothed over 5 cm; cobbles, humps \(h_r\sin^2(\pi s/0.1\,\mathrm m)\) every 10 cm, 6 mm high; and a rough road, a sum of 120 sine waves whose amplitudes follow the ISO 8608 spectrum of a class C road, \(G_d(n) = G_d(n_0)(n/n_0)^{-2}\) with \(G_d(n_0) = 256\times10^{-6}\) m³ at \(n_0 = 0.1\) cycles per metre [16], between 0.05 and 10 cycles per metre, with fixed random phases. At walking pace, 1.5 m/s, the bump takes a third of a second, about one swing of the stout.

Key result · the passive cart spills

Driven over the 30 mm bump at 1.5 m/s on its springs and dampers alone, the body pitches by 2.7° and the stout climbs 61 mm at the rim in the linear model: far past where the model means anything, and far past the rim computed. Over the kerb it climbs 27 mm; over the 6 mm cobbles 4 mm, which the dome of the head just holds. A waiter with a tray would never pitch it like that, which is what the next section is about.

11Keeping the pint level

A good waiter does not fight the tray; they let the floor do what it does and keep the tray level with their wrists, and if the beer begins to rock they move the tray a little to calm it. This section builds the same two reflexes into the cart: an actuator in each strut, and a controller that watches the body and the slosh.

The actuators

Each strut gets a moving-coil actuator in parallel with its spring and damper, the loudspeaker’s principle: a coil of wire of length \(\ell\) in a magnetic field \(B\) feels the Lorentz force \(F = B\ell I\) when a current \(I\) flows, so the force is set by the current (Fig. 9 derives this actuator in detail). On the page \(B\ell = 20\) N/A and the current is limited to \(I_\text{max} = 8\) A, a force of 160 N at most per strut; the static load on each strut is about 127 N. The force \(u\) pushes up on the body and down on the wheel: an actuator cannot lift the cart off the road, it can only change how the body and the wheel share the road’s shove. Every \(T_s = 10\) ms the controller reads the state and sets both currents, which then stay constant until its next look.

Two laws

Skyhook. The oldest idea in active suspension [17]: damp the body as if a damper tied each of its corners to the sky, \(u_f = -c_\text{sky}(\dot z_b - l_f\dot\psi)\) and \(u_r = -c_\text{sky}(\dot z_b + l_r\dot\psi)\), the velocities of the two corners of the body in the room. A damper to the ground must shake the body whenever the ground shakes; a damper to the sky only ever slows the body down. With \(c_\text{sky} = 400\) N·s/m it needs no model at all, and it already halves the pitch over the bump.

LQR. The second law uses the model. It works in the coordinates a suspension controller can measure [15]: the strut deflections \(d_f, d_r\), the tyre deflections \(\zeta_f, \zeta_r\) (wheel height minus road height), the slosh amplitudes \(a_n\) of the modes along \(x\), and the rates \(\dot x_c, \dot z_b, \dot\psi, \dot z_f, \dot z_r, \dot a_n\): 13 numbers in a state \(\mathbf x\). In these coordinates the road enters (19) only through its rate of rise under each wheel, as a disturbance the regulator does not see, and the tilt is not a state of its own: on a flat road \(\psi = (d_r - d_f + \zeta_r - \zeta_f)/(l_f + l_r)\), and the controller uses that. (A road slope would add to the true tilt; a controller without an inclinometer cannot know a slope from a tilt, and we give it none.) The dynamics are then \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathsf A\mathbf x + \mathsf B\mathbf u\) with \(\mathbf u = (u_f, u_r)\), built by evaluating (19) on each unit state in turn. Holding \(\mathbf u\) for one sample turns this into the map \(\mathbf x_{k+1} = \mathsf A_d\mathbf x_k + \mathsf B_d\mathbf u_k\), with \(\mathsf A_d = e^{\mathsf AT_s}\) and \(\mathsf B_d\) the push of one held force, both from one matrix exponential (Fig. 11 derives the zero-order hold).

The controller sets \(\mathbf u_k = -\mathsf K\mathbf x_k\), with the gains \(\mathsf K\) chosen to make the total cost

\[ J = \sum_k\left[\frac{\eta_\text{rim}^2}{\sigma_\eta^2} + \frac{\psi^2}{\sigma_\psi^2} + \frac{\dot z_b^2}{\sigma_v^2} + \frac{u_f^2 + u_r^2}{\sigma_u^2}\right], \qquad \eta_\text{rim} = \sum_n a_n\psi_n(\text{rim}, 0) , \tag{20} \]

as small as possible, where \(\eta_\text{rim}\) is the rise of the stout at the front of the rim, in the glass’s own frame, which is what spills. Each \(\sigma\) is a tolerance: a wave, a tilt, a heave speed and a force that we are willing to accept. This is the linear–quadratic regulator; with \(\mathsf Q\) and \(\mathsf R\) the weights of (20), its gains are \(\mathsf K = (\mathsf R + \mathsf B_d^{\mathsf T}\mathsf S\mathsf B_d)^{-1}\mathsf B_d^{\mathsf T}\mathsf S\mathsf A_d\), where \(\mathsf S\) solves the discrete Riccati equation \(\mathsf S = \mathsf Q + \mathsf A_d^{\mathsf T}\mathsf S(\mathsf A_d - \mathsf B_d\mathsf K)\); the page iterates this equation to its fixed point whenever a slider moves, about a thousand iterations. On the page \(\sigma_\eta = 0.5\) mm, \(\sigma_\psi = 0.57^\circ\), \(\sigma_v = 50\) mm/s and \(\sigma_u = 20\) N. The slowest motion of the closed loop then decays with a time constant of 1.93 s, which is the cart rolling to a stop along the road, a motion the actuators cannot touch computed.

Why these coordinates, and not simply the body’s height and the wheels’ heights? Because a regulator drives every state it is given toward zero. Given the wheel’s height as a state, it reads a wheel on a bump as a wheel that has hopped, and pushes it down, which lifts the body and tilts the glass: that is what our first design did. Given the tyre’s deflection instead, it reads the same situation as a loaded tyre, which it leaves alone, and spends its force on holding the body still while the wheel rides over. A controller is only as good as the question it is asked.

Key result · what the controller buys

Over the 30 mm bump at walking pace the stout rises 61 mm at the rim on the passive cart (the pint is lost), 15.0 mm with the skyhook damper, and 3.8 mm under the LQR, which keeps the head under its dome limit using at most 60 N, 3.0 A. Over the 20 mm kerb: 27 mm, 7.0 mm and 4.3 mm, the LQR reaching 102 N. On the cobbles the passive cart’s 4.0 mm becomes 0.9 mm computed. The LQR’s tilt over the bump, 1.4°, is larger than the skyhook’s 1.3°: it does not try to hold the body level, it tries to keep the stout level in the glass, and it tilts the glass against the push when that helps, the way a waiter tips a tray into a turn.

How it is checked. The page computes its gains in the browser; an independent design in Python (SciPy’s Riccati solver, on the matrices of plant.py) gives the same gains to \(6\times10^{-12}\) and the same closed-loop time constant, and the page’s own integrator reproduces every peak of Figure 6 to the digits quoted computed (node notes/fig12/gate-ctl3.cjs, python3 tools/fig12/gate_ctl3.py).

What the sliders teach

Shrink \(\sigma_\eta\) and the controller works harder on the slosh and lets the body move more; shrink \(\sigma_\psi\) and it holds the body level even where tilting would have helped the pint. Lower the current limit: on the bump the LQR asks for 60 N of 160, so nothing changes until the limit falls below that; on the kerb it asks for 102 N, and a limit of 60 N lets the stout climb again. Stretch \(T_s\) toward 60 ms and the controller looks too rarely to catch a 3 Hz slosh; the gains it designs for that slow look are gentler, as they must be. Soften the springs and the pitch frequency moves away from the slosh, which helps the passive cart more than any gain. Opinion: the honest limit of this controller is the model it trusts. It assumes it knows the slosh amplitudes exactly; a real one would have to estimate them, for example from a camera looking at the glass, and it would fight the swirl of section 9 with a linear law. It also has no preview of the road: a car that sees the bump coming does better still.
