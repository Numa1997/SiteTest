A Pint of Guinness on a One-Wheel Cart.

Carry a full pint over a doorstep and it is not the lift that spills it but the jolt forward and back. Here the stout is computed in three dimensions, particle by particle, in the real shape of the glass, while a motor drives the cart over the bumps. Push it, drag it, and switch its controller off.

Run

Push or drag the cart

Or hold the ← and → keys, or press on the cart in the figure and drag it: your hand pulls like a spring of 300 N/m with a damper, at most 25 N.

Drive and suspension

Open loop: the motor gives the fixed torque that balances the drag at the set speed. Speed control: a PI loop sampled every 1 ms sets the motor current. The suspension adds up to 40 N between body and axle against the body’s vertical speed.

Road and grid

A cosine bump 25 cm long every 2 m. The glass is open: stout that rises over the rim spills and falls to the road. Measured accuracy: 3 mm swings 4 % slow and tilts 4–6 % too far under a steady push; 4 mm, 6 % and up to 18 %.

Lecture note · Fig. 12 · fluid dynamics · vehicle dynamics · control

Why the pint spills, and how a motor can stop it

Numa Koudsie · for school students and first- and second-year undergraduates

Abstract. A pint of stout in the Guinness tulip glass rides a cart with one wheel, one spring and one damper over bumps. When the wheel climbs a bump, the road pushes on it along the normal to its surface, which leans backward: the cart is braked, then shoved forward on the way down. In the glass’s frame that jolt acts like gravity tipping for a moment, and the stout rocks at its own rhythm, about 3.2 times a second, and can go over the rim. The page computes the stout from the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in three dimensions in the measured shape of the glass, the cart from Newton’s laws with a DC drive motor, a speed controller and an active suspension, and couples the two both ways; spilled stout falls to the road. Every number in this note was measured from the page’s own code.

01Over the doorstep

Walk across a room with a full pint and step over a doorstep. Lifting the glass straight up and setting it down spills almost nothing. What spills it is the moment your hand jerks forward or stops short.

The surface then tilts like the bubble in a spirit level, low on one side and high on the other, and rocks back and forth on its own after your hand is still, about three times a second for a pint. If your steps, or the bumps under a cart, come at that rhythm, each one adds to the last and the stout climbs the wall. A careful waiter does two things: keeps the hand moving at a steady speed, and lets the arm give under the up-and-down of each step. The figure does the same with a motor that holds the speed and a suspension that gives.

Nothing in the picture is drawn from a formula for waves: the stout is computed from the laws of motion of a liquid, and the cart from Newton’s laws. Push the cart, drag it, change its speed, switch the controller off and on, and watch what the stout does.

02The glass

The Guinness pint is the tulip glass: narrow at the foot, swelling to its widest about three quarters of the way up, and closing a little at the rim. Retailers list it as 160 mm tall and 85 mm across at the widest, an imperial pint, 568 ml [1]. No dimensioned drawing of that glass was found; the closest is dimensions.com’s section of the same tulip as a 16 oz glass, 149 mm tall, 3 in across [2]. Its inner outline was measured from that drawing pixel by pixel (the drawing’s widest outer diameter measures 76.6 mm against the 76.2 mm it states) and stretched to the Guinness size: heights by 160/149.2, radii so that the widest outer diameter is 85 mm (tools/fig12/glass_profile.py).

The result is the inner radius \(R(z)\) every 0.5 mm above the floor of the cavity: 28 mm in the lower part, 39.3 mm at the widest (113 mm up), 37.5 mm at the rim, 147.5 mm up; the bottom corner is rounded. Brim-full it holds 530.7 ml, less than an imperial pint: the drawing is the smaller US glass stretched, not the imperial glass itself, and that difference is the uncertainty in the shape. The stout is poured to \(h = 125\) mm: \(\rho\int_0^h\pi R(z)^2\,dz = 426.6\) g.

03The stout in the glass’s frame

On a braking train your coffee leans forward as if gravity had tipped. In the frame of an accelerating container, its acceleration \(\mathbf a_g(t)\) acts on every parcel of liquid as an extra force per unit mass \(-\mathbf a_g\). The glass here moves with the body and never turns (one wheel, a body guided straight up and down), so this is the only change from a glass at rest.

Let \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x, t)\) be the velocity of the stout relative to the glass, \(p\) its pressure, \(\rho\) its density and \(\nu\) its kinematic viscosity. The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in the glass’s frame are

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \mathbf g - \mathbf a_g(t), \tag{1} \] \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0 . \tag{2} \]

Equation (1) is Newton’s second law per unit mass: \(-\nabla p/\rho\) pushes from high to low pressure, \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) is the friction between layers sliding past each other, \(\mathbf g = (0,0,-g)\) is the weight, and \(-\mathbf a_g\) the push of the jolt. Equation (2) says the stout cannot be squeezed. At the wall \(r = R(z)\) and the floor the stout cannot flow through, \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n = 0\); on the free surface the pressure is that of the air, \(p = 0\). Above the rim there is no wall: stout that rises over it leaves the glass.

Two yardsticks. Under a steady push \(a\) the stout comes to rest with a plane surface of slope \(-a/g\); to first order its centre of mass then moves by \(\bar x = -(a/g)\,\pi R(h)^4/(4V)\), for any glass, \(V\) the volume. And a straight glass of radius \(R\) filled to \(h\) swings, by linear theory, at

\[ f_{11} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g\,\xi}{R}\tanh\frac{\xi h}{R}}, \qquad J_1'(\xi) = 0,\ \xi = 1.8412 . \tag{3} \]

The tulip has no such formula; the page’s solver uses neither, they are checks (Section 09).

04The cart and the road’s push

The cart has three coordinates: its position \(x\) along the road, the height \(z_b\) of the body (with the glass), and the height \(z_w\) of the wheel’s axle. A rigid wheel of radius \(R_w = 4\) cm rolling over the road \(z_r(x)\) carries its axle along

\[ z_a(x) = \max_{|s - x| \le R_w}\Big[z_r(s) + \sqrt{R_w^2 - (s - x)^2}\Big], \tag{4} \]

the highest the wheel can sit while touching the road. The tyre is a stiff radial spring and damper; its load is

\[ N = \max\big(0,\; k_t(z_a - z_w) + c_t(\dot z_a - \dot z_w)\big), \tag{5} \]

zero when the wheel leaves the road. The road pushes along the line from the contact point to the axle, perpendicular to the path \(z_a(x)\); its part along the road is

\[ F_\text{road} = -N\,z_a'(x), \tag{6} \]

backward while the wheel climbs, forward on the way down: the jolt. Newton’s laws are

\[ M_x\ddot x = F_\text{road} + F_m - c_x\dot x + F_\text{push} + F_\text{hand} + F_{\ell,x}, \tag{7} \] \[ M_s\ddot z_b = -k(z_b - z_w - L_s) - c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w) + u_s - M_s g + F_{\ell,z}, \tag{8} \] \[ m_w\ddot z_w = k(z_b - z_w - L_s) + c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w) - u_s - m_w g + N . \tag{9} \]

\(M_s = M_b + m_\ell\) is the body and glass (1.5 kg) with the stout still in it; \(m_w = 0.3\) kg the wheel; \(M_x = M_s + m_w + (I_w + N_g^2 J_m)/R_w^2\) adds the rolling inertia of the wheel (a disc) and of the motor’s rotor seen through the gearbox, 2.50 kg with a full glass. The spring \(k = 600\) N/m and damper \(c = 8\) N s/m give the body a bounce at 2.81 Hz; the tyre has \(k_t = 2\times10^4\) N/m, \(c_t = 5\) N s/m; rolling and air drag \(c_x = 2\) N s/m. \(F_m\) is the motor (Section 05), \(u_s\) the suspension actuator (Section 06), \(F_\text{push}\) your buttons or keys, \(F_\text{hand}\) your hand when you drag the cart: a spring of 300 N/m and a damper of 30 N s/m toward the pointer, at most 25 N. \(F_\ell\) is the stout’s (Section 07).

05The motor and its speed controller

The wheel is driven through a gearbox of ratio \(N_g = 10\) by a small DC motor (torque constant \(K_m = 0.02\) N m/A, which is also its back-EMF constant in V s/rad; armature resistance \(R_a = 2\ \Omega\); rotor inertia \(J_m = 2\times10^{-6}\) kg m²; supply 12 V). The wheel rolls without slipping, so the motor turns at \(\omega_m = N_g\dot x/R_w\) and pushes the cart with

\[ F_m = b\,i, \qquad b = \frac{N_g K_m}{R_w} = 5\ \text{N/A}, \tag{10} \]

\(i\) the motor current. A current-controlled drive sets the current: it follows the command with a lag of \(\tau_i = 2\) ms, but never beyond the drive’s limit \(I_\text{max} = 3\) A (15 N), nor beyond what the voltage left over the back-EMF \(e = K_m\omega_m\) can drive, \(|R_a i + e| \le 12\) V.

Open loop. The command is the current that balances the drag at the set speed, \(i_0 = c_x v_0/b\), 0.40 A at 1 m/s. Nothing holds the speed: a bump’s jolt passes straight into the cart’s speed, and so into the glass.

Speed control. Every \(T_s = 1\) ms a controller reads the speed and commands

\[ i_\text{cmd} = i_0 + K_p\,(v_0 - \dot x) + K_i\int (v_0 - \dot x)\,dt . \tag{11} \]

For a mass \(M_x\) pushed by \(b\,i\), the loop crosses over at \(\omega_c\) when \(K_p = M_x\omega_c/b\); we take \(\omega_c = 2\pi\times 8\) Hz, well above the stout’s 3.2 Hz, so the jolt is absorbed by the motor before the glass feels it, and \(K_i = K_p\omega_c/4\), which places the integral’s corner a quarter of the way down: \(K_p = 25.1\) A/(m/s), \(K_i = 316\) A/m with a full glass. The integral stops while the current is at its limit (anti-windup). The controller answers your push too: pushing a speed-controlled cart only makes the motor brake.

06The active suspension

Beside the spring an actuator can push between body and axle with a force \(u_s\). The skyhook law asks for a force against the body’s vertical speed in the room, as if a damper hung it from the sky:

\[ u_\text{s,cmd} = -c_\text{sky}\,\dot z_b, \qquad c_\text{sky} = 2\cdot 0.7\sqrt{k M_s} = 47.6\ \text{N s/m}, \tag{12} \]

sampled every 1 ms, limited to ±40 N, delivered with a lag of 5 ms. It calms the body’s bounce, and with it the up-and-down acceleration that changes the stout’s apparent gravity.

07The stout pushes back, and spills

The stout’s momentum in the room is \(m_\ell\mathbf V_g + \mathbf P\), with \(\mathbf V_g\) the glass’s velocity and \(\mathbf P = \int\rho\,\mathbf u\,dV\) its momentum relative to the glass. Besides gravity only the glass pushes on it, so it pushes on the glass with

\[ \mathbf F_\ell = -m_\ell\,\mathbf a_g - \frac{d\mathbf P}{dt} - m_\ell\,\mathbf g . \tag{13} \]

The first and last terms are the stout as a rigid load; they are in \(M_s\) and \(M_x\). The middle term is the sloshing; the page measures \(\mathbf P\) from the particles at every step and hands \(-\Delta\mathbf P/\Delta t\) to the cart.

Spilling. A particle of stout that is above the rim and outside the rim’s radius has left the glass. Its mass leaves \(m_\ell\); its momentum is counted out of \(\Delta\mathbf P\) (the glass did not push it away at that instant). It becomes a drop in the room, at the glass’s position plus its own and with the glass’s velocity plus its own, and falls freely, \(\ddot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf g\), until it lands on the body’s top or on the road.

08Solving it ★

Particles and a grid. The stout is carried by particles; a grid of 4 mm (or 3 mm) cubes enforces (2). Every \(\Delta t = 5\) ms the particles hand their velocities to the grid; the grid adds gravity, the jolt and viscosity; a pressure is found that makes the flow out of every cell zero; the corrected velocities return to the particles, and the particles move. Each particle also carries how the velocity changes around it, so that little is lost in the handing over: the affine particle-in-cell method, APIC [3].

The pressure. Requiring \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0\) gives a Poisson equation for the pressure, solved by conjugate gradients with an incomplete-Cholesky preconditioner [4]. The glass’s curved wall cuts through the square cells; each cell face counts by the fraction of it inside \(R(z)\) at its height [5]. At the free surface the pressure is zero at the surface itself, found between cell centres from a smooth function built from the particles [4, 6]; a particle that strays outside the wall is put back onto it along the wall’s normal \(\mathbf n \propto (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, -R'(z))\), losing its speed along \(\mathbf n\).

A defect found and fixed. The first version could not hold a steady tilt: under a steady push its centre of mass reached only 15 % of the value it must reach. On a tilted surface many cells hold a few particles yet sit just above the zero of the smooth function; forced to count as liquid there, they received a needless pressure kick every step, which stirred a circulation down the high wall that ate the tilt. Giving each particle a radius of 1.8 particle spacings in that function makes every cell with particles read as liquid, and the tilt holds (Section 09).

Half the glass. The road pushes only along \(x\) and up, and the glass is round, so the flow is its own mirror image across the vertical plane of travel; only \(y \ge 0\) is computed: 25 666 particles on 20 × 10 × 45 cells at 4 mm, 61 407 at 3 mm. Swirling waves that break this symmetry are left out.

Viscosity. \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) is in, with \(\nu = 10^{-6}\) m²/s (water’s). The layer at the wall where viscosity holds the liquid, \(\sqrt{\nu/\omega}\approx 0.2\) mm thick, is far thinner than a cell, so the stout slides freely along the wall.

Time. The cart, motor, controller and suspension are stepped every 0.1 ms inside each fluid step (symplectic Euler for the mechanics; the current and the actuator follow their lags exactly over a step; the controller runs every 1 ms), with the glass’s mean acceleration over the step handed to the stout. Your computer sets the pace: the caption shows the seconds simulated per second. One fluid step (5 ms of the stout’s life) costs about 19 ms of computing at 4 mm on the machine this page was built on, so the run goes about four times slower than life. The solver skips only work whose answer is known beforehand (air with no particle within reach, cells away from the liquid), and gives the same numbers, to the last digit, as the plain loops.

10What the picture shows

The surface drawn is the height of the column of stout over each square of the floor: the volume of the particles standing on it, matched to the volume the glass’s shape opens up to that height, both smoothed over one cell. That is the surface the computation holds, averaged; a wave curling over cannot be shown this way. Within 2.5 cells of the wall the columns are cut by it and hold too few particles to count; there the height is extended straight out from the columns inside.

The head is drawn, not computed. A cream layer 12 mm thick is laid on the computed surface. Real foam is a different material, lighter and partly elastic, and it damps the swing; it is not in this model. The glass is seen slightly from above, 14°; spilled drops are drawn where the solver moves them, with their mirror images.

11What the model leaves out

  • The glass’s exact imperial shape: the profile is the US tulip stretched to the Guinness size (Section 02).
  • The foam head; density and viscosity are water’s; surface tension (with \(\sigma = 0.072\) N/m it would raise the swing frequency by a factor \(\sqrt{1 + \sigma k^2/(\rho g)}\), \(k = \xi/R\): 0.78 %).
  • The boundary layer at the wall, and so the real damping (Section 08); swirl that breaks the mirror symmetry.
  • Air drag on drops; drops that land stay where they land.
  • The motor’s and controller’s numbers are a plausible small drive, not a measured one; the speed sensor and the body’s vertical-speed sensor are taken as ideal.
  • The cart does not pitch or roll: one wheel, a body guided straight up and down.
