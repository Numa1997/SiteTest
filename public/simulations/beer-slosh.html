Lecture note · Fig. 12 · fluid dynamics

Why the pint spills: a jolt, a wave, and the Navier–Stokes equations

Numa Koudsie · for school students and first- and second-year undergraduates

Abstract. A cart with one wheel, one spring and one damper carries a pint of stout over bumps. When the wheel climbs a bump, the road pushes on it along the normal to its surface, which leans backward: the cart is braked, then shoved forward on the way down. In the frame of the glass that jolt acts like gravity tipping for a moment, and the stout starts to rock at its own rhythm, about three and a half times a second. The page computes the stout from the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in three dimensions, with about thirty thousand particles in half the glass, and the cart from Newton’s laws; the two are coupled both ways. Every number in this note was measured from the page’s own code.

01Over the doorstep

Walk across a room with a full pint and step over a doorstep. Lifting the glass straight up and setting it down spills almost nothing. What spills it is the moment your hand jerks forward or stops short.

The surface then tilts like the bubble in a spirit level, low on one side and high on the other, and rocks back and forth on its own after your hand is still. For a pint glass the rocking takes about 0.3 s per swing. If your steps, or the bumps under a cart, happen to come at that rhythm, each one adds to the last and the stout climbs the wall.

The figure is that experiment. The glass sits on a body; under the body are a spring and a damper; under them one wheel rolls on a road with bumps. Nothing in the picture is drawn from a formula for waves: the stout is computed from the laws of motion of a liquid, and the cart from Newton’s laws.

02The stout in the glass’s frame

On a braking train your coffee leans forward as if gravity had tipped. In the frame of an accelerating container, its acceleration \(\mathbf a_g(t)\) acts on every parcel of liquid as an extra force per unit mass \(-\mathbf a_g\). The glass here moves with the body and never turns (one wheel, a body guided straight up and down), so this is the only change from a glass at rest.

Let \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x, t)\) be the velocity of the stout relative to the glass, \(p\) its pressure, \(\rho\) its density and \(\nu\) its kinematic viscosity. The incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in the glass’s frame are

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \mathbf g - \mathbf a_g(t), \tag{1} \] \[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0 . \tag{2} \]

Equation (1) is Newton’s second law per unit mass: the left side is the acceleration of a parcel, the right side the forces on it. \(-\nabla p/\rho\) pushes from high to low pressure, \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) is the friction between layers sliding past each other, \(\mathbf g = (0, 0, -g)\) is the weight, and \(-\mathbf a_g\) is the push the jolt gives. Equation (2) says the stout cannot be squeezed: as much flows into any small box as flows out.

At the walls and the bottom the stout cannot flow through: \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n = 0\). On the free surface the pressure is that of the air, \(p = 0\), and the surface moves with the liquid. The glass is an upright cylinder of inner radius \(R = 4\) cm filled to \(h = 10\) cm, 503 g of stout.

What to expect. For a steady push \(a_x\) the liquid comes to rest tilted, at slope \(-a_x/g\) (a spirit level). For a short jolt it swings. Linear theory of small waves in a cylinder (the slowest mode, with \(J_1'(\xi) = 0\), \(\xi = 1.8412\)) gives the swing frequency

\[ f_{11} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g\,\xi}{R}\tanh\frac{\xi h}{R}} = 3.382\ \text{Hz}, \qquad T = 0.296\ \text{s}. \tag{3} \]

The page does not use (3); it is the yardstick the computation is checked against in Section 06.

03The cart and the road’s push

The cart has three coordinates: its position \(x\) along the road, the height \(z_b\) of the body (with the glass), and the height \(z_w\) of the wheel’s axle. A rigid wheel of radius \(R_w = 4\) cm rolling over the road \(z_r(x)\) carries its axle along the path

\[ z_a(x) = \max_{|s - x| \le R_w}\Big[z_r(s) + \sqrt{R_w^2 - (s - x)^2}\Big], \tag{4} \]

the highest the wheel can sit while touching the road. The tyre is a stiff radial spring and damper: its load is

\[ N = \max\big(0,\; k_t(z_a - z_w) + c_t(\dot z_a - \dot z_w)\big), \tag{5} \]

zero when the wheel leaves the road. The force of the road acts along the line from the contact point to the axle, perpendicular to the path \(z_a(x)\). Its part along the road is

\[ F_x = -N\,z_a'(x) : \tag{6} \]

backward while the wheel climbs (\(z_a' > 0\)), forward on the way down. That is the jolt. Newton’s laws for the three coordinates are

\[ M_x\ddot x = -N z_a'(x) + F_\text{drive} - c_x\dot x + F_\text{push} + F_{\ell,x}, \tag{7} \] \[ M_s\ddot z_b = -k(z_b - z_w - L_s) - c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w) - M_s g + F_{\ell,z}, \tag{8} \] \[ m_w\ddot z_w = k(z_b - z_w - L_s) + c(\dot z_b - \dot z_w) - m_w g + N . \tag{9} \]

\(M_s = 2.00\) kg is the body, glass and stout; \(m_w = 0.3\) kg the wheel; \(M_x = M_s + m_w + I_w/R_w^2\) adds the wheel’s rolling inertia (a disc). The spring \(k = 600\) N/m and damper \(c = 8\) N s/m give the body a bounce at 2.75 Hz with damping ratio 0.12; the tyre has \(k_t = 2\times10^4\) N/m, \(c_t = 5\) N s/m. The drive holds a set speed \(v_0\) against the drag \(c_x\dot x\), \(c_x = 2\) N s/m, by a constant force \(F_\text{drive} = c_x v_0\): it does not resist a jolt, so the cart slows on the climb and speeds up after. \(F_\text{push}\) is yours. \(F_\ell\) is the stout’s, next.

04The stout pushes back

The stout’s momentum in the room is \(m_\ell\mathbf V_g + \mathbf P\), where \(\mathbf V_g\) is the glass’s velocity and \(\mathbf P = \int\rho\,\mathbf u\,dV\) the momentum of its motion relative to the glass. The glass is the only thing besides gravity that pushes on the stout, so by Newton’s third law the stout pushes on the glass with

\[ \mathbf F_\ell = -m_\ell\,\mathbf a_g - \frac{d\mathbf P}{dt} - m_\ell\,\mathbf g . \tag{10} \]

The first and last terms are the stout as a rigid load (its weight and its mass carried along); they are in \(M_s\) and \(M_x\) above. The middle term is the sloshing: the page measures \(\mathbf P\) from the particles at every step and passes \(-\Delta\mathbf P/\Delta t\) to the cart at the next. A swinging pint shoves the cart back and forth.

05Solving it ★

Particles and a grid. The stout is carried by particles; a grid of 4 mm cubes enforces (2). Each step of \(\Delta t = 5\) ms the particles hand their velocities to the grid; the grid adds gravity, the jolt and viscosity; a pressure is found that makes the flow out of every cell zero; the corrected velocities go back to the particles, and the particles move. Handing velocities back and forth loses energy unless each particle also carries how the velocity changes around it; this is the affine particle-in-cell method, APIC [1].

The pressure. Requiring \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0\) after the pressure push gives a Poisson equation \(\nabla^2 p = (\rho/\Delta t)\nabla\cdot\mathbf u^*\) for the pressure, solved by conjugate gradients with an incomplete-Cholesky preconditioner [2]. The round wall cuts through the square cells; each cell face counts by the fraction of it that is open, so the wall is round to the solver, not a staircase [3]. At the free surface the pressure is set to zero at the surface itself, found between cell centres from a smooth function built from the particles [2, 4].

Half the glass. The road pushes only along \(x\) and up; the glass is round. The flow is then a mirror image of itself across the vertical plane through the direction of travel, and only the half \(y \ge 0\) is computed: 30 498 particles on 20 × 10 × 33 cells. Turning swirls that break this symmetry (they can appear in real glasses near resonance) are therefore left out.

Viscosity. The term \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) is in, with \(\nu = 10^{-6}\) m²/s (taken as water’s). At the wall the stout slides freely: the layer where viscosity would hold it, \(\sqrt{\nu/\omega}\approx 0.2\) mm thick, is far thinner than a 4 mm cell, so the grid cannot resolve it.

Time. The cart is stepped every 0.1 ms inside each 5 ms fluid step, with the glass’s mean acceleration over the step handed to the fluid. Your computer decides how fast this runs: the caption shows how many seconds of simulated time pass per second of yours.

07What the picture shows

The black surface is the height of the column of stout over each 4 mm square of the bottom: the volume of the particles standing on it divided by its area, smoothed over one cell. That is the surface the computation holds, averaged; drops thrown above it and a wave curling over cannot be shown this way. Within 1 cm of the wall the columns are cut by the wall and hold too few particles to count; there the height is extended straight out from the two columns inside it.

The head is drawn, not computed. A cream layer 12 mm thick is laid on the computed surface. Real foam is a different material, much lighter and partly elastic, and it damps the swing; it is not in this model.

The readout “front − back wall” is the drawn surface at the front of the glass (the direction of travel) minus that at the back. The glass is seen slightly from above, 14°.

08What the model leaves out

  • The glass is a straight cylinder, not the tulip-shaped Guinness pint.
  • Density and viscosity are water’s; surface tension is left out. With \(\sigma = 0.072\) N/m (water’s, taken here) it would raise the swing frequency by a factor \(\sqrt{1 + \sigma k^2/(\rho g)}\), \(k = \xi/R\): 0.78 %.
  • The foam head (Section 07).
  • Spilling: the solver box has a lid at the rim, 3 cm above the surface. Higher bumps make the stout hit it.
  • The boundary layer at the wall, and so the real damping (Section 05).
  • Swirling waves that break the mirror symmetry (Section 05).
  • The cart does not pitch or roll: one wheel, a body guided straight up and down.
