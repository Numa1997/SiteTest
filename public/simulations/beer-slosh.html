Fig. 12 · lecture note · fluid dynamics

Sloshing in a pint glass

Numa Koudsie · for readers who know Newton’s laws, a little calculus and the idea of a normal mode

Abstract. Anyone who has carried a full pint through a crowded pub knows that the stout does not simply ride along: it rocks, it climbs the wall on one side, and the head goes over the rim. We build the physics of that rocking from Newton’s second law for a fluid. In the frame of the moving glass the stout feels gravity plus the glass’s acceleration; inside it the flow has no rotation, so its velocity is the gradient of a potential that obeys Laplace’s equation; at the surface, gravity pulls the stout back to level. The result is a set of sloshing modes, each an oscillator driven by the glass’s sideways acceleration and pumped by its vertical acceleration. For a straight glass the modes are Bessel functions; for the real Guinness tulip, measured from a photograph and rebuilt in Blender, we compute them by finite elements and check the program against the straight glass. Then we follow the energy: into the thin boundary layers on the wall, into the skin of the surface, and into the friction of the head on the glass, which turns out to be the largest loss of all. A pint with its head settles in about six seconds; without it, in about eleven.

01Carrying a pint

Pick up a full glass and walk. For a few steps nothing happens; then the surface starts to rock, a little higher on each step, until the stout climbs the side of the glass and the head spills over your fingers. Stop walking and the rocking does not stop at once: it dies away over a few seconds, and the dark liquid settles back under its cream-coloured head.

Three things are already visible in that experience. First, the stout has a rhythm of its own: it rocks at a definite frequency, about three times a second in a pint glass, whatever your hand does. Second, your hand can feed that rhythm: if your steps come at a rate close to it, each step adds to the swing, and the amplitude grows. Third, something takes the energy away again, because the rocking dies when you stand still. A physicist recognises a driven, damped oscillator. This note shows that the stout in a glass is a whole family of such oscillators, called sloshing modes, derives their frequencies from the laws of fluid motion and the shape of the glass, and works out, one by one, the processes that damp them.

We use the glass Guinness serves its draught stout in, the 20-ounce tulip pint, and the stout itself at its serving temperature. Nothing steers the glass yet except your hand or a shaker table; a controller that carries a pint without spilling is a later chapter of this collection.

How claims are marked

A statement with a number in square brackets, such as [3], is a fact taken from that source, listed at the end. A statement marked computed is a fact produced by this page’s own code or by the scripts in tools/fig12/, which anyone can rerun. Inference marks reasoning from those facts, and opinion marks a judgement. Everything else is derived on the page.

02The glass and the stout

Before any equation we need two things: the shape of the container, and the properties of what is in it.

The shape, from a photograph

Guinness publishes no drawing of its glass, and retail listings give only an overall size: 160 mm tall and 85 mm across at the widest, holding 568 mL, one imperial pint [8]. So we measured the shape from a side-on product photograph [9]. In each row of pixels the outermost dark pixels mark the edge of the glass, and half their distance is the outer radius at that height. The rim and the foot appear in the photograph as thin ellipses, because the camera looks slightly down on the glass; their centres, not their lowest points, mark the planes of rim and foot. Setting the distance between those planes to the published 160 mm fixes the scale at 0.393 mm per pixel computed. The outline was then smoothed to 0.2 mm, about half a pixel, so that the photograph’s pixel steps do not appear as ripples in the wall.

Two checks say the result is the real glass. The widest diameter comes out as 84.1 mm, against the published 85 mm, a difference of 1 %. And the volume agrees with the glass’s purpose: taking a wall 2.0 mm thick and a solid base 8 mm thick (both read from the photograph), the cavity holds 603.7 mL to the brim, and 568 mL fills it to 152.0 mm, eight millimetres below the rim, where a lined pint glass puts its line computed. Blender revolved the same profile into a closed mesh of 96 sides and measured 603.3 mL; the 0.4 mL difference is exactly what a 96-sided polygon loses against a circle, a factor \(\tfrac{96}{2\pi}\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{96} = 0.99929\) computed.

03A fluid in a moving glass

On a train that brakes, your coffee leans forward as if gravity had tipped. That is the first idea: in the frame of the glass, the glass’s acceleration acts like an extra gravity pointing the other way.

Put coordinates on the glass: \(x\) and \(y\) horizontal, \(z\) up from the foot, and let the glass move without turning, its acceleration being \(\mathbf a_g = (\ddot x, \ddot y, \ddot z)\). A small parcel of stout of density \(\rho\) and velocity \(\mathbf u\) relative to the glass obeys Newton’s second law. In the laboratory, its acceleration is that of the glass plus its own, so in the glass’s frame

\[ \rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \rho\left(\mathbf g - \mathbf a_g\right), \tag{1} \]

with \(p\) the pressure, \(\mu\) the viscosity and \(\mathbf g = (0, 0, -g)\). This is the Navier–Stokes equation; the only new thing is the term \(-\rho\mathbf a_g\), the inertial force of the accelerating frame. Because the stout is incompressible, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u = 0\). The two body forces combine into the gradient of one potential,

\[ \mathbf g - \mathbf a_g = -\nabla\Psi, \qquad \Psi = (g + \ddot z)\,z + \ddot x\,x + \ddot y\,y , \tag{2} \]

as differentiating \(\Psi\) shows. So the stout feels an effective gravity \(\mathbf g_\text{eff} = -\nabla\Psi\): tilted when the glass speeds up sideways, stronger when the glass is lifted with increasing speed, weaker when it is lowered. If \(g + \ddot z\) turns negative, the glass falls faster than free fall and the stout leaves the floor; the page warns when that happens.

Why the interior has no rotation ★

Take the curl of (1). The curl of any gradient is zero, so both \(\nabla p\) and \(\rho\nabla\Psi\) drop out (\(\rho\) is constant). With the vorticity \(\boldsymbol\omega = \nabla\times\mathbf u\) and the identity \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = \nabla(\tfrac12 u^2) - \mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega\), we are left with

\[ \frac{\partial\boldsymbol\omega}{\partial t} = \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\omega) + \nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega . \tag{3} \]

Every term on the right contains \(\boldsymbol\omega\). A stout that starts at rest, \(\boldsymbol\omega = 0\), therefore stays free of vorticity, however the glass is shaken, except where something outside (3) creates it. That something is the wall: the stout must stick to the glass (no slip), while a flow without vorticity slides along it. The conflict is settled in a thin Stokes layer, where the velocity drops to zero and all the vorticity lives. Vorticity spreads by viscosity, a diffusion with coefficient \(\nu\), so in one oscillation period of angular frequency \(\omega\) it reaches a distance

\[ \delta = \sqrt{2\nu/\omega} . \tag{4} \]

For the slowest slosh of our pint, \(\omega = 20.4\) rad/s, this is \(\delta = 0.45\) mm, about one percent of the glass’s radius computed. Outside that skin the flow is irrotational.

Key result · irrotational interior

Outside boundary layers 0.45 mm thick, the velocity of the stout relative to the glass is the gradient of a velocity potential, \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), and incompressibility makes it obey Laplace’s equation, \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\). Viscosity drops out of the interior entirely: for \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), \(\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u = \mu\nabla(\nabla^2\phi) = 0\). It acts only in the layers, where it takes energy away (section 7).

With \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), equation (1) can be integrated once in space. Using \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = \nabla(\tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2)\) when \(\boldsymbol\omega = 0\), every term is a gradient, and

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2 + \frac{p}{\rho} + \Psi = C(t), \tag{5} \]

the unsteady Bernoulli equation. The function \(C(t)\) can be absorbed into \(\phi\), which is defined only up to a function of time.

04Linear sloshing in a straight glass

Laplace’s equation alone has no waves in it. The waves come from the free surface, where gravity pulls a raised patch of stout back down, and the stout below must move to let it.

Boundary conditions

Let the calm surface lie at height \(z_s\), and let the stout surface be raised by \(\eta(x, y, t)\) above it. Three conditions close the problem.

On the wall and the floor the stout cannot pass through the glass: \(\partial\phi/\partial n = 0\), with \(n\) the direction normal to the glass.

At the surface, kinematics: a parcel on the surface stays on the surface, so the surface rises at the vertical speed of the stout there: \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi\) to first order in the small amplitude.

At the surface, dynamics: the pressure just under the surface equals the air pressure (we come back to surface tension and the head in section 5). Putting \(p = p_\text{air}\) in (5) at \(z = z_s + \eta\), dropping the square of the small velocity and moving the constant into \(\phi\),

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + (g + \ddot z)\,\eta + \ddot x\,x + \ddot y\,y = 0 \quad\text{at } z = z_s . \tag{6} \]

Linearisation means evaluating both surface conditions at the calm level \(z_s\) rather than at \(z_s + \eta\). The error is of the order of the wave slope; the page warns when the wave at the wall exceeds a fifth of the radius.

The modes of a cylinder

First let the glass stand still. In a cylinder of radius \(a\) and depth \(D\), with \(r, \theta\) polar coordinates and \(z\) measured from the calm surface, Laplace’s equation separates. Trying \(\phi = \Phi(r)\,\cos(m\theta)\,Z(z)\,e^{i\omega t}\) gives Bessel’s equation for \(\Phi\) and \(Z'' = k^2 Z\); the solution that is finite on the axis and has no flow through the floor at \(z = -D\) is

\[ \phi = A\,J_m(kr)\cos(m\theta)\,\frac{\cosh k(z + D)}{\cosh kD}\,\cos\omega t . \tag{7} \]

No flow through the side wall requires \(J_m'(ka) = 0\), so \(ka\) must be one of the zeros \(\xi_{mn}\) of \(J_m'\): \(\xi_{11} = 1.8412\), \(\xi_{21} = 3.0542\), \(\xi_{01} = 3.8317\), \(\xi_{12} = 5.3314\) (computed with Wolfram Language and SciPy, which agree to all digits). Eliminating \(\eta\) between the two surface conditions with the glass at rest gives \(\partial_t^2\phi + g\,\partial_z\phi = 0\) at \(z = 0\), and putting (7) in,

\[ \omega_{mn}^2 = g\,k_{mn}\tanh(k_{mn}D), \qquad k_{mn} = \xi_{mn}/a . \tag{8} \]

The number \(m\) counts the nodal diameters, lines across the surface that stay level: \(m = 1\) is the familiar rocking, with one side up while the other is down; \(m = 2\) has two nodal diameters; \(m = 0\) is a ring wave that heaves the middle against the edge. The number \(n\) counts nodal circles.

Example 4.1 · the brief’s straight glass

For \(a = 40\) mm and \(D = 99.5\) mm, \(k_{11} = 46.03\) m⁻¹ and \(\tanh(k_{11}D) = 1.000\): the glass is “deep”, since \(\tanh\) has saturated. Then \(\omega_{11} = \sqrt{9.81\times46.03} = 21.25\) rad/s and \(f_{11} = 3.382\) Hz, confirmed with Wolfram Language. The depth no longer matters once \(kD \gt 3\); in our pint \(kD = 6.1\) computed.

Key result · deep sloshing

In a deep glass the slowest mode rocks at \(\omega_{11} \approx \sqrt{1.84\,g/a}\), set only by the radius of the surface: like a pendulum of length \(a/1.84\), about 21 mm for a pint. A wider glass sloshes more slowly.

05The real glass, by finite elements

The tulip glass is not a cylinder: it narrows to a waist, swells, and leans inward again near the rim. Separation of variables fails, but the problem keeps its structure, and a computer can solve it on the measured shape.

The eigenvalue problem

The glass is still a body of revolution, so each mode still varies as \(\cos m\theta\); write \(\phi = \Phi(r, z)\cos m\theta\cos\omega t\). In the meridional plane, the region \(0 \le r \le R_i(z)\) between the floor and the calm surface, with \(R_i\) the inner radius of the glass, \(\Phi\) satisfies

\[ \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial z^2} - \frac{m^2}{r^2}\Phi = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial n} = 0 \text{ on glass}, \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} = \lambda\Phi \text{ at } z = z_s , \tag{9} \]

with the eigenvalue \(\lambda = \omega^2/g\) appearing only in the surface condition. Mathematicians call this a Steklov problem. Multiply the equation by any smooth function \(\psi\) and by \(r\), integrate over the region and integrate by parts once. The wall and floor terms vanish because \(\partial_n\Phi = 0\), and the surface term is replaced using its condition:

\[ \int\!\!\int\left(\Phi_r\psi_r + \Phi_z\psi_z + \frac{m^2}{r^2}\Phi\psi\right) r\,dr\,dz = \lambda\int_0^{a}\Phi\,\psi\;r\,dr . \tag{10} \]

This weak form is what the finite-element method solves. The region is cut into triangles; on each, \(\Phi\) is a quadratic polynomial fixed by its values at six nodes; requiring (10) for every such basis function \(\psi\) gives a matrix equation \(\mathsf K\boldsymbol\Phi = \lambda\,\mathsf M\boldsymbol\Phi\), in which \(\mathsf M\) has entries only at the surface nodes. Eliminating the interior nodes leaves a small symmetric eigenvalue problem on the surface computed, tools/fig12/modes.py.

Checking the program on the cylinder

A program that has not reproduced a known answer has proved nothing. For a cylinder of 40 mm radius and 100 mm depth the exact eigenvalues are \(\lambda = k\tanh kD\) from (8). On a mesh of 16 × 40 quadratic cells the program gives \(\lambda_{11} = 46.02036\) m⁻¹ against the exact 46.02035, a relative error of \(3\times10^{-7}\); the worst of nine modes, (0,3), is off by \(2.4\times10^{-4}\), and halving the cells reduces every error computed, tools/fig12/check_cylinder.py. The integrals the damping needs (section 7) agree with the exact Bessel integrals, evaluated independently in Wolfram Language, to \(7\times10^{-5}\).

What the tulip shape does

Run on the measured glass for every fill from 300 to 590 mL, the program gives the frequencies of Figure 2. Below the widest point the wall leans outward as it rises; above it, the wall leans inward. Compared with a straight glass of the same surface radius and depth, the outward-leaning glass sloshes more slowly (3.14 against 3.25 Hz at 300 mL) and the inward-leaning one faster (3.37 against 3.30 Hz at 590 mL) computed. Inference: near the surface, where the sloshing flow lives, an outward-leaning wall gives the stout more room to swing sideways for the same rise at the wall, which acts like a heavier pendulum bob; an inward-leaning wall squeezes the same flow into less room, a stiffer spring.

The head and the surface tension

Two corrections change the surface condition (6). Surface tension adds a pressure \(-\sigma\nabla^2\eta\) under a curved surface, which raises \(\omega^2\) by the fraction \(\sigma\lambda^2/(\rho g)\); for our pint that is 1.0 % in \(\omega^2\) computed. The head is heavier. Treat it as a layer of mass \(M_f\) per area that rides up and down with the surface: Newton’s law for the layer says the stout must push on it with an extra pressure \(M_f\,\ddot\eta\), which enters (6) as an inertia. With both,

\[ \omega_n^2 = \frac{g\lambda_n + \sigma\lambda_n^3/\rho}{1 + \mu_{f,n}}, \qquad \mu_{f,n} = \frac{M_f\lambda_n}{\rho} . \tag{11} \]

The added-mass ratio \(\mu_f\) compares the head’s mass with the mass of stout that sloshes, a layer of depth \(1/\lambda\). For the default pour, \(\mu_{f,11} = 0.128\), and the head lowers \(f_1\) from 3.445 to 3.244 Hz computed. Opinion: this is the crudest piece of the model. A real head also has a little stiffness of its own, and its liquid fraction is not measured; the slider for \(\varepsilon_f\) lets you see how much it matters.

Example 5.1 · the default pint

500 mL of stout fills the tulip to \(z_s = 137.5\) mm, where the surface has radius \(a = 39.4\) mm and the stout is \(D = 129.5\) mm deep. The program gives \(\lambda_{11} = 47.27\) m⁻¹, so the bare frequency is \(\sqrt{9.81\times47.27}/2\pi = 3.427\) Hz; surface tension raises it to 3.445 Hz, and the head lowers it to 3.244 Hz computed.

06Driving the modes: hand and shaker

Now let the glass move. The motion enters only through the surface condition (6), and there it does two different things.

Write the surface displacement as a sum over modes, \(\eta = \sum_n a_n(t)\,f_n(r)\cos m\theta\) plus the matching \(\sin m\theta\) copies, where \(f_n(r) = \Phi_n(r, z_s)\) is the mode’s surface shape, scaled so that \(f_n = 1\) at the wall: then \(a_n\) is the rise of the stout at the wall, in metres. The potential is \(\phi = \sum_n (\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\,\Phi_n\cos m\theta\), because the kinematic condition requires \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi = \lambda_n\phi\) at the surface for each mode. Put both into (6), multiply by \(f_k\cos m\theta\, r\) and integrate over the surface. The modes are orthogonal on the surface (a consequence of (10) being symmetric), so one equation is left for each mode:

\[ \ddot a_n + 2\gamma_n\dot a_n + \omega_n^2\left(1 + \frac{\ddot z}{g}\right)a_n = -\frac{\lambda_n Q_n}{1 + \mu_{f,n}}\,\ddot x, \qquad Q_n = \frac{\int_0^a f_n\,r^2\,dr}{\int_0^a f_n^2\,r\,dr} , \tag{12} \]

with the damping rate \(\gamma_n\) of section 7 added. (For clarity the \(\sigma\) term of (11) is not repeated; the page keeps it.) The \(\sin\) copies obey the same equation with \(\ddot y\) in place of \(\ddot x\).

Key result · two ways in

Sideways acceleration drives. The term \(\ddot x\,x\) in (6) is a tilt of the effective gravity; it pushes on every mode whose shape has a sideways lean, which in a round glass means \(m = 1\) only, through the coupling \(Q_n\). Vertical acceleration pumps. \(\ddot z\) multiplies \(a_n\): it changes the strength of gravity, the spring constant of every mode, without pushing any of them. It can only amplify a slosh that is already there.

For a straight glass the coupling has a closed form: with \(f = J_1(kr)/J_1(\xi)\) and the Bessel integrals \(\int_0^a r^2J_1(kr)\,dr = a^2J_2(\xi)/k\) and \(\int_0^a rJ_1^2\,dr = \tfrac{a^2}{2}(1 - 1/\xi^2)J_1^2(\xi)\), using \(J_2(\xi) = J_1(\xi)/\xi\) at a zero of \(J_1'\), one finds \(Q = 2a/(\xi^2 - 1)\). The finite-element program reproduces the equivalent coefficient \(2a/((\xi^2-1)J_1(\xi)) = 0.057528\) m of the brief to all six digits computed.

Resonance

If the glass is shaken sideways as \(x = A\sin\Omega t\) at the mode’s own frequency, \(\Omega = \omega_n\), the steady amplitude of (12) is the push divided by the damping, \(a_n = \lambda_nQ_nA\omega_n^2/[(1 + \mu_f)\,2\gamma_n\omega_n]\).

Example 6.1 · a fifth of a millimetre

Shake the default pint sideways by \(A = 0.2\) mm at \(f_1 = 3.24\) Hz. The glass’s acceleration is only \(A\omega^2 = 0.083\) m/s², under a hundredth of \(g\); yet the steady wave at the wall is 5.6 mm computed, 28 times the shaking. That is what the button Sideways at f₁ does.

Pumping: Faraday’s waves ★

Shake the glass up and down, \(z = A\sin\Omega t\), and (12) for one mode becomes \(\ddot a + 2\gamma\dot a + \omega^2(1 - \epsilon\sin\Omega t)\,a = 0\) with \(\epsilon = A\Omega^2/g\): a pendulum whose gravity is modulated, the Mathieu equation. Its strongest instability lies at \(\Omega = 2\omega\), twice the mode’s frequency. There a slosh of amplitude \(a\cos(\omega t + \varphi)\), with the right phase, gains energy each half cycle, at an amplitude growth rate \(\epsilon\omega/4\), as averaging the modulated spring over one period shows. It grows when the gain beats the damping,

\[ \frac{\epsilon\,\omega}{4} \gt \gamma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A \gt \frac{4\zeta g}{\Omega^2}, \qquad \zeta = \gamma/\omega . \tag{13} \]

Michael Faraday saw these waves in 1831, at half the frequency of the vibration that drove them. For the default pint the threshold is \(A = 0.58\) mm at \(\Omega = 2\omega_1\); the button Up and down at 2f₁ shakes 2 mm, for which (13) predicts a net growth rate of 1.2 per second. Run on the page’s own integrator, a micrometre of initial disorder grows at 0.6 per second in the first two seconds, while the part of the seed with the wrong phase dies away, and at 1.1 per second from the fifth second on computed, notes/fig12/gate.cjs. (A linear model gives no seed by itself: the page starts every mode at about a micrometre, standing for the disorder a real surface always has.)

The hand

Your pointer does not move the glass directly. The glass follows it like a mass on a critically damped spring of natural frequency \(\Omega_h\), \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g = \Omega_h^2(\mathbf x_\text{hand} - \mathbf x_g) - 2\Omega_h\dot{\mathbf x}_g\), so a sudden jerk of the pointer becomes a finite acceleration. Opinion: this is a cartoon of a human arm, not a model of one; its one honest content is that a stiff hand (large \(\Omega_h\)) transmits sharp accelerations and a soft hand filters them. People walk at about 1 to 2.5 steps per second, and a coffee cup sloshes at 2.6 to 4.3 Hz [2], close enough for the steps to excite the cup on their harmonics; the same holds for a pint.

07Where the energy goes

A sloshing pint stops within seconds; an ideal fluid would rock for ever. Each loss below is computed on its own and has its own switch on the page.

The energy method

The energy of a mode is its kinetic energy at the moment the surface passes level, \(E = \tfrac12\rho\int|\nabla\phi|^2\,dV\). If a process dissipates the average power \(\bar P\), the energy decays as \(e^{-2\gamma t}\) and the amplitude as \(e^{-\gamma t}\), with

\[ \gamma = \frac{\bar P}{2E} . \tag{14} \]

Each loss is then a matter of computing \(\bar P\) with the flow of the lossless mode. This is accurate as long as the losses are small, \(\gamma \ll \omega\); here \(\gamma/\omega\) is 2 to 4 %.

The boundary layers on wall and floor ★

Just outside the Stokes layer the flow slides along the glass with the velocity \(U\cos\omega t\) given by the potential. Inside, at distance \(s\) from the glass, the solution of the diffusion equation that sticks to the glass is \(u = U[\cos\omega t - e^{-s/\delta}\cos(\omega t - s/\delta)]\). The power per unit area turned into heat is \(\mu\int(\partial u/\partial s)^2 ds\). The square of the gradient averages over a period to \((U^2/\delta^2)\,e^{-2s/\delta}\), and the integral to \(U^2/(2\delta)\), so

\[ \bar P_w = \frac{\rho}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\int_\text{wall + floor}|\nabla_t\phi|^2\,dS , \tag{15} \]

with \(\nabla_t\phi\) the slip velocity amplitude along the glass. Dividing by \(2E\) and using Green’s identity \(\int|\nabla\Phi|^2dV = \lambda\int f^2 dS\) for the energy,

\[ \gamma_w = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\;\frac{I_w + I_b}{E}, \qquad I_w = \int_\text{wall}\left(\Phi_\ell^2 + \frac{m^2\Phi^2}{r^2}\right)r\,d\ell , \tag{16} \]

with \(\Phi_\ell\) the derivative along the wall’s profile and \(I_b\) the same integral over the floor. The program evaluates \(I_w\), \(I_b\) and \(E\) for each mode of the tulip glass.

Check. For the deep cylinder and \(m = 1\), with \(\Phi = J_1(kr)e^{kz}\), the integrals are elementary: \(I_w/E = (1 + 1/\xi^2)/[a(1 - 1/\xi^2)] = 1.837/a\). The logarithmic decrement \(\Delta = 2\pi\gamma/\omega\) is then

\[ \Delta_{11} = 3.50\,\nu^{1/2}a^{-3/4}g^{-1/4} \quad\text{(clean walls, theory)}, \qquad \Delta_{11} = 4.98\,\nu^{1/2}a^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\quad\text{(experiments in tanks [6])} . \tag{17} \]

The measured coefficient is 1.42 times the clean-wall theory. That is no contradiction: Miles showed that the damping observed in the classic experiments exceeds wall damping alone by factors of up to two or three, and traced the excess to surface films and to the contact line [5]. Those are the next two terms.

The skin of the surface

Beer is full of proteins that gather at the surface, and under a head the stout touches the underside of a foam. Either way, the surface resists being stretched. If it cannot stretch at all, the surface is a wall for horizontal motion, and its own Stokes layer dissipates by the same formula (15), now integrated over the surface: \(\gamma_s = c_s\,\tfrac12\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,I_s/E\), with \(c_s = 0\) for a clean surface and \(c_s = 1\) for one that cannot stretch [5]. For the default pint the skin, at \(c_s = 1\), takes about as much as the wall.

The bulk

Even an irrotational flow dissipates, because its parcels are stretched. The dissipation is \(2\mu\int e_{ij}e_{ij}\,dV\) with the strain rate \(e_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\phi\); for a mode that decays with depth as \(e^{\lambda z}\) this gives \(\gamma_b = 2\nu\lambda^2\), the damping of deep-water waves. For the slowest mode it is 0.009 per second, 2 % of the total: negligible for the slowest mode, but it grows as \(\lambda^2\), so short waves feel it most computed.

The contact line

Where the surface meets the glass, the edge of the liquid has to creep up and down the wall. A wetting liquid loses somewhat more energy than viscous theory predicts, and non-wetting combinations lose several times more [7]; the loss depends on the amplitude, rising as the waves get smaller until the edge sticks [7]. No first-principles formula for a stout under a head exists. The page uses a constant rate \(\gamma_c\), default 0.05 per second, at the low end of the 0.05 to 0.25 per second measured in a water tank [7]. Opinion: under a head the edge of the stout is hidden in foam, and the head term below probably absorbs most of this.

The head

Sauret and co-workers measured how a layer of foam damps sloshing in a narrow tank [1]. The foam adds a damping rate that grows with its thickness and saturates once the foam is about as thick as the depth the wave reaches, \(1/k\). They traced the loss to the foam’s films sliding on the glass walls, so it scales as the wall area per volume of foam: as \(1/w\) for their tank of width \(w\), with \(w\gamma_f = 1.6 \pm 0.3\) cm/s measured [1]. For a round glass the wall per volume is \(2/a\) instead of \(2/w\), so we take

\[ \gamma_f = k_f\,\frac{U_f}{a}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda h_f}\right), \qquad U_f = 1.6\text{ cm/s} , \tag{18} \]

with the factor \(k_f\), default 1, on a slider. Inference: this transfer from a slot to a round glass, from 3 mm bubbles in dish soap to sub-millimetre bubbles in stout, is the least certain step on this page; it could be off by a factor of three either way [1].

Key result · how long a pint rocks

With its head, the default pint’s slowest slosh decays as \(e^{-\gamma t}\) with \(\gamma = 0.50\) s⁻¹: its amplitude falls to 1/e in 2.0 s and to 5 % in 6.0 s. Without the head the rate is 0.27 s⁻¹, and the same fall takes 3.7 s and 11.1 s. With clean walls alone it would be 8.9 s to 1/e computed. Inference: the walls set the floor of the damping, and everything else in a real pint at least doubles it.

No measured decay time of sloshing in a beer glass was found in the sources searched; the walking-with-coffee study gives a computed, not measured, decrement for a cup [2]. A real test of this note is therefore within anyone’s reach: film a pint after a tap on the glass and count the swings.

08The velocity field in the cut

The figure under the glass is a vertical cut through it along the direction in which the stout rocks. The arrows are the velocity \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) of the stout relative to the glass.

Each mode contributes \((\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\nabla(\Phi_n\cos m\theta)\): the arrows are largest when the surface passes level and vanish at the extremes of the swing, when the stout is momentarily at rest and all its energy sits in the raised surface. In the cut, the component \(\partial_r\Phi\) points along the cut and \(\partial_z\Phi\) up; the component across the cut, \(-m\Phi\sin(m\theta)/r\), is drawn as a ring whose size is its speed.

The fading with depth follows from (7): \(\cosh k(z + D)/\cosh kD \approx e^{kz}\) when \(kD \gg 1\). Laplace’s equation trades variation along the surface for variation with depth: a pattern that changes over a distance \(a\) across the glass must also die out over a distance of order \(a/\xi\) downward. That is why a pint sloshes only in its top two or three centimetres, and why the shape of the lower glass hardly changes the frequency.

09On the screen, and what is left out

The numerical method

Offline, tools/fig12/build_modes.py solves (9) for 30 fills from 300 to 590 mL and keeps eight radial shapes: \(m = 0\) with \(n = 1, 2\); \(m = 1\) with \(n = 1, 2, 3\); \(m = 2\) with \(n = 1, 2\); and \(m = 3\) with \(n = 1\). With the \(\sin\) copies of \(m \ge 1\) that makes 14 oscillators. For each it stores \(\lambda_n\), \(Q_n\), the damping integrals, the surface shape \(f_n\) at 25 radii, and, for six fills, \(\Phi\), \(\Phi_r\) and \(\Phi_z\) on a 12 × 20 grid in the cut. The page interpolates between fills; for the straight glass it computes the same quantities from Bessel functions.

Live, the page integrates the hand’s follower and the 14 equations (12) with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method at a fixed step \(h = 1\) ms. The fastest mode kept, (1,3) at about 6.4 Hz, has \(\omega h = 0.04\), far inside the method’s stability limit (\(|\omega h| \lt 2.8\)), with a phase error per step of order \((\omega h)^5/120\), about \(10^{-9}\). Each frame runs as many steps as real time has passed, capped at 50 ms per frame.

Where the drawing differs from the model

The 3D view draws the glass from the measured profile and the stout’s surface from \(\eta(r, \theta)\) at 16 radii and 72 angles. The head is drawn as a layer of constant thickness on top of the surface, which is how the model treats its mass; a real head bends. The cut uses the velocity fields of the nearest of the six stored fills, scaled to the present one. Arrows longer than 34 px are cut short and drawn pink. While the arrows are shown, the stout is drawn see-through, which real stout is not.

Limits of the model

The model is linear: it holds while the wave at the wall is small compared with the radius. Beyond about a fifth of the radius, real sloshing in a round glass turns into a swirl, the surface rotating around the glass, an effect of the nonlinear terms that this page leaves out. The contact line is treated as a constant damping, the head as a rigid mass with wall friction, and the glass as perfectly smooth. The next model would keep the quadratic terms of the surface conditions (giving swirl and the bending of the resonance curve), give the head an elasticity of its own, and measure its damping in a real glass.
