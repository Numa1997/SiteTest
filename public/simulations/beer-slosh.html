Fig. 12 · lecture note · fluid dynamics and control

A pint of stout on a suspension

Numa Koudsie · for readers who know Newton’s laws, derivatives and the idea of a normal mode; the parts marked ★ use vector calculus, Bessel functions and matrices, and their results are restated without them

Abstract. A pint carried across a pub, or driven over a speed bump on a trolley, does not simply ride along: the stout rocks, climbs the glass on one side, and the head goes over the rim. We build the physics of that rocking from Newton’s second law for a fluid. In the frame of the glass the stout feels gravity plus the glass’s acceleration; inside it the flow has no rotation, so its velocity is the gradient of a potential; at the surface, gravity pulls the stout back to level. The result is a set of sloshing modes, each an oscillator driven by the glass’s sideways acceleration and by its tilt, and pumped by its vertical acceleration. For a straight glass the modes are Bessel functions; for the real Guinness glass, drawn from its engineering elevation and sculpted on two sides like the real thing, we compute them by finite elements in three dimensions and check the program against the straight glass. We follow the energy into the boundary layers, the skin of the surface and the friction of the head, which is the largest loss. Then the glass is put on a cart with one sprung wheel and driven over a bump. The road pushes the wheel along the normal to its surface, back on the way up and forward on the way down, and that kick along the road, lasting one period of the stout at walking pace, throws it over the rim. A moving-coil actuator in the strut and a drive motor, under a controller designed from the whole model, cart and stout together, keep the wave at the rim to about a millimetre. Every number on the page is computed, and the ones that are guesses are marked as such.

01Carrying a pint

Pick up a full glass and walk. For a few steps nothing happens; then the surface starts to rock, a little higher on each step, until the stout climbs the side of the glass and the head spills over your fingers. Stop walking and the rocking does not stop at once: it dies away over a few seconds, and the dark liquid settles back under its cream-coloured head.

Three things are already visible in that experience. First, the stout has a rhythm of its own: it rocks at a definite frequency, about three times a second in a pint glass, whatever your hand does. Second, your hand can feed that rhythm: if your steps come at a rate close to it, each step adds to the swing, and the amplitude grows. Third, something takes the energy away again, because the rocking dies when you stand still. A physicist recognises a driven, damped oscillator. This note shows that the stout in a glass is a whole family of such oscillators, called sloshing modes, derives their frequencies from the laws of fluid motion and the shape of the glass, and works out, one by one, the processes that damp them.

We use the glass Guinness serves its draught stout in, the 20-imperial-ounce “gravity” glass of 2010 (568 mL), and the stout itself at its serving temperature. The glass stands on a cart: a body on one sprung wheel, the simplest picture of a car or a trolley, which drives over a bump, a kerb or cobbles while you watch the stout. In sections 10 and 11 the cart gets a strut actuator, a drive motor and a controller, and the question becomes the one every waiter answers with their wrists: how do you keep the pint level when the ground does not stay still?

02The glass and the stout

Before any equation we need two things: the shape of the container, and the properties of what is in it.

The shape, from a drawing

Guinness publishes no drawing of its glass; retail listings give an overall size, 160 mm tall and 85 mm across at the widest, holding 568 mL, one imperial pint [8]. A dimensioned elevation of the same tulip shape exists, however, drawn for the 473 mL version sold in America: 149 mm tall and 76 mm across [9]. Its outline is a vector drawing, so the half-width of the glass can be read off at every height with no pixel noise. We write \(Z\) for the height above the foot of the glass. Scaling the drawing to the imperial glass takes two different factors, 160/149 = 1.074 in height and 85/76 = 1.12 in width: the imperial glass is 4 % wider for its height than the drawing of the American one, so our glass is the drawing stretched 4 % more in width than in height, a modelling choice, stated here once.

The outline alone gives the outside. The inside needs two more numbers. The floor of the cavity sits about 8 mm above the foot, as the thick base of the real glass shows; the wall we take 2.0 mm thick, a typical value for a toughened pint glass, measured normal to the wall. The body of revolution this gives has its waist 61.5 mm across at \(Z = 40\) mm, its widest point 85.0 mm at \(Z = 124.5\) mm, and a rim of 80.7 mm; it holds 576 mL to the brim computed. Inference: that is the right size. British pint glasses are brim measure: the 568 mL fill the glass to its rim, and the head stands above it as a dome. Our cavity takes the pint to 158.2 mm, 1.8 mm below the rim. The check is only as strong as its two free numbers, the 2.0 mm wall and the depth of the sculpted panels below: each millimetre of panel depth moves the brim volume by 7 mL, the same size as the 8 mL margin the check rests on, so it says the geometry is right to about a millimetre, no better.

The sculpted panels

The glass is not quite a body of revolution. When Diageo redesigned it in 2010, the lower body was “embossed with curves inspired by the harp logo” [13]; in photographs of the clear glass [14] a crease sweeps from the centre of the foot up to each side at about half height, a V on the front and another on the back, and the wall inside each V is set back a little. So the wall is pressed in on two opposite sides, and the glass keeps a two-fold symmetry: turn it by 180° about its axis and it looks the same, but a quarter turn changes it. We model the recess as a smooth panel of depth \(d_p = 2.0\) mm inside the V, blending into the round body between 60 and 100 mm up; the V’s bottom sits 16 mm above the foot and its arms reach the sides at 80 mm. Opinion: the depth is read from the photographs’ shading, not measured; it could be half or double. At 50 mm up the cavity is 27.0 mm in radius at the front and 29.0 mm at the sides computed. The panels remove 14 mL from the round cavity’s 590 mL, which is how the brim volume comes to 576 mL.

03A fluid in a moving glass

On a train that brakes, your coffee leans forward as if gravity had tipped. That is the first idea: in the frame of the glass, the glass’s acceleration acts like an extra gravity pointing the other way.

Put coordinates on the glass: \(x\) and \(y\) horizontal, \(z\) vertical, all fixed to the glass and moving with it. Let the glass move without turning for now (it turns in section 6), its own position being \((x_g, y_g, z_g)\) in the room, and write its acceleration \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g = (\ddot x_g, \ddot y_g, \ddot z_g)\); a dot means a time derivative. Take a small parcel of stout of density \(\rho\) and velocity \(\mathbf u\) relative to the glass. In the room its acceleration is that of the glass plus its own, \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_g + D\mathbf u/Dt\), where \(D\mathbf u/Dt = \partial\mathbf u/\partial t + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\) is the acceleration of the parcel as it moves along, not of the point it happens to pass. Newton’s second law per unit volume, with the forces a fluid can exert, is the Navier–Stokes equation; moving the frame’s acceleration to the right-hand side,

\[ \rho\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf u + \rho\left(\mathbf g - \ddot{\mathbf x}_g\right). \tag{1} \]

Each term is a force per unit volume. On the left is mass times acceleration. \(-\nabla p\) is the push of the pressure \(p\): a parcel is pushed from where the pressure is high to where it is low. \(\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u\) is the rubbing of neighbouring layers that slide past each other, with \(\mu\) the viscosity. \(\rho\mathbf g\), with \(\mathbf g = (0, 0, -g)\), is the weight, and \(-\rho\ddot{\mathbf x}_g\) is the inertial force of the accelerating frame, the push your coffee feels on the braking train. (Notation: \(\partial/\partial t\) is the rate of change at a fixed point, holding the other variables still; \(\nabla\), “nabla”, collects the rates of change in the three directions, \(\nabla p = (\partial_xp, \partial_yp, \partial_zp)\); \(\nabla^2 = \partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2\) is the Laplacian, the operator that compares a quantity with the average over its neighbours.)

The two body forces combine into the gradient of one potential,

\[ \mathbf g - \ddot{\mathbf x}_g = -\nabla\Psi, \qquad \Psi = (g + \ddot z_g)\,z + \ddot x_g\,x + \ddot y_g\,y , \tag{2} \]

as differentiating \(\Psi\) shows. \(\Psi\) is a potential energy per kilogram, in J/kg = m²/s². So the stout feels an effective gravity \(\mathbf g_\text{eff} = -\nabla\Psi\): tilted when the glass speeds up or slows down sideways, stronger when the glass accelerates upward (starts a lift, or hits the far side of a dip), weaker when it accelerates downward. A glass carried at constant velocity, up, down or sideways, changes nothing. If \(g + \ddot z_g\) turns negative, the glass is accelerated downward faster than a falling stone and the stout leaves the floor; the page warns when that happens.

Why the interior has no rotation ★

Divide (1) by \(\rho\) and take its curl. The curl of any gradient is zero, so both \(\nabla p\) and \(\nabla\Psi\) drop out. With the vorticity \(\mathbf w = \nabla\times\mathbf u\), the identity \((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u = \nabla(\tfrac12 u^2) - \mathbf u\times\mathbf w\), and the fact that the curl and the Laplacian commute, we are left with

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf w}{\partial t} = \nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf w) + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf w , \tag{3} \]

with \(\nu = \mu/\rho\). Every term on the right contains \(\mathbf w\). A settled pint, \(\mathbf w = 0\), therefore stays free of vorticity however the glass is shaken, except where something outside (3) creates it. (A freshly poured Guinness is not settled: its sinking bubbles drive a circulation [3], [4], which we wait out.) What creates vorticity is the wall: the stout must stick to the glass, while a flow without vorticity slides along it. The conflict is settled in a thin Stokes layer. In Stokes’s exact solution for a fluid beside a wall oscillating at angular frequency \(\omega\), the disturbance falls by a factor \(e\) over the distance

\[ \delta = \sqrt{2\nu/\omega} , \tag{4} \]

the distance vorticity diffuses in a time \(1/\omega\). For the slowest slosh of our pint, \(\omega = 20.3\) rad/s, this is \(\delta = 0.45\) mm, about one percent of the glass’s radius computed.

The flow outside the layers

Key result · irrotational interior

Outside boundary layers about 0.45 mm thick, the stout does not rotate, and its velocity relative to the glass is the gradient of a velocity potential \(\phi\), \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\): the flow runs uphill on the landscape \(\phi\), steepest where the landscape is steepest. Incompressibility then reads \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\), Laplace’s equation. Viscosity drops out of the interior entirely, because for \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) the viscous force is \(\mu\nabla(\nabla^2\phi) = 0\); it acts only in the layers, where it takes energy away (section 7).

With \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\), every term of (1) is a gradient (the convective term becomes \(\nabla(\tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2)\)), so (1) can be integrated once in space:

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \tfrac12|\nabla\phi|^2 + \frac{p}{\rho} + \Psi = C(t), \tag{5} \]

the unsteady Bernoulli equation. The function \(C(t)\) can be absorbed into \(\phi\): only the gradient of \(\phi\) is a velocity, so adding a function of time alone to \(\phi\) changes nothing physical.

The model in one place

The stout is incompressible and settled; the interior is irrotational; the surface stays close to level (linear theory); the head is a layer of mass that rides on the surface and rubs on the wall; the edge of the stout on the glass adds a constant damping rate; the glass translates with the cart, in a frame moving with the cart’s steady speed, and may tilt by small angles (the cart of section 10 keeps it level). The numbers that decide what matters, for the default pint: the depth times the slowest wave’s wavenumber, \(\lambda D = 6.4\) (deep; \(\lambda\), defined in section 4, is the inverse of the depth over which a wave fades), boundary layer over radius \(\delta/R = 0.011\) (thin), surface tension over gravity \(\sigma\lambda^2/\rho g = 0.010\) (small), damping ratio \(\zeta = 0.024\) (light), the head’s added mass over the sloshing mass \(\kappa = 0.13\) (not small), and the time the cart takes to cross the bump over the slosh period, 1.08 (nearly resonant, section 10) computed.

04Linear sloshing in a straight glass

Laplace’s equation alone has no waves in it. The waves come from the free surface, where gravity pulls a raised patch of stout back down, and the stout below must move to let it.

Boundary conditions

From here on \(z\) is measured upward from the calm surface, so the liquid fills \(-D \le z \le 0\), with \(D\) its depth; heights above the foot keep the letter \(Z\), and the calm surface sits at \(Z_s\). Let the stout surface be raised by \(\eta(x, y, t)\) above its calm level. Three conditions close the problem.

On the wall and the floor the stout cannot pass through the glass: \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\phi = 0\), with \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) the unit vector normal to the glass.

At the surface, kinematics: a parcel on the surface stays on the surface, so the surface rises at the vertical speed of the stout there: \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi\) to first order in the small amplitude.

At the surface, dynamics: the pressure just under the surface equals the air pressure (we come back to surface tension and the head in section 5). Putting \(p = p_\text{air}\) and \(z = \eta\) in (5), dropping the square of the small velocity, and moving everything that depends on time alone into \(\phi\),

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + (g + \ddot z_g)\,\eta + \ddot x_g\,x + \ddot y_g\,y = 0 \quad\text{at } z = 0 . \tag{6} \]

Linearisation means evaluating both surface conditions at the calm level \(z = 0\) rather than at \(z = \eta\). The error is of the order of the wave slope, the height of the wave divided by its horizontal scale; the page warns when the wave at the rim exceeds a fifth of the radius.

Check: a steady push tilts the surface. If the glass accelerates sideways at a constant \(\ddot x_g\), the stout comes to rest relative to the glass, \(\phi\) is constant in time, and (6) gives \(\eta = -(\ddot x_g/g)\,x\): a plane surface, low on the side the glass accelerates toward, tilted at \(\tan\alpha = \ddot x_g/g\). That is the coffee on the braking train.

The modes of a cylinder

First let the glass stand still. Eliminating \(\eta\) between the two surface conditions (differentiate (6) in time and put \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi\)) gives one condition on \(\phi\) alone, \(\partial_t^2\phi + g\,\partial_z\phi = 0\) at \(z = 0\). In a cylinder of radius \(R\), with polar coordinates \(r, \theta\), the solution of Laplace’s equation that satisfies it and the wall and floor conditions is a standing wave

\[ \phi = \phi_0\,J_m(kr)\cos(m\theta)\,\frac{\cosh k(z + D)}{\cosh kD}\,\cos\omega t . \tag{7} \]

Here \(J_m\) is the Bessel function of order \(m\), which behaves like a cosine whose swings weaken as \(r\) grows; it plays the part the sine plays on a guitar string, adapted to a round boundary. The factor \(\cosh k(z + D)\) makes the motion strongest at the surface and gives zero vertical velocity at the floor. No flow through the side wall requires \(J_m'(kR) = 0\), so \(kR\) must be one of the zeros \(\xi_{mn}\) of \(J_m'\): \(\xi_{11} = 1.8412\), \(\xi_{21} = 3.0542\), \(\xi_{01} = 3.8317\), \(\xi_{12} = 5.3314\) (computed with Wolfram Language and with SciPy, which agree to all digits). Putting (7) into the surface condition, where \(\partial_z\) of the cosh factor gives \(k\tanh kD\), fixes the frequency:

\[ \omega_{mn}^2 = g\,\lambda_{mn}, \qquad \lambda_{mn} = k_{mn}\tanh(k_{mn}D), \qquad k_{mn} = \xi_{mn}/R . \tag{8} \]

Where (7) comes from ★

In polar coordinates Laplace’s equation reads \(\partial_r^2\phi + r^{-1}\partial_r\phi + r^{-2}\partial_\theta^2\phi + \partial_z^2\phi = 0\). Try \(\phi = \Phi(r)\cos(m\theta)G(z)\) and divide by \(\phi\): the terms in \(z\) alone and in \(r\) alone must each be constant, say \(G''/G = k^2\) and \(\Phi'' + \Phi'/r + (k^2 - m^2/r^2)\Phi = 0\). The second is Bessel’s equation; of its two solutions only \(J_m(kr)\) stays finite on the axis (the other, \(Y_m\), is infinite there). \(G'' = k^2G\) with \(G'(-D) = 0\) gives the cosh factor. The time factor \(\cos\omega t\) follows from the surface condition.

The number \(m\) counts the nodal diameters, lines across the surface that stay level: \(m = 1\) is the familiar rocking, with one side up while the other is down; \(m = 2\) has two nodal diameters; \(m = 0\) is a ring wave that heaves the middle against the edge. The number \(n\) orders the modes of one \(m\) by frequency; mode \((m, n)\) has \(n - 1\) nodal circles when \(m \ge 1\), and \(n\) when \(m = 0\). Every mode with \(m \ge 1\) comes twice, as a \(\cos m\theta\) and a \(\sin m\theta\) copy, rocking along \(x\) or along \(y\); in a round glass the two have the same frequency. Shove the cart along the road and the 3D view shows the \(\cos\) copy of mode (1,1); shove it sideways and the \(\sin\) copy appears.

Example 4.1 · a straight half-litre glass

A cylinder of radius \(R = 40\) mm filled to \(D = 99.5\) mm holds \(\pi R^2D = 0.500\) L. Then \(k_{11} = 46.03\) m⁻¹ and \(\tanh(k_{11}D) = 0.9998\): the glass is “deep”, since \(\tanh\) has saturated. So \(\omega_{11} = \sqrt{g\,k_{11}\tanh(k_{11}D)} = \sqrt{9.81\times46.02} = 21.25\) rad/s and \(f_{11} = \omega_{11}/2\pi = 3.382\) Hz, confirmed with Wolfram Language. Once \(kD \gt 3\), \(\tanh kD \gt 0.995\) and the depth changes \(\omega\) by less than 0.3 %; in our pint \(\lambda D = 6.4\) computed.

Key result · deep sloshing

In a deep glass the slowest mode rocks at \(\omega_{11} \approx \sqrt{1.84\,g/R}\), set only by the radius of the surface: like a pendulum of length \(R/1.84\), about 21 mm for a pint. A wider glass sloshes more slowly.

05The real glass, in three dimensions

The Guinness glass is not a cylinder: it narrows to a waist, swells, leans inward again near the rim, and carries its two sculpted panels. Separation of variables fails twice over, but the problem keeps its structure, and a computer can solve it on the real shape.

For a body of revolution each mode would still vary as \(\cos m\theta\) around the axis, and a two-dimensional computation in a vertical cut would do. The panels break that: a mode of the real glass is a function \(\Phi(x, y, z)\) of all three coordinates, and nothing but its symmetry under a half turn survives. So we solve the problem in three dimensions. The modes still oscillate as \(\cos\omega t\); what the computer finds, for each fill, are the numbers \(\lambda = \omega^2/g\) and the shapes \(\Phi\) of the modes kept. The method is the next part, and can be skipped.

The eigenvalue problem ★

In the cavity \(\Omega\) below the calm surface \(S\),

\[ \nabla^2\Phi = 0 \ \text{in } \Omega, \qquad \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\Phi = 0 \ \text{on the glass}, \qquad \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z} = \lambda\Phi \ \text{on } S , \tag{9} \]

where the last condition is \(\partial_t^2\phi + g\partial_z\phi = 0\) of section 4 with \(\phi \propto \cos\omega t\). The eigenvalue appears only in a boundary condition; mathematicians call this a Steklov problem. Multiply the equation by any smooth function \(v\), integrate over the cavity and integrate by parts once. The wall and floor terms vanish because \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\Phi = 0\), and the surface term is replaced using its condition:

\[ \int_\Omega\nabla\Phi\cdot\nabla v\,dV = \lambda\int_S\Phi\,v\,dS . \tag{10} \]

This weak form is what the finite-element method solves. The cavity is divided into curved bricks, mapped from a grid in (radius fraction, angle, height) so that the outer bricks follow the real wall, panels included; on each brick \(\Phi\) is a product of three quadratics, fixed by its values at 27 points. Requiring (10) for every such function \(v\) gives a matrix equation \(\mathsf K_h\boldsymbol\Phi = \lambda\,\mathsf M_h\boldsymbol\Phi\), in which \(\mathsf M_h\) has entries only at the surface points. Eliminating the interior points leaves a dense symmetric eigenvalue problem on the surface, about 900 unknowns for 33 000 points in all computed. Each mode’s shape on the surface, \(\psi(s, \theta) = \Phi|_S\), with \(s\) the fraction of the way from the axis (\(s = 0\)) to the wall (\(s = 1\)), is then written as a short Fourier series in the angle, \(\psi = \sum_j[c_j(s)\cos j\theta + d_j(s)\sin j\theta]\), which is exact for a body of revolution (one term) and keeps three terms, \(j = m, m \pm 2\), for the sculpted glass: by their two-fold symmetry the panels can only mix an angular pattern with the ones an even number of steps away, and we keep the nearest two. We scale every mode so that its largest value on the rim is 1: the amplitude of a mode is then the wave height at the rim where that mode is largest, in metres.

Checking the program

A program that has not reproduced a known answer has proved nothing. For a cylinder of 40 mm radius and 100 mm depth the exact eigenvalues are \(\lambda = k\tanh kD\) from (8). Run on the glass for every fill from 300 to 576 mL, the program gives the frequencies of Figure 2. Below the widest point the wall leans outward as it rises; above it, the wall leans inward. The sculpting is real, it is in the glass the page draws and in the cavity the program meshes, and it is also, for the frequencies, irrelevant: a fact worth more than a guess either way.

The head and the surface tension

Two corrections change the surface condition (6). Surface tension makes the surface behave like a stretched drum skin: a curved surface pulls back toward flat with an extra pressure \(-\sigma\nabla_h^2\eta\), where \(\nabla_h^2\) is the Laplacian along the surface. For a mode of a straight glass it multiplies \(\eta\) by \(k^2\), which is \(\lambda^2/\tanh^2 kD\): equal to \(\lambda^2\) within \(10^{-5}\) at our depth, and the page uses \(\lambda^2\) for every mode of the real glass. It raises \(\omega^2\) by the fraction \(\sigma\lambda^2/(\rho g)\): 1.0 % for our pint computed. We let the edge of the surface slide freely on the glass. The head is heavier. Treat it as a layer of mass \(M_f\) per area that is lifted and lowered with the surface beneath it: it adds the inertia \(M_f\partial_t^2\eta\) to the pressure balance at the surface, and nothing to the restoring force, because a foam weighs the same wherever the surface takes it. For a mode, \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi = \lambda\phi\) at the surface, so \(\partial_t^2\eta = \lambda\,\partial_t\phi\), and the surface condition (6) becomes \((\rho + M_f\lambda)\,\partial_t\phi + \rho g\eta = 0\): the head adds \(M_f\lambda\) to the inertia \(\rho\) of the sloshing layer, and

\[ \omega_n^2 = \frac{g\lambda_n + \sigma\lambda_n^3/\rho}{1 + \kappa_n}, \qquad \kappa_n = \frac{M_f\lambda_n}{\rho} . \tag{11} \]

The added-mass ratio \(\kappa_n\) compares the head’s mass with the mass of stout that sloshes, a layer of depth \(1/\lambda\). For the default pint, \(\kappa_{11} = 0.127\), and the head lowers \(f_1\) from 3.432 to 3.233 Hz computed. Opinion: this is the crudest piece of the model. A real head also has a little stiffness of its own, and its liquid fraction is not measured; the slider for \(\varepsilon_f\) lets you see how much it matters.

06Driving the modes: push, tilt, pump

Now let the glass move: it is pushed along, it may tilt, and it is lifted and dropped by the road. The motion enters only through the surface condition, and there it does three different things. The cart of section 10 keeps its tray level, so on this page only the push and the pump are at work; the tilt is derived here because a hand or a car that pitches would bring it in.

The glass turns ★

Section 3 took the glass to translate without turning. A hand, or a car that pitches, also tilts it, by an angle \(\beta\), small and positive when the front goes down. Turning changes the problem in a way the frame-of-the-glass argument does not handle gracefully, because a rotating frame adds forces that are not the gradient of a potential. So we work in the room instead, or rather in the frame that moves with the cart’s steady speed \(v\), which is inertial too: there the glass’s own velocity is small, the stout’s flow is irrotational and has a potential \(\Phi\) (no subscript: the whole potential, as against the modes \(\Phi_n\)) whether or not the glass turns, and we let the boundary move. Fix coordinates \((x', y', z')\) to the glass with their origin at \(O\), the centre of its foot, which the cart carries to \((x_O, z_O)\) in the room. A point \(z'\) up the glass’s axis sits at \(x_O + \beta z'\) in the room when the glass is tilted by \(\beta\): the tilt sweeps the top of the glass sideways.

Split the potential into the part the glass would impose on a liquid held under a lid that turns with it, and the sloshing part: \(\Phi = \dot x_O\,x' + \dot z_O\,z' + \dot\beta\,\chi(x', y', z') + \phi_s\). The first two terms are the translation. The third is the flow of a liquid that turns with its container, and \(\chi\) is fixed by Laplace’s equation inside the cavity with the condition that the liquid moves with the glass at every boundary, \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\chi = \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\hat{\mathbf y}\times\mathbf r')\) on the wall, the floor, and the mean surface too, as if a lid were turning with the glass: the Stokes–Joukowski potential of the cavity, which the finite-element program of section 5 computes alongside the modes. Why a lid: because then the sloshing part \(\phi_s\) alone has to carry the free surface, with \(\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla\phi_s = 0\) on wall and floor, exactly the conditions the modes were built for. A liquid that is swung round does not simply turn with its glass. The reason is equation (3): a rigid turn at the rate \(\dot\beta\) has vorticity \(2\dot\beta\), and a settled pint cannot acquire any, so the stout moves only as much as the walls force it to, like a raw egg spun on a table, whose inside is left behind. \(\chi\) says by how much.

Two things change in the surface conditions. The surface’s height in the room is \(z_O + Z_s + \eta - \beta x'\), so its vertical velocity has the extra term \(-\dot\beta x'\); but \(\partial_z\Phi\) on the surface contains \(\dot\beta\,\partial_z\chi = -\dot\beta x'\) from the lid condition, and the two cancel: the kinematic condition keeps its old form, \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi_s\). In the pressure condition (5), the weight’s potential \(gZ\) is evaluated on the tilted surface, which gives \(-g\beta x'\), and \(\partial_t\Phi\) brings \(\ddot x_O x'\), \(\ddot z_O\eta\) and \(\ddot\beta\chi\). The dynamic condition becomes (before the head and the surface tension of (11) are put back)

\[ \frac{\partial\phi_s}{\partial t} + (g + \ddot z_O)\,\eta = -\left(\ddot x_O - g\beta\right)x' - \ddot\beta\,\chi \quad\text{on } S , \tag{12a} \]

which is (6) with two new terms: the tilt \(g\beta\), which is a sideways gravity in the glass’s own frame, and the turn \(\ddot\beta\chi\), the liquid’s inertia against being swung. (In the room the cart’s speed would add terms such as \(v\,\partial_x\phi_s\) to \(\partial_t\Phi\); they cancel exactly against the same terms in \(\tfrac12|\nabla\Phi|^2\), which is Galilean invariance, and in the moving frame they never appear. What that frame leaves, the products of the glass’s own small velocities, is second order and dropped, as everywhere in this note. Also, \(\dot x_Ox' + \dot z_Oz'\) is the translation along the glass’s tilted axes; in room axes it differs by \(\beta\) times a velocity, second order again.) The head rides on the surface, and when the glass turns it is lifted at \(x'\) by \(-\beta x'\) along with the stout beneath it, so its pressure \(M_f\,\partial_t^2\eta\) of (11) becomes \(M_f(\partial_t^2\eta - \ddot\beta x')\); that is the head’s own inertia in the turn, and it appears below as a correction to \(Q_{rn}\).

One equation per mode

Write the surface displacement as a sum over the modes of section 5, \(\eta = \sum_n a_n(t)\,\psi_n\), with \(\psi_n\) scaled to 1 at its largest point on the rim, so that \(a_n\) is a wave height in metres. The potential is \(\phi_s = \sum_n(\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\Phi_n\), because the kinematic condition requires \(\partial_t\eta = \partial_z\phi_s = \lambda_n\phi_s\) at the surface for each mode. Put both sums into the dynamic condition, multiply by \(\psi_{n'}\) and integrate over the surface. The modes are orthogonal there (a consequence of the symmetry of (10)), so one equation is left for each mode; with the head and the surface tension of (11) and the damping rate \(\gamma_n\) of section 7,

\[ \ddot a_n + 2\gamma_n\dot a_n + \omega_n^2\left(1 + \frac{\ddot z_O}{g}\right)a_n = -\frac{\lambda_n}{1 + \kappa_n}\left[\left(\ddot x_O - g\beta\right)Q_n + \ddot\beta\left(Q_{rn} - \frac{\kappa_n}{\lambda_n}Q_n\right)\right], \qquad Q_n = \frac{\int_S\psi_n\,x'\,dS}{\int_S\psi_n^2\,dS}, \quad Q_{rn} = \frac{\int_S\psi_n\,\chi\,dS}{\int_S\psi_n^2\,dS} . \tag{12} \]

(For clarity the \(\sigma\) term of (11) is not repeated; the page keeps it, and the vertical acceleration does not scale it: \(\ddot z_O\) changes gravity, not surface tension. The \(\kappa_n Q_n/\lambda_n\) in the turn is the head’s inertia from (12a): 2.7 mm against \(Q_{rn}/Q_n = 103\) mm for the slowest mode, a 2.6 % correction computed.) The modes that rock along \(y\) obey the same equation with \(\ddot y_O\) and the roll of the glass in place of \(\ddot x_O\) and the pitch; the cart does not roll, so they are driven by a sideways push alone. The minus sign is the coffee on the train: accelerate the glass toward \(+x\) and the stout rises on the \(-x\) side.

Key result · three ways in

A sideways acceleration drives. The term \(\ddot x_O x\) is a tilt of the effective gravity; it pushes on every mode whose shape leans sideways, which means \(m = 1\) (and, faintly, the modes the panels mix with them), through the coupling \(Q_n\). A tilt drives in the same way. A glass tilted by \(\beta\) feels gravity pointing sideways by \(g\beta\) in its own frame: one degree of tilt is 0.17 m/s², the same as a modest push, and it drives the same modes. A turn also drives, through \(Q_{rn}\), the turning analogue of \(Q_n\): a turn \(\ddot\beta\) pushes mode \(n\) as a sideways acceleration \(\ddot\beta\,Q_{rn}/Q_n\) would, about \(0.10\ \mathrm{m}\times\ddot\beta\) for the slowest mode, as if the push were applied a hand’s breadth below the surface. A vertical acceleration pumps. \(\ddot z_O\) multiplies \(a_n\): it changes the strength of gravity, the spring constant of every mode, without pushing any of them. It can only amplify a slosh that is already there, and only when the shaking beats the damping: at twice the slosh frequency it wins once \(\epsilon\omega/4 \gt \gamma\), where \(\epsilon\) is the shaking acceleration over \(g\); for the default pint that is 0.58 mm of shaking at \(2f_1\), shown in the ★ part below.

Checks. For a straight glass the coupling has a closed form: with the shape \(\psi_{11} = J_1(kr)\cos\theta/J_1(\xi)\) and the Bessel integrals \(\int_0^R r^2J_1(kr)\,dr = R^2J_2(\xi)/k\) and \(\int_0^R rJ_1^2\,dr = \tfrac{R^2}{2}(1 - 1/\xi^2)J_1^2(\xi)\), using \(J_2(\xi) = J_1(\xi)/\xi\) at a zero of \(J_1'\), one finds \(Q = 2R/(\xi^2 - 1)\). For \(R = 40\) mm this is 33.473 mm, and the three-dimensional program reproduces it to six digits computed. A steady push must give the tilted plane of section 4: with \(\ddot a = \dot a = 0\), (12) gives \(a_n = -Q_n\ddot x_O/g\) (surface tension aside; the head cancels, since \(\omega_n^2(1 + \kappa_n) = g\lambda_n\)), and the modes must add up to \(\sum_n Q_n\psi_n = x\). The two \(m = 1\) modes along \(x\) that the page keeps reproduce 90 % of the tilt at the rim; the rest lies in faster modes it leaves out computed. And a check on the turn: if the liquid were frozen, a point on the surface at height \(Z_s\) above the foot would be swung sideways by \(\ddot\beta Z_s\), so \(Q_{rn}\) would equal \(Q_nZ_s\). For a straight glass the lid potential has a closed form, \(\chi = x'z' - \sum_n(2Q_n/k_n)\psi_n f_n(z')\) with \(f_n = 1\) on the lid: it satisfies Laplace’s equation, slides along the wall, and gives \(\partial_z\chi = -x'\) on floor and lid as the rotation requires. Projecting on mode \(n\) gives \(Q_{rn} = Q_n(Z_s - 2/k_n)\): the turn pushes the stout as a sideways push applied \(2/k_n\), about one decay length, below the surface. For the default pint \(Z_s - 2R/\xi = 144.1 - 43.2 = 100.9\) mm, and the three-dimensional program gives \(Q_{r}/Q = 103\) mm for the real glass computed, within 2 %: the stout is swung only 71 % as hard as a frozen block, because the liquid is left behind below the surface.

Resonance and the damping ratio

If the glass were shaken sideways as \(x_O = A\sin\Omega t\) at a mode’s own frequency, at \(\Omega = \omega_n\) the spring and inertia terms of (12) cancel, and only the damping is left to balance the push: \(2\gamma_n\omega_n a_n = \lambda_nQ_nA\omega_n^2/(1 + \kappa_n)\). The damping ratio \(\zeta_n = \gamma_n/\omega_n\), shown on the page as \(\zeta_1\), is the fraction of its amplitude a mode loses per radian of swing (its energy loses twice that fraction). Shake the default pint by \(A = 0.2\) mm at \(f_1 = 3.23\) Hz, an acceleration of only \(A\omega^2 = 0.083\) m/s², and the steady wave at the rim is 5.6 mm, 28 times the shaking computed. On the cart the road does the shaking: cobbles 0.10 m apart at a speed \(v\) hit the wheel \(v/(0.1\,\mathrm m)\) times a second, 15 Hz at walking pace, well above the slosh; but a single bump half a metre long, crossed at walking pace, is one push of about the slosh’s own period (section 10).

Pumping: Faraday’s waves ★

Shake the glass up and down, \(z_O = A\sin\Omega t\), and (12) for one mode becomes \(\ddot a + 2\gamma\dot a + \omega^2(1 - \epsilon\sin\Omega t)\,a = 0\) with \(\epsilon = A\Omega^2/g\): a pendulum whose gravity is modulated, the Mathieu equation. Its strongest instability lies at \(\Omega = 2\omega\), twice the mode’s frequency. To see it, write \(a = b_1(t)\cos\omega t + b_2(t)\sin\omega t\) with slowly varying \(b_1, b_2\) and the usual condition \(\dot b_1\cos\omega t + \dot b_2\sin\omega t = 0\). The equation then gives \(\dot b_1 = -f\sin\omega t/\omega\) and \(\dot b_2 = f\cos\omega t/\omega\), with \(f = -2\gamma\dot a + \epsilon\omega^2\sin(2\omega t)\,a\). Averaged over one period, using \(\langle\sin^2\omega t\cos^2\omega t\rangle = \tfrac18\),

\[ \dot b_1 = -\left(\gamma + \frac{\epsilon\omega}{4}\right)b_1, \qquad \dot b_2 = \left(\frac{\epsilon\omega}{4} - \gamma\right)b_2 . \]

The \(\sin\) part grows when the gain beats the damping:

\[ \frac{\epsilon\,\omega}{4} \gt \gamma \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A \gt \frac{4\zeta g}{\Omega^2} . \tag{13} \]

These standing waves at half the drive frequency are called Faraday waves. For the default pint the threshold is \(A = 0.58\) mm at \(\Omega = 2\omega_1\) computed. On the cart the vertical shaking comes from the road through the suspension: the pumping is there in the equations, with \(\ddot z_O\) taken from the glass’s motion, and section 10 measures what it does: alone it starts nothing, since a wave must already be there to grow, but over a bump it doubles the wave the road’s kick has started, because the body’s heave swings the stout’s gravity by up to 0.78 g while the wave is building. (A linear model gives no seed by itself: the page starts every mode at about a micrometre, standing for the disorder a real surface always has. And when \(g + \ddot z_O\) would fall below \(0.2g\), the page holds it there: the stout would be leaving the floor, and nothing linear describes that.)

The push

Your pointer does not move the cart directly. Dragging in the picture applies a force, proportional to the drag as if you leaned on it through a spring: sideways along the road, capped at 80 N, and downwards on the body, capped at 150 N. The buttons give a shove of 40 N for 0.3 s along the road, or press down on the body with 120 N. A push along the road accelerates the whole cart, 12.4 kg of it, and the stout feels \(\ddot x_O\); a press on the body compresses the spring and loads the tyre, and the stout only feels the change of gravity. Which way in each push uses is something the cut and the trace let you see.

07Where the energy goes

A sloshing pint stops within seconds; an ideal fluid would rock for ever. Each loss below is computed on its own and has its own switch on the page.

The energy method

The energy of a mode is its kinetic energy at the moment the surface passes level, \(E = \tfrac12\rho\int|\nabla\phi|^2\,dV\). If a process dissipates the average power \(\bar P\), the energy decays as \(e^{-2\gamma t}\) and the amplitude as \(e^{-\gamma t}\), with

\[ \gamma = \frac{\bar P}{2E} . \tag{14} \]

Each loss is then a matter of computing \(\bar P\) with the flow of the lossless mode. This is accurate as long as the losses are small, \(\zeta = \gamma/\omega \ll 1\); here \(\zeta\) is 2 to 4 %.

The boundary layers on wall and floor ★

Just outside the Stokes layer the flow slides along the glass with the velocity \(U\cos\omega t\) given by the potential. Inside, at distance \(y_\perp\) from the glass, measured into the liquid, the solution of the diffusion equation that sticks to the glass is \(u = U[\cos\omega t - e^{-y_\perp/\delta}\cos(\omega t - y_\perp/\delta)]\). The power per unit area turned into heat by the rubbing of neighbouring layers is \(\mu\int(\partial u/\partial y_\perp)^2 dy_\perp\). The square of the gradient averages over a period to \((U^2/\delta^2)\,e^{-2y_\perp/\delta}\), and the integral to \(U^2/(2\delta)\), so

\[ \bar P_w = \frac{\rho}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\int_\text{wall + floor}|\nabla_t\phi|^2\,dS , \tag{15} \]

with \(\nabla_t\phi\) the slip velocity amplitude along the glass. Dividing by twice the energy \(E\) of (14),

\[ \gamma_w = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{\nu\omega}{2}}\;\frac{I_w + I_b}{E_\Phi}, \qquad I_w = \int_\text{wall}|\nabla_t\Phi|^2\,dS , \tag{16} \]

with \(I_b\) the same integral over the floor. Here \(E_\Phi = \int|\nabla\Phi|^2\,dV\) is the energy (14) without the factors \(\tfrac12\rho\) and the amplitude, which cancel in the ratio. The program evaluates \(I_w\), \(I_b\) and \(E_\Phi\) for each mode of the real glass, on its real wall.

Check. For the deep cylinder and \(m = 1\), with \(\Phi = J_1(kr)\cos\theta\,e^{kz}\), the integrals are elementary: \(I_w/E_\Phi = (1 + 1/\xi^2)/[R(1 - 1/\xi^2)] = 1.837/R\). The logarithmic decrement, the fall of \(\ln a\) per period, is \(\Delta = 2\pi\gamma/\omega\); with \(\omega = \sqrt{\xi g/R}\) this gives \(\Delta = \pi\cdot1.837\,\nu^{1/2}/(\sqrt2\,\xi^{1/4})\,R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\), that is

\[ \Delta_{11} = 3.50\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4} \quad\text{(clean walls, theory)}, \qquad \Delta_{11} = 4.98\,\nu^{1/2}R^{-3/4}g^{-1/4}\quad\text{(experiments in tanks [6])} . \tag{17} \]

The measured coefficient is 1.42 times the clean-wall theory. That is no contradiction: the abstract of Miles’s paper on damping in closed basins reports that observed decay rates exceed wall damping alone by factors of two to three, and names surface films and the contact line as the causes [5]. Those are the next two terms.

The skin of the surface

Beer is full of proteins that gather at the surface, and under a head the stout touches the underside of a foam. Either way, the surface resists being stretched. If it cannot stretch at all, the surface is a wall for horizontal motion, and its own Stokes layer dissipates by the same formula (15), now integrated over the surface: \(\gamma_s = c_s\,\tfrac12\sqrt{\nu\omega/2}\,I_s/E_\Phi\), with \(I_s\) the integral of (16) taken over the surface instead of the wall, \(c_s = 0\) for a clean surface and \(c_s = 1\) for one that cannot stretch. The page’s default is \(c_s = 1\); opinion: a stout under a head is closer to a skin than to a clean surface. With it, the skin takes about as much as the wall.

The interior ★

Even an irrotational flow dissipates, because its parcels are stretched. The power lost per volume is \(2\mu\sum_{ij}e_{ij}^2\), with the rate of strain \(e_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\phi\). Because \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\), its integral becomes a surface integral, \(\mu\oint\partial_n|\nabla\phi|^2dS\). For a deep mode, \(\phi \propto e^{\lambda z}\), so \(|\nabla\phi|^2\) grows toward the surface as \(e^{2\lambda z}\) and its normal derivative there is \(2\lambda|\nabla\phi|^2\). Dividing the period-averaged loss by \(2E\) gives

\[ \gamma_v = 2\nu\lambda^2 , \]

the damping of waves on deep open water. In a round glass the value for the slowest mode, from the full viscous stress of the mode’s own flow (the same integral the program evaluates, tools/fig12/note_numbers.py), is \(1.88\nu k^2\), 6 % lower computed; on the total damping that is 0.1 %. For the slowest mode \(\gamma_v\) is 0.009 per second, 2 % of the total: negligible there, but it grows as \(\lambda^2\), so short waves feel it most computed.

The contact line

Where the surface meets the glass, the edge of the liquid has to creep up and down the wall. A wetting liquid loses somewhat more energy than viscous theory predicts, and non-wetting combinations lose several times more [7]; the loss depends on the amplitude, rising as the waves get smaller until the edge sticks [7]. No first-principles formula for a stout under a head exists. The page uses a constant rate \(\gamma_c\), default 0.05 per second, at the low end of the 0.05 to 0.25 per second measured in a glass tank of water 60 cm long and 6 cm wide [7]. Opinion: a rate measured in another tank, at another frequency, transfers only as an order of magnitude; and under a head the edge of the stout is hidden in foam, so the head term below may already contain part of this loss. Set \(\gamma_c = 0\) to remove any double count.

The head

Sauret and co-workers measured how a layer of foam damps sloshing in a narrow tank [1]. The foam adds a damping rate that grows with its thickness and saturates once the foam is about as thick as the depth the wave reaches, \(1/k\): they found that the bubbles move less the higher they sit above the liquid, fading over that depth. They traced the loss to the foam’s films sliding on the glass walls, so it scales as the wall area per volume of foam: as \(1/w\) for their tank of width \(w\), with \(w\gamma_f = 1.6 \pm 0.3\) cm/s measured [1]. For a round glass the wall per volume is \(2/R\) instead of \(2/w\), so we take

\[ \gamma_f = c_f\,\frac{u_f}{R}\left(1 - e^{-\lambda h_f}\right), \qquad u_f = 1.6\text{ cm/s} , \tag{18} \]

with the factor \(c_f\), default 1, on a slider. This fits with section 5 as follows. Inference: the head is lifted bodily by the surface beneath it (a foam resists being sheared like a soft solid, so its vertical motion is passed up through it), which is what loads the surface; but it slides against the wall only where the sideways flow beneath drags it, in a layer of depth about \(1/\lambda\), which is what dissipates. The transfer from a slot to a round glass, and from 3 mm bubbles in dish soap to the much finer bubbles of a stout’s head, is the least certain step on this page; it could be off by a factor of three either way.

Key result · how long a pint rocks

With its head, the default pint’s slowest slosh decays as \(e^{-\gamma t}\) with \(\gamma = 0.49\) s⁻¹: its amplitude falls to 1/e in 2.0 s and to 5 % in 6.1 s. Without the head the rate is 0.27 s⁻¹, and the same fall takes 3.7 s and 11.2 s. With the walls and the interior alone, and no head, it would be 9.0 s to 1/e computed. Inference: the walls set the floor of the damping, and everything else in a real pint at least doubles it.

No measured decay time of sloshing in a beer glass was found in the sources searched; the walking-with-coffee study gives a computed, not measured, decrement for a cup [2]. A real test of this note is therefore within anyone’s reach: film a pint after a tap on the glass and count the swings.

08The velocity field in the cut

The figure under the cart is a vertical cut through the glass along the direction in which the stout rocks. The arrows are the velocity \(\mathbf u = \nabla\phi\) of the stout relative to the glass.

Each mode contributes \((\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\nabla\Phi_n\): the arrows are largest when the surface passes level and vanish at the extremes of the swing, when the stout is momentarily at rest and all its energy sits in the raised surface. The program stores each \(\Phi_n\) and its gradient in the same angular Fourier form as the surface shapes, so the page can evaluate the field in any vertical plane through the axis. In the cut, \(\partial_r\Phi\) points along the cut and \(\partial_z\Phi\) up; the component across the cut, \(r^{-1}\partial_\theta\Phi\), is drawn as a ring whose size is its speed. For pure rocking along the cut it is zero; rings appear when modes rock across the cut too, when the cart is pushed sideways, or when \(m \ge 2\) modes are excited.

The fading with depth follows from (7): \(\cosh k(z + D)/\cosh kD \approx e^{kz}\) when \(kD \gg 1\). Laplace’s equation trades variation along the surface for variation with depth: a pattern that changes over a distance \(R\) across the glass must also die out over a distance of order \(R/\xi\) downward. That is why a pint sloshes only in its top two or three centimetres, why the shape of the lower glass hardly changes the frequency, and why the sculpted panels of section 2, ten centimetres down, do not show in Figure 2.

09On the screen, and what is left out

Everything the page draws comes from the equations above, solved in two stages: the modes once, offline, and the whole motion live, while you watch.

The numerical method

Offline, the finite-element program of section 5 solves (9) for 15 fills from 300 to 576 mL and keeps the 14 slowest modes with \(m \le 3\): for the default pint, the pairs (1,1), (2,1), (3,1), (1,2), (2,2), (3,2) along \(x\) and along \(y\), and the ring modes (0,1) and (0,2). The cart’s push along the road drives only the \(m = 1\) modes along \(x\), but the road’s vertical shaking pumps all of them (section 6), and a jolted surface shows the others; keeping them costs little. For each mode it stores \(\lambda_n\), \(N_n = \int\psi_n^2dS\), the couplings \(Q_n\) and \(Q_{rn}\) for \(x\), \(y\) and a tilt, the damping integrals, and the surface shape as its angular Fourier components at 17 radii; for two fills, 500 and 568 mL, also \(\Phi\) and its gradient on a grid of 17 levels in the cut. The page interpolates between fills; for the straight glass it computes the same quantities from Bessel functions.

Live, the page integrates one system for everything that moves: the cart’s three coordinates and the 14 modes together, \(\mathsf M_c\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf C_c\dot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf K_c\mathbf q = \mathbf F\) (section 10) with the road’s kick and the pumping added as forces at every stage, with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method at a fixed step \(\Delta t = 1\) ms, the controller’s forces held between its samples. We chose it because the road makes the forcing, and the vertical acceleration the coefficients, change in time, and the kick is not linear at all, so no exact step formula applies, and because one method for cart and stout keeps the code short. The fastest motion kept, the wheel’s hop at about 30 Hz, has \(\omega\Delta t = 0.18\), well inside the method’s stability limit (\(|\omega\Delta t| \lt 2.8\)), with a phase error per step of order \((\omega\Delta t)^5/120\), about \(2\times10^{-6}\). Measured: the page’s own integrator and an independent one in Python, given the same cart, the same road and the same gains, agree on the peak wave of every run of section 11 to within 0.11 % computed. Each frame runs as many steps as real time has passed, but at most 50 ms of it, so a slow computer slows the clock rather than skipping ahead. The vertical acceleration that pumps the modes is taken from the previous step; one millisecond of lag.

Where the drawing differs from the model

The side view draws the cart and the road to scale and the glass 3.2 times larger, so that a wave of a millimetre can be seen; the stout’s surface in it is \(\eta\) in the cut along the road, at the glass’s own scale. The strut is drawn longer than its working length, which does not enter the physics. Limits of the model

The model is linear: it holds while the wave at the rim is small compared with the radius. The page’s warning at a fifth of the radius is our choice, not a measured threshold, and a generous one: there the steepest slope of the slowest mode’s surface is already about 0.3, and the results are qualitative well before it. The passive cart on the speed bump of section 10 goes far past it; its 19.8 mm “wave” is half the radius, two and a half times the warning, so read it as “the pint is lost”, not as a height. A strongly driven slosh in a round glass can also turn into a swirl, the surface rotating around the glass, an effect of the nonlinear terms that this page leaves out. Inference: we have not opened a source for the swirl here, so take it as a reason to distrust the picture at large amplitude, not as a measured fact. The contact line is treated as a constant damping and the head as a lifted mass with wall friction; the dome the head can hold above the rim is a guess. The cart has one wheel and a level tray; its tyre is a linear spring that may leave the road but not grip it, and its push follows the rigid wheel’s normal. The next model would keep the quadratic terms of the surface conditions, which give the swirl and the dependence of frequency on amplitude, and a tyre with its own contact patch.

10The cart and the road

Push a shopping trolley over a bump and you feel it twice in your hands: it holds back as the wheel climbs, and runs ahead as it rolls down the other side. The bump is not only a lift; it is a brake and then a shove, along the road. This section writes down a cart that feels exactly that, and finds that the shove along the road, not the lift, is what throws the stout.

The machine

The cart is the quarter-car of vehicle dynamics, the textbook model of one corner of a car: a body of mass \(M_b = 10\) kg on one strut, a spring \(k_s = 3000\) N/m and a damper \(c = 60\) N·s/m, standing on one wheel of \(m_w = 1.5\) kg and radius \(R_w = 80\) mm whose tyre is a spring of \(k_t = 50\) kN/m (with a little damping, \(c_t = 20\) N·s/m). Posts carry a tray \(h_g = 0.25\) m above the body’s centre of mass, and the glass, 0.42 kg empty, stands on the tray. The tray is guided so that it stays level: the glass never tilts, and of the three ways in of section 6 only the push and the pump are left. The cart is pulled along the road at a steady speed \(v\) by a drive that resists any change of that speed with a force \(-c_\text{drive}\dot x_c\), \(c_\text{drive} = 200\) N·s/m, as a hand on a rope would; the wheel also has a rolling resistance \(c_x = 5\) N·s/m. All of these are our choice for the page; the sliders change the ones that matter most. The coordinates are the body’s heave \(z_b\), the wheel’s heave \(z_w\), and \(x_c\), how far the cart is ahead of its steady travel; the axle is then at \(X = vt + x_c\) along the road.

The kick

A rigid wheel rolling over a road touches it at one point, and the road can only push; with no friction worth counting at a free-rolling wheel, the push is along the normal to the road at the contact point. Where is that point? The axle rides at a constant distance \(R_w\) from the road, so it moves on the curve swept by the centre of a circle rolled along the profile \(z_r(X)\): the envelope \[ z_a(X) = \max_{|X - x'| \le R_w}\left[z_r(x') + \sqrt{R_w^2 - (X - x')^2}\right] - R_w , \tag{19a} \] measured from the axle’s rest height. On a gentle hump \(z_a\) nearly follows \(z_r\) (on the flanks of the 30 mm bump the axle rides up to 1.4 mm higher, because the rim touches a little up the slope) computed; at a sharp kerb the rim meets the edge before the axle is above it, and the axle climbs on a circle of radius \(R_w\) round the edge. The normal at the contact point passes through the axle, so the push points along the normal of the axle’s path, \((-z_a', 1)/\sqrt{1 + z_a'^2}\). Call its vertical part \(N\), the tyre load. Its horizontal part is then \[ F_\text{kick} = -N\,z_a'(X) , \qquad N = M g + k_t(z_a - z_w) + c_t(\dot z_a - \dot z_w) \ge 0 , \tag{19b} \] with \(M = M_s + m_w\) the whole cart and \(M_s = M_b + m_g + m_l\) the part on the spring. Uphill, \(z_a' \gt 0\), the road pushes back; downhill it pushes forward. A tyre cannot pull, so where (19b) would give a negative \(N\) the wheel has left the road and both pushes are zero; the page counts each lift-off and the readout says so. The tyre is an elastic layer, not a rigid rim; the page takes its push along the rigid wheel’s normal, which is right while the tyre’s compression, a few millimetres, is small against the wheel. Two small masses are left out: the head’s 13.5 g rides with the surface and is counted only in the modes’ inertia, and a wheel that spins adds, for a solid disc, half its mass, 0.75 kg, to the inertia along the road computed.

Check, by energy. Take the drive away and let the cart roll freely over the bump, its weight on the road. Take the rolling resistance away too. Then \(M\ddot X = M\ddot x_c = -Mg\,z_a'\), and \(\tfrac{d}{dt}\tfrac12M\dot X^2 = -Mg\,z_a'\dot X = -Mg\,\tfrac{dz_a}{dt}\): the kinetic energy lost on the way up is exactly \(Mg\) times the height climbed, the roller coaster’s rule. Over the 30 mm bump at 1.5 m/s the cart would slow to \(\sqrt{v^2 - 2gh} = 1.29\) m/s at the top, 0.21 m/s slower (\(gh/v = 0.20\) m/s for a small rise), and get it all back on the way down computed (python3 tools/fig12/check_plant.py integrates the free roll and finds the energy kept to \(6\times10^{-6}\)). The stout does not slow with the glass; for it the glass lurches back and then forward, which is a push \(\ddot x_O\) of (12).

The equations, from a Lagrangian ★

Lagrange’s method needs only the energies. The kinetic energy of the body, the glass and the wheel is \(\tfrac12(M - m_l)\dot x_c^2 + \tfrac12M_s'\dot z_b^2 + \tfrac12m_w\dot z_w^2\), with \(M_s' = M_b + m_g\) (the stout’s own share comes next); the stout’s is \(\tfrac12\rho\int|\nabla\Phi|^2dV\) with the potential of section 6 on a level glass, \(\Phi = \dot x_O x' + \dot z_O z' + \sum_n(\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\Phi_n\). Expanding the square gives \(\tfrac12m_l(\dot x_c^2 + \dot z_b^2)\), the liquid translating; \(\tfrac12(\rho N_n/\lambda_n)(1 + \kappa_n)\dot a_n^2\) for each mode, head included; and the cross term \(\rho\dot x_c(\dot a_n/\lambda_n)\!\int\!\nabla x'\!\cdot\!\nabla\Phi_n\,dV = \rho\,Q_nN_n\,\dot x_c\dot a_n\) by Green’s identity (\(\partial_n\Phi_n = \lambda_n\psi_n\) on the surface, 0 on the glass), with \(N_n = \int_S\psi_n^2dS\). The cross term \(\dot z_b\dot a_n\) vanishes, because \(\int_S\psi_n = 0\) for every wave: lifting the glass does not push any mode, it only changes its gravity, which is the pumping term of (12) and is third order in the Lagrangian. The potential energy holds the spring \(\tfrac12k_s(z_b - z_w)^2\), the tyre \(\tfrac12k_t(z_w - z_a)^2\) and the waves \(\tfrac12\rho gN_n(1 + \sigma\lambda_n^2/\rho g)a_n^2\); a Rayleigh function holds the damper, the tyre’s damping, \(\tfrac12(c_x + c_\text{drive})\dot x_c^2\) and the modes’ \((\rho N_n/\lambda_n)(1 + \kappa_n)\gamma_n\dot a_n^2\). Lagrange’s equations give \[ \mathsf M_c\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf C_c\dot{\mathbf q} + \mathsf K_c\mathbf q = \mathbf F, \qquad \mathbf q = (x_c, z_b, z_w, a_1, \dots, a_{14}) , \tag{20} \] whose matrices are the second derivatives of the three energies. \(\mathbf F\) holds the road, \(k_tz_a + c_t\dot z_a\) on the wheel, the kick (19b) on \(x_c\), the actuators of section 11 and your push. The kick is the product of the load and the slope, two things that both change, so it is not linear and stays out of the matrices; the page adds it as a force at every step, with the pumping term. The row of (20) for a mode, divided by \((\rho N_n/\lambda_n)(1 + \kappa_n)\), is (12) with \(\ddot x_O = \ddot x_c\) and no tilt, which is one check; the others are that the page and an independent Python program, given the same cart and road, agree on every peak of section 11 (section 9).

Key result · the stout pushes back

The coupling \(\rho Q_nN_n\) in \(\mathsf M_c\) works both ways: the cart’s acceleration drives the stout, and the stout’s acceleration \(\ddot a_n\) pushes back on the cart with \(-\rho Q_nN_n\ddot a_n\). The part of the stout that swings rather than rides is the sloshing mass \(m_n = \rho\lambda_nQ_n^2N_n/(1 + \kappa_n)\): 81 g for the slowest mode of the default pint, 2.3 g for the next, out of 504 g of stout computed. On a 12 kg cart that push is small; on a waiter’s tray it is what they feel.

What the cart does by itself

Hold the wheel still and the body is a mass of \(M_s = 10.9\) kg on a spring: it heaves at \(f_z = \sqrt{k_s/M_s}/2\pi = 2.64\) Hz with the damping ratio 0.17; the wheel hops on its tyre at about 30 Hz computed. The drive takes \(M/(c_x + c_\text{drive}) = 0.06\) s to pull a lost speed back computed. Nothing in this list is near the stout’s 3.23 Hz but the heave, and the heave cannot push the stout sideways. What the road can do is shown by two numbers: the bump is half a metre long, so at 1.5 m/s the wheel takes 0.33 s to cross it, and the slowest slosh swings once in 0.31 s computed. Inference: the bump’s brake-then-shove lasts one period of the stout; it is a resonant kick.

The road

Four profiles, each starting 1 m down the road. A speed bump, \(z_r = \tfrac12 h_r[1 - \cos(2\pi X/0.5\,\mathrm m)]\) over half a metre, 30 mm high by default, whose steepest slope is \(\pi h_r/0.5\,\mathrm m = 0.19\); a kerb, a step of 20 mm smoothed over 5 cm, which the 80 mm wheel meets with its rim; cobbles, humps \(h_r\sin^2(\pi X/0.1\,\mathrm m)\) every 10 cm, 6 mm high; and a rough road, a sum of 120 sine waves whose amplitudes follow the ISO 8608 spectrum of a class C road, \(G_d(n) = G_d(n_0)(n/n_0)^{-2}\) with \(G_d(n_0) = 256\times10^{-6}\) m³ at \(n_0 = 0.1\) cycles per metre [16], between 0.05 and 10 cycles per metre, with fixed random phases, faded in over its first 20 cm and scaled by the slider, whose 13 mm default is, to the millimetre, the profile as generated: its largest departure from level in the first 3 m, a dip of 12.7 mm. The page stores the road and the axle’s path (19a) on a 2 mm grid.

Key result · the passive cart spills

Over the 30 mm bump at 1.5 m/s, on its spring and damper alone, the road’s kick reaches 30 N and the stout climbs 19.8 mm at the rim, and the head runs over computed. The highest rise is at the back: as the road brakes the wheel the stout runs forward, 15.5 mm up the front wall, and as the road shoves the cart off the bump it swings back, 19.8 mm up the back wall computed. Split the two ways in: the kick alone, with the pumping switched off, gives 10.6 mm; the pumping alone gives nothing, since vertical shaking cannot start a wave from rest; together they give 19.8 mm, because the kick starts the wave while the body’s heave, up to 0.78 g, swings the stout’s gravity under it computed. Slower or faster the bump is no longer a resonant kick: 4.4 mm at 0.5 m/s, 11.0 mm at 3 m/s computed. Over the kerb the stout climbs 12.9 mm, over the 6 mm cobbles 1.8 mm, on the rough road 5.2 mm computed.

11Keeping the pint level

A waiter who feels the tray lurch does two things without thinking: they keep the tray from bouncing, and they hold it steady against the lurch, so the beer is not jerked. This section gives the cart the same two reflexes: an actuator in the strut, and the drive motor under the controller’s command.

The actuators

The strut gets a moving-coil actuator in parallel with its spring and damper, the loudspeaker’s principle: a coil of wire of length \(\ell\) in a magnetic field \(B\) feels the Lorentz force \(F = B\ell I\) when a current \(I\) flows, so the force is set by the current (Fig. 9 derives this actuator in detail). Its force \(u_s\) pushes up on the body and down on the wheel: it can change how the body and the wheel share the road’s shove, never lift the cart. The drive gets a motor of the same kind, whose extra force \(u_m\) pushes the cart along the road. On the page \(B\ell = 20\) N/A and each current is limited to \(I_\text{max} = 8\) A, 160 N; the static tyre load is 122 N. Every \(T_s = 10\) ms the controller reads the state and sets both currents, which then stay constant until its next look.

Why both? Look at (20) without the kick. The strut’s force appears only in the rows of \(z_b\) and \(z_w\), and nothing couples those rows to the stout: the tray stays level and \(\int_S\psi_n = 0\). Inference: in the linear model the strut cannot touch the slosh at all. It can only act through the kick, by holding the tyre load \(N\) steady, so that the kick is closer to its static part \(-Mg\,z_a'\). That static part is the roller coaster of section 10, and no strut removes it; only a force along the road can. The drive motor can.

Two laws

Skyhook. The oldest idea in active suspension [17]: damp the body as if a damper tied it to the sky, \(u_s = -c_\text{sky}\dot z_b\), \(c_\text{sky} = 300\) N·s/m. A damper to the ground must shake the body whenever the ground shakes; a damper to the sky only ever slows the body down. It needs no model, and it calms the ride, but the motor stays idle and the kick keeps its static part.

LQR. The second law uses the model. It works in the coordinates a suspension controller can measure [15]: the strut deflection \(d = z_b - z_w\), the tyre deflection \(w = z_w - z_a\), the slosh amplitudes \(a_n\) of the two modes along \(x\), (1,1) and (1,2), and the rates \(\dot x_c, \dot z_b, \dot z_w, \dot a_n\): 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 9 numbers in a state \(\mathbf x\). The other twelve modes stay out: in a round glass they have no linear coupling to the cart, and in the sculpted one only a faint one. The cart’s position along the road stays out too: the motor holds the speed, not a timetable, and a position no force is asked to restore would sit on the unit circle of the map below, where a Riccati equation has no stabilising solution. In these coordinates the road enters (20) only as a disturbance, through its rate under the wheel and through the kick, which the regulator does not see. The dynamics are \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathsf A\mathbf x + \mathsf B\mathbf u\) with \(\mathbf u = (u_s, u_m)\), built by evaluating (20) on each unit state in turn. Holding \(\mathbf u\) for one sample turns this into the map \(\mathbf x_{i+1} = \mathsf A_d\mathbf x_i + \mathsf B_d\mathbf u_i\), with \(\mathsf A_d = e^{\mathsf AT_s}\) and \(\mathsf B_d\) the push of one held force, both from one matrix exponential (the project page Balancing Two Rods on a Cart derives this zero-order hold).

The controller sets \(\mathbf u_i = -\mathsf K\mathbf x_i\), with the gains \(\mathsf K\) chosen to make the total cost

\[ \mathcal J = \sum_i\left[\frac{\eta_\text{rim}^2}{\sigma_\eta^2} + \frac{w^2}{\sigma_w^2} + \frac{\dot z_b^2}{\sigma_v^2} + \frac{u_s^2}{\sigma_u^2} + \frac{u_m^2}{\sigma_m^2}\right], \qquad \eta_\text{rim} = \sum_n a_n\psi_n(\text{rim}, 0) , \tag{21} \]

as small as possible, where \(\eta_\text{rim}\) is the rise of the stout at the front of the rim, which is what spills. Each \(\sigma\) with a subscript is a tolerance (not the surface tension): a wave, a tyre deflection, a heave speed and two forces that we are willing to accept. The cost watches the front of the rim only, which is enough because the modes along \(x\) are odd in \(x\). Each penalised quantity is a row of the state, \(\eta_\text{rim} = \mathbf h_\eta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\) and so on, so the cost is \(\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf W\mathbf x + \mathbf u^{\mathsf T}\mathsf W_u\mathbf u\) with \(\mathsf W = \sum\mathbf h\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}/\sigma^2\) and \(\mathsf W_u = \mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_u^2, 1/\sigma_m^2)\); this is the linear–quadratic regulator. Its gains are \(\mathsf K = (\mathsf W_u + \mathsf B_d^{\mathsf T}\mathsf P\mathsf B_d)^{-1}\mathsf B_d^{\mathsf T}\mathsf P\mathsf A_d\), where \(\mathsf P\) solves the discrete Riccati equation \(\mathsf P = \mathsf W + \mathsf A_d^{\mathsf T}\mathsf P(\mathsf A_d - \mathsf B_d\mathsf K)\); the page iterates this equation to its fixed point whenever a slider moves, about 900 iterations from cold, starting from the last solution, which is usually but not always quicker. On the page \(\sigma_\eta = 0.5\) mm, \(\sigma_w = 1\) mm, \(\sigma_v = 50\) mm/s and \(\sigma_u = \sigma_m = 20\) N. The slowest motion of the closed loop then decays with a time constant of 1.12 s computed.

Key result · what the controller buys

Over the 30 mm bump at walking pace the stout rises 19.8 mm at the rim on the passive cart (the head runs over), 13.5 mm with the skyhook damper (it still runs over), and 0.9 mm under the LQR, which keeps the head under its dome limit using at most 50 N in the strut and 24 N in the motor computed. The kick is nearly the same under all three, 26 to 30 N computed: inference, the LQR does not save the pint by softening the kick but by answering it: Figure 6 shows the motor pushing against the kick almost force for force, forward while the road brakes and back while it shoves, so the cart’s speed hardly changes (its largest acceleration falls from 1.42 to 0.67 m/s²) and the glass hardly lurches. That is the waiter’s steady hand. Over the 20 mm kerb: 12.9 mm, 9.9 mm and 2.8 mm, the motor reaching 121 N; on the cobbles the passive cart’s 1.8 mm becomes 0.7 mm; on the rough road 5.2 mm becomes 0.4 mm computed.

How it is checked. The page computes its gains in the browser; an independent design in Python (SciPy’s Riccati solver, on the matrices of plant.py) gives the same gains to \(2\times10^{-9}\) and the same closed-loop time constant, and the page’s own integrator reproduces every peak of the bump, the kerb and the cobbles under all three laws to within 0.11 % computed (node notes/fig12/gate-ctl3.cjs, python3 tools/fig12/gate_ctl3.py).

What the sliders teach

Stiffen the drive: at \(c_\text{drive} = 2000\) N·s/m the passive cart’s 19.8 mm falls to 2.5 mm, because a drive that will not let the speed change takes the kick itself; slacken it to 50 N·s/m and the passive cart climbs 25.1 mm computed. (The slider stops at 50: with no hold at all the speed itself would sit on the unit circle of the LQR’s map, the obstruction that keeps the position out of its state.) Inference: the cheapest cure is a firm hand, and the controller’s motor is a firm hand that pushes only when the road does, instead of fighting every change of speed. Shrink \(\sigma_m\) and the motor is spent more sparingly, and the pint rises again; shrink \(\sigma_w\) to 0.1 mm and the opposite of what one might hope happens: holding the tyre’s deflection small means pressing the wheel hard into every rise, the tyre load swings between 52 and 173 N, the kick grows from 27 to 32 N and the wave from 0.9 to 1.1 mm computed. The strut serves the ride; it cannot serve the stout. Lower the current limit: on the bump the motor asks for 24 N of 160, so the stout hardly notices until the limit falls below that, 1.2 A, although the strut’s 50 N is clipped sooner; on the kerb the motor asks for 121 N. Stretch \(T_s\) to 60 ms and the controller looks only five times per swing of the stout: it still catches it, but late, and the wave grows from 0.9 to 3.6 mm computed. Opinion: the honest limit of this controller is the model it trusts. It assumes it knows the slosh amplitudes exactly; a real one would have to estimate them, for example from a camera looking at the glass. It also has no preview of the road: a cart that sees the bump coming could start giving way before the kick arrives, and do better still.
