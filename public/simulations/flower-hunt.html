Study notes · Fig. 13 · periodic orbits

Hunting the flower pendulum

Numa Koudsie · study notes, 8 November 2026; the full lecture note on Fig. 13 will build on them

Abstract. A double pendulum that hangs still and is kicked once draws a curve with the tip of its lower rod. For most kicks the curve never closes. We show that the tip draws one petal each time the arms fold through, that without gravity a single number, the kick's direction, decides how far each new petal turns, and that a flower closes when that turn is a rational part of a full circle. With gravity we find the closed flowers exactly, as periodic orbits, by Newton's method, and we measure which of them survive a small push. A butterfly turns out to be a pendulum inside the pendulum: the direction of the petals swings back and forth about straight down.

01Where a petal comes from

Put your forearm in line with your upper arm and your hand is as far from your shoulder as it can be. Fold the elbow all the way and your hand comes back to the shoulder. A double pendulum does the same with its tip.

Each rod has length \(l\), the upper rod makes the angle \(\theta_1\) with the downward vertical and the lower rod the angle \(\theta_2\). The tip is the sum of two arrows of length \(l\). Written as a complex number, with the real axis pointing down and the imaginary axis pointing right,

\[ z_{\text{tip}} = l\,e^{i\theta_1} + l\,e^{i\theta_2} = 2l\cos\frac{\Delta}{2}\;e^{i\psi}, \qquad \Delta = \theta_1 - \theta_2,\quad \psi = \frac{\theta_1 + \theta_2}{2}. \tag{1} \]

To see the second form, take \(e^{i\psi}\) out of both terms: what is left is \(l(e^{i\Delta/2} + e^{-i\Delta/2}) = 2l\cos(\Delta/2)\). So the tip lies at the distance \(2l\,|\cos(\Delta/2)|\) from the pivot. When the arms line up (\(\Delta = 0\) or a whole number of turns) the tip is on the rim, \(2l\) away. When they fold (\(\Delta = \pi\)) the tip is at the pivot.

If the angle between the arms keeps turning, the tip runs out to the rim, back through the pivot, out to the rim on the other side, and so on. One full turn of \(\Delta\) draws one petal, a loop through the centre. The tip of that petal lies in the direction of the lower rod at the moment the arms are lined up. Call \(\delta\) the angle the lower rod turns between two such moments. Then the next petal tip is \(\delta\) further round, and the flower closes after \(k\) petals exactly when

\[ \frac{\delta}{2\pi} = \frac{q}{k} \pmod 1, \qquad q, k \text{ whole numbers with no common factor}. \tag{2} \]

Every pendulum on this page starts hanging straight down, \(\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0\). The arms are lined up, so the start is itself a petal tip: every flower here has a petal pointing straight down. The arms fold through only for some kicks. For the others \(\Delta\) rocks back and forth without reaching \(\pi\), the tip never passes the pivot, and the pendulum draws rings instead of petals.

02Without gravity, one number decides

A hard kick makes gravity a small correction. So first switch gravity off.

Without gravity the pendulum is a free, jointed stick spinning about the pivot. Nothing outside twists it, so its total angular momentum \(p_1 + p_2\) stays constant. It also has no length scale in time: if a motion \(\theta(t)\) is possible with the kick \(\mathbf p\), then \(\theta(\lambda t)\) is possible with the kick \(\lambda\mathbf p\), because every term of the kinetic energy scales with \(\lambda^2\). Kick twice as hard and the same curve is drawn twice as fast. So \(\delta\) depends only on the direction of the kick, the angle \(\alpha\) in \(\mathbf p = |\mathbf p|(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)\).

Whether the arms fold through follows from energy alone. At the start, for two equal uniform rods of mass \(m\), the energy is \(E = \tfrac{6}{7ml^2}(p_1^2 - 3p_1p_2 + 4p_2^2)\). To fold, the stick must spin with its arms folded, where its moment of inertia about the pivot is smallest, \(\tfrac23 ml^2\). Spinning with the angular momentum \(p_1+p_2\) then costs \(\tfrac{3(p_1+p_2)^2}{4ml^2}\), the way a skater pulling in her arms spins faster and must supply energy to do so. The arms fold through when the kick provides more than that:

\[ 3p_1^2 - 114\,p_1p_2 + 75\,p_2^2 > 0 . \tag{3} \]

The edge of this region is two straight lines through the origin, \(p_1 = (19 \mp 4\sqrt{21})\,p_2\), which is \(p_1 = 0.6697\,p_2\) and \(p_1 = 37.33\,p_2\). They are the two arms of the X in Fig. 13's chaos map.

We integrate one turn of \(\Delta\) for 1800 kick directions, 0.1° apart, and read off \(\delta(\alpha)\). It is the curve in the bench's chart. Solving (2) on it gives 80 directions for flowers of 2 to 12 petals.

Key result · without gravity

A flower's shape is fixed by the direction of the kick alone. Its size in time is set by the strength of the kick. Closed flowers sit at the directions where \(\delta/2\pi\) is a fraction \(q/k\).

03With gravity: closed orbits

Gravity spoils both symmetries: the time scaling and the freedom to turn the flower. A closed flower must now be found as a whole orbit.

A flower of \(k\) petals is closed if, after \(k\) turns of \(\Delta\), the pendulum is back where it started: hanging straight down, \(\theta_2\) a whole number of turns, with the same momenta. Energy is conserved, so once \(p_1\) matches, \(p_2\) matches too. That leaves two conditions for the two unknowns of the kick:

\[ F(p_1, p_2) = \Bigl( \theta_2(t_k) - 2\pi n,\;\; p_1(t_k) - p_1(0) \Bigr) = (0,\,0), \tag{4} \]

where \(t_k\) is the moment of the \(k\)-th alignment. We solve (4) by Newton's method, starting from the gravity-free flower at a kick strength of 18 or 30 kg·m²/s, and integrate with an eighth-order Runge–Kutta method (DOP853) at a relative tolerance of \(10^{-11}\). Every orbit in the garden closes to better than \(10^{-9}\).

Example 3.1 · the seven-petal daisy

The kick \(\mathbf p = (-28.032,\ 0.476)\) kg·m²/s gives the energy \(E = 689.0\) J, 17.6 times the 39.2 J it takes to lift both rods straight up. The tip draws seven petals and returns in \(T = 0.794\) s; after seven petals the state differs from the start by \(1.8\times10^{-11}\).

A closed orbit is useful only if it survives a small push. Watch the moments when the arms line up: the state there is \((\theta_2, p_1)\), and one flower is a map of that plane onto itself. Its Jacobian \(J\) has determinant 1, because Hamiltonian motion keeps phase-space area. If \(|\operatorname{tr} J| < 2\), nearby kicks circle the flower and draw nearly the same picture: the orbit is stable. If \(|\operatorname{tr} J| > 2\), nearby kicks run away from it and the picture smears within a few periods: unstable. Within 0.01 of 2 our finite differences cannot tell, and we say neutral. This is common at high energy, where gravity is weak and almost a whole family of kicks nearly closes. In the garden the 3-petal flower at \(\mathbf p = (24.42,\ 0.00)\) has \(\operatorname{tr} J = 1.96\): stable. The 7-petal flower at \((7.51,\ 12.34)\) has \(\operatorname{tr} J = -14.8\): it is there on purpose, so you can watch an unstable flower fall apart.

04The butterfly

A butterfly is a flower that cannot decide where to point.

Take the kick \(\mathbf p = (3.556,\ 10)\), a cell of the video's grid. Its petal tips point, in order, 57°, 97°, 119°, 126°, 121°, 104°, 74°, 33°, −14°, … from straight down. Each petal lands close to the previous one: the lower rod turns by almost a whole turn between petals, so \(\delta/2\pi\) is close to 0, the one-petal resonance. Without gravity the petals would creep round the circle at a steady rate. Gravity pulls the petal direction towards straight down, so it swings about the bottom like a pendulum, out to \(\pm126°\) and back. A pendulum is slowest at its turning points, so the petals pile up there: two dense fans, left and right. Those are the wings.

Along the line \(p_1 = 3.556\) every kick from \(p_2 = 7.0\) to \(17.9\) is caught in this swing (89 of 161 kicks, 0.1 apart). The wings open between about \(\pm123°\) and \(\pm165°\), widest for the hardest kicks, and one swing takes between 10 and 45 petals. When one swing takes a whole number \(N\) of petals, the butterfly closes. It is then a periodic orbit with \(N\) turns of \(\Delta\), and (4) finds it.

Example 4.1 · the 24-petal butterfly

\(\mathbf p = (3.557,\ 12.211)\) kg·m²/s, \(E = 390.8\) J. The petal direction swings out to \(\pm135.6°\), the tip draws 24 petals and returns in \(T = 2.659\) s; the state closes to \(9\times10^{-12}\). Its trace is 2.000: neutral.

The butterflies with 12, 14 and 16 petals have traces of 2.39, 2.17 and 2.04: unstable. This is expected. Resonant closed orbits come in pairs, one stable and one unstable (the Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem), and Newton's method found the unstable member of each of these three pairs. Their stable partners are not yet computed.

05How we know

The model is the compound double pendulum of two equal uniform rods, \(m = 1\) kg, \(l = 1\) m, \(g = 9.8\) m/s². For two equal rods its equations reduce exactly to 2swap's and to Heyl's (checked with SymPy); the energy, the fold-through lines (3) and the small-swing frequencies were checked again with Wolfram. The search runs in tools/fig13/hunt.py, the stability in hunt_inspect.py, the butterflies in butterfly.py. The fold-through edges found by integration, 0.0262 and 0.9791 rad, agree with (3), 0.0268 and 0.9807 rad, to one scan step. The page itself integrates with its own fourth-order Runge–Kutta at \(h = 1/9000\) s on the bench and \(1/4500\) s in the garden. On the bench it measures \(\delta/2\pi\) for the 7-petal flower at \((-30.04,\ 2.52)\) within \(4\times10^{-5}\) of \(4/7\) over 64 petals: the browser confirms the closure the offline search found.

What is not shown yet: the stable partners of the three unstable butterflies, flowers of 6 and of 9 to 12 petals with gravity (Newton's method did not converge from our starting kicks), and the same hunt for point masses and unequal rods.
