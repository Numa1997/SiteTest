Lecture note · Fig. 2 · classical mechanics

The double pendulum: exact laws, unpredictable motion

Numa Koudsie · for school students and first- and second-year undergraduates

Abstract. A pendulum hung from the end of another pendulum is the simplest machine whose future we can write down exactly and still cannot predict. This note builds its equations of motion from energy alone, with Lagrange’s method; finds the two gentle ways it can swing when the angles are small; shows, with numbers from the simulation above, how two almost identical starts part ways; and explains how the page solves the equations, step by step, sixty times a second. Sections marked ★ carry the heavier mathematics and can be skipped on a first reading without losing the thread.

01A pendulum on a pendulum

Let a pen swing loosely between two fingers: that is a pendulum, and since Galileo we have known how to predict it. Now hang a second pen from the tip of the first.

You carry a double pendulum with you every day. Let your arm hang and swing it from the shoulder: the upper arm swings about the shoulder, the forearm about the elbow, and the hand follows a path that neither joint alone would draw. A gymnast turning on the high bar, a nunchaku, a crane hook hanging from its cable are all the same machine: two masses, two rods, one gravitational field.

Released from a small angle, the double pendulum rocks gently and regularly, the two arms passing energy back and forth like two children on linked swings. Released from high up, the lower arm whips over the top, stops, reverses, and never settles into a pattern. Nothing random is involved. The equations we are about to derive fix the entire future from the starting state, and the computer solves them so accurately that the energy stays fixed to better than one part in ten million. Yet two starts that differ by a thousandth of a radian, one millimetre at the end of a one-metre rod, end up in unrelated places within five seconds.

This pairing of exact laws with practically unpredictable outcomes is called deterministic chaos. It is the same mechanism that limits weather forecasts to about two weeks: the atmosphere obeys known equations, but we can never measure today’s state precisely enough.

02The model

Physics begins by deciding what to leave out. We keep two point masses on rigid rods that weigh nothing, swinging in one vertical plane, with no friction and no air. Put the pivot at the origin with \(y\) pointing up. Reading the positions off Figure 1 with a little trigonometry,

\[ \begin{aligned} x_1 &= l_1\sin\theta_1, & y_1 &= -l_1\cos\theta_1, \\ x_2 &= x_1 + l_2\sin\theta_2, & y_2 &= y_1 - l_2\cos\theta_2 . \end{aligned} \tag{1} \]

Two angles fix the shape completely, so the system has two degrees of freedom. To know where it goes next we also need how fast each angle is changing, \(\omega_i = \mathrm{d}\theta_i/\mathrm{d}t\). The four numbers \((\theta_1, \theta_2, \omega_1, \omega_2)\) are the state: give them to the equations and the whole future follows. On the page the upper mass and rod are fixed at \(m_1 = 1\ \mathrm{kg}\), \(l_1 = 1\ \mathrm{m}\), with \(g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\); the sliders set the ratios \(m_2/m_1\) and \(l_2/l_1\).

03Energy

Instead of chasing forces, we will describe the pendulum by its energy. Differentiate the positions (1) with respect to time, remembering that \(\theta_i\) changes and \(l_i\) does not:

\[ \begin{aligned} \dot x_1 &= l_1\omega_1\cos\theta_1, & \dot y_1 &= l_1\omega_1\sin\theta_1, \\ \dot x_2 &= \dot x_1 + l_2\omega_2\cos\theta_2, & \dot y_2 &= \dot y_1 + l_2\omega_2\sin\theta_2 . \end{aligned} \tag{2} \]

The kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12 m v^2\) for each mass. Squaring and adding the components, the mixed products combine through \(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 = \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\), and the potential energy is \(mgy\) for each mass:

\[ T = \tfrac12 m_1 l_1^2\omega_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\left[\, l_1^2\omega_1^2 + l_2^2\omega_2^2 + 2\,l_1 l_2\,\omega_1\omega_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) \right], \tag{3} \] \[ V = -(m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2\,g\,l_2\cos\theta_2 . \tag{4} \]

The last term in (3) carries the whole story. The lower mass rides on the upper one, the way a passenger walking down the aisle rides on a train. When the two rods point the same way and turn the same way, \(\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) = 1\) and the speeds simply add, \(v_2 = l_1\omega_1 + l_2\omega_2\). When the rods are at right angles, the cosine vanishes and the two speeds add like the sides of a right triangle. Because the rods' directions keep changing, the way the two motions combine keeps changing too: each rod is forever pushing and pulling on the other.

Energy is conserved

Nothing in \(T\) or \(V\) depends on the clock time itself, and there is no friction, so the total \(E = T + V\) never changes. The pendulum can only trade height for speed and back. This gives the simulation a built-in check: the Drift readout above shows how far the computed energy wanders from its starting value.

04From energy to motion★

With Newton’s laws we would need the tension in each rod, two unknown forces that change direction and size at every instant. Lagrange found a way around them. Form the Lagrangian \(L = T - V\). Among all the ways the angles could get from a given start to a given end, the real motion is the one that makes the action \(S = \int L\,\mathrm{d}t\) stationary (Hamilton’s principle). The rod forces never appear, because they act along the rods and do no work on any motion the rods allow.

4.1The Euler–Lagrange equation

Change one angle by a small amount \(\delta\theta_i(t)\) that vanishes at the start and at the end. The action changes by \(\delta S = \int \left[ \partial_{\theta_i}L\,\delta\theta_i + \partial_{\omega_i}L\,\delta\omega_i \right]\mathrm{d}t\), with \(\delta\omega_i = \mathrm{d}(\delta\theta_i)/\mathrm{d}t\). Integrating the second term by parts moves the time derivative across; the boundary term vanishes because \(\delta\theta_i\) does:

\[ \delta S = \int \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_i} - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\omega_i} \right] \delta\theta_i\,\mathrm{d}t = 0 \quad\text{for every } \delta\theta_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\omega_i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_i} = 0 . \tag{5} \]

The quantity \(p_i = \partial L/\partial\omega_i\) is the generalised momentum belonging to \(\theta_i\). For the upper angle it is \(p_1 = (m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2\,\omega_1 + m_2 l_1 l_2\,\omega_2\cos\delta\), with \(\delta = \theta_1 - \theta_2\): an angular momentum that already contains the lower mass, not simply \(m_1 l_1^2\omega_1\).

4.2The upper angle

Two kinds of derivative meet in (5). A partial derivative such as \(\partial L/\partial\omega_1\) treats \(L\) as a function of four independent numbers and varies one of them alone. The time derivative then follows the actual motion, in which all four change together; by the chain rule \(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cos\delta = -\sin\delta\,(\omega_1-\omega_2)\). Writing \(\alpha_i = \mathrm{d}\omega_i/\mathrm{d}t\) for the angular accelerations,

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial L}{\partial\omega_1} &= (m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2\,\omega_1 + m_2 l_1 l_2\,\omega_2\cos\delta, \\ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\omega_1} &= (m_1+m_2)\,l_1^2\,\alpha_1 + m_2 l_1 l_2\,\alpha_2\cos\delta - m_2 l_1 l_2\,\omega_2(\omega_1-\omega_2)\sin\delta, \\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_1} &= -m_2 l_1 l_2\,\omega_1\omega_2\sin\delta - (m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1\sin\theta_1 . \end{aligned} \]

In the difference the two \(\omega_1\omega_2\) terms cancel. Dividing by \(l_1\):

\[ (m_1+m_2)\,l_1\,\alpha_1 + m_2 l_2\,\alpha_2\cos\delta = -m_2 l_2\,\omega_2^2\sin\delta - (m_1+m_2)\,g\sin\theta_1 . \tag{6} \]

4.3The lower angle

The same three steps for \(\theta_2\), with \(\partial L/\partial\omega_2 = m_2 l_2^2\,\omega_2 + m_2 l_1 l_2\,\omega_1\cos\delta\), again cancel the \(\omega_1\omega_2\) terms; dividing by \(m_2 l_2\):

\[ l_1\,\alpha_1\cos\delta + l_2\,\alpha_2 = l_1\,\omega_1^2\sin\delta - g\sin\theta_2 . \tag{7} \]

4.4Solving for the accelerations

Each equation contains both accelerations, because each rod pushes on the other. A computer stepping forward in time needs each acceleration on its own, so we solve the linear system

\[ \begin{pmatrix} (m_1+m_2)\,l_1 & m_2 l_2\cos\delta \\ l_1\cos\delta & l_2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_1 \\ \alpha_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -m_2 l_2\,\omega_2^2\sin\delta - (m_1+m_2)\,g\sin\theta_1 \\ l_1\,\omega_1^2\sin\delta - g\sin\theta_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = l_1 l_2\,(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\delta) > 0 . \tag{8} \]

The determinant can never vanish, so there is always exactly one answer. Cramer’s rule, then \(2\sin^2\delta = 1-\cos 2\delta\) and \(2\sin\theta_2\cos\delta = \sin\theta_1 - \sin(\theta_1 - 2\theta_2)\), bring it to the form the page computes:

\[ \begin{aligned} \alpha_1 &= \frac{-g\,(2m_1+m_2)\sin\theta_1 - m_2\,g\sin(\theta_1-2\theta_2) - 2\,m_2\sin\delta\,\left(\omega_2^2\,l_2 + \omega_1^2\,l_1\cos\delta\right)}{l_1\,(2m_1+m_2-m_2\cos 2\delta)}, \\[4pt] \alpha_2 &= \frac{2\sin\delta\,\left(\omega_1^2\,l_1\,(m_1+m_2) + g\,(m_1+m_2)\cos\theta_1 + \omega_2^2\,l_2\,m_2\cos\delta\right)}{l_2\,(2m_1+m_2-m_2\cos 2\delta)} . \end{aligned} \tag{9} \]

Checked against a direct numerical solve of (8) at 10 000 random states and parameter values: worst relative difference 8 × 10⁻¹⁴, which is rounding error.

05Small swings: two normal modes

Before facing the full equations, look at the gentle case. For small angles, \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\), \(\cos\delta\approx 1\), and the \(\omega^2\) terms are too small to matter. With equal masses and equal lengths \(l\), equations (6) and (7) become linear:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \ddot\theta_1 \\ \ddot\theta_2 \end{pmatrix} = -\frac{g}{l} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \theta_1 \\ \theta_2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10} \]

Linear equations like these have special solutions in which both angles swing at one common frequency \(\omega\), \(\theta_i = u_i\cos\omega t\). Substituting gives a solution only if the determinant of the combined matrix vanishes, \(2\,(g/l - \omega^2)^2 = \omega^4\), so

\[ \omega_\mp^2 = \left(2\mp\sqrt2\right)\frac{g}{l}, \qquad \omega_- = 2.40\ \mathrm{rad/s},\quad \omega_+ = 5.79\ \mathrm{rad/s} \quad (l = 1\ \mathrm{m}). \tag{11} \]

The first row of (10) then fixes the shape of each motion, \(u_2/u_1 = 2\,(g/l-\omega^2)/\omega^2\). For the slow mode this is \(+\sqrt2\): both arms swing together, the lower one about 1.4 times as far. For the fast mode it is \(-\sqrt2\): the arms swing against each other (Figure 2). These are the normal modes, and any small motion is a mixture of the two. Because \(\omega_+/\omega_-\) is irrational, the mixture never repeats exactly, but it stays perfectly regular.

Exercise 5.1 · on the figure above

Keep both ratio sliders at 1 and both angular velocities at 0. Set \(\theta_1 = 5^\circ\) and \(\theta_2 = 7^\circ\), close to \(\sqrt2\times 5^\circ\): the pendulum swings almost as one piece, with a period near 2.6 s. Now set \(\theta_2 = -7^\circ\): the arms swing against each other, twice as fast as before and a little more. Time ten swings of each and compare with (11).

06Chaos

Raise the energy and the small-angle picture breaks down: the \(\sin\) and \(\omega^2\) terms we dropped now dominate, and the motion becomes chaotic. The clearest sign is sensitive dependence on the starting state. Start two pendulums whose upper angles differ by \(0.001\ \mathrm{rad}\) and follow the distance \(d(t)\) between their lower masses. Figure 3 shows it for two starts, computed with the same equations and the same time step as the page.

A straight line on a logarithmic scale means \(d(t) \approx d_0\,e^{\lambda t}\). For the high-energy start of Figure 3 the line rises at about \(1.7\) per second, so the distance doubles roughly every \(0.4\ \mathrm{s}\) and reaches \(0.1\ \mathrm{m}\) after \(4.1\ \mathrm{s}\). The long-time average of this growth rate is the largest Lyapunov exponent,

\[ \lambda = \lim_{t\to\infty}\ \lim_{d_0\to 0}\ \frac{1}{t}\,\ln\frac{d(t)}{d_0} , \tag{12} \]

and the 1.7 per second read off one pair of runs is only an estimate of it. Its meaning is practical. Every extra second we want to predict costs a factor \(e^{1.7}\approx 5.5\) in the precision of the starting angles: to see ten seconds further, we would need to know the start about \(5.5^{10}\approx 2\times10^{7}\) times more precisely. Note what chaos is not: it is not noise, and it is not a failure of energy conservation. The energy stays fixed while the trajectory wanders unpredictably over everything that energy allows.

07Solving it step by step

Equations (9) give the accelerations for any state, but no formula gives the angles at a later time. The computer therefore walks forward in small steps. Write the state as \(s = (\theta_1,\theta_2,\omega_1,\omega_2)\) and the equations as \(\mathrm{d}s/\mathrm{d}t = F(s) = (\omega_1,\omega_2,\alpha_1,\alpha_2)\). The fourth-order Runge–Kutta method looks at the slope four times per step, at the start, twice in the middle and once at the end, and takes a weighted average:

\[ \begin{aligned} k_1 &= F(s), & k_2 &= F\!\left(s + \tfrac{h}{2}k_1\right), & k_3 &= F\!\left(s + \tfrac{h}{2}k_2\right), & k_4 &= F(s + h\,k_3), \\ & & s(t+h) &= s + \tfrac{h}{6}\left(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\right). \end{aligned} \tag{13} \]

The error made in one step shrinks like \(h^5\) and the error accumulated over a fixed time like \(h^4\): halving the step makes the result sixteen times more accurate. The page uses \(h = 1/600\ \mathrm{s}\), ten steps per frame at sixty frames a second.

  • Each frame advances by the real time elapsed multiplied by the Time scale, in steps of \(h\), at most 600 steps per frame.
  • RK4 does not conserve energy exactly, which is why the Drift readout is there. It measures \(|E - E_0|\) against the depth of the potential well, \(E_\text{scale} = (m_1+m_2)\,g\,l_1 + m_2\,g\,l_2\), rather than against \(E_0\), which can be close to zero. Over the first 20 s of the default start it stays below \(3\times10^{-8}\).
  • Moving a mass, length, angle or angular-velocity slider restarts the motion from those values; the Time scale slider does not.

08From metres to pixels

The pivot sits at the centre of the figure, and the scale is chosen so that the fully stretched pendulum just fits: \(\text{pixels per metre} = (\min(W, H)/2 - 34)/(l_1 + l_2)\). Screen \(y\) points down, so the masses are drawn at \((x_0 + l\sin\theta,\ y_0 + l\cos\theta)\). The protractor marks \(\theta\) every \(30^\circ\) from the downward vertical.

The burgundy trail of the lower mass is drawn on its own transparent layer, one segment per RK4 step, and dimmed every frame by a factor \((1-0.008)^{60\,\Delta t}\). Its brightness therefore halves every \(\ln 2/(60\times 0.00803)\approx 1.4\ \mathrm{s}\). Each mass is a disc with radius proportional to \(m^{1/3}\), as for balls of the same material.

09What the model leaves out

  • Real rods have mass and a moment of inertia. A double pendulum made of two bars obeys equations of the same kind, with different coefficients.
  • There is no friction. A real pendulum slowly loses energy, and its chaos eventually calms into small, regular swings.
  • The masses may pass through each other and through the pivot; real rods would collide.
  • After a few Lyapunov times any computed trajectory is a faithful example of how the pendulum can move, not the exact path from that start. Averages over many swings remain correct.
