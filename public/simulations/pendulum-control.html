Lecture note · Fig. 11 · control systems

A double pendulum on a leash: holding a chain still by pushing only a cart

Numa Koudsie · for school students and first- and second-year undergraduates

Abstract. Our aim is to bring a double pendulum back to rest, at a place we choose, by pushing nothing but a cart. The pendulum hangs from a fixed pivot. A strut made of two sliding tubes, with a spring inside, ties its lower ball to a cart, and a moving coil pushes the cart along a rail. The coil is the only thing anyone can push, yet it must bring three bodies to rest at a place we choose. We count the degrees of freedom, write the Lagrangian, and find that the strut must be under tension: to first order its spring stiffness drops out, and only the pull it carries couples the cart to the chain, like a guitar string. We find the family of rests the coil can hold, linearise around one of them, test whether a single push can reach every motion, and design the push by the linear–quadratic regulator. Then we look at the result in the six-dimensional phase space: a stable focus, a Lyapunov function that comes with the design, and the edge of the region the controller can rescue. The page solves the whole run first and stores it, and the note says how good that stored run is. Sections marked ★ carry the heavier mathematics and can be skipped on a first reading.

01A chain on a leash

Hold a heavy lamp that hangs from the ceiling on two links, not by the lamp itself but by a rubber cord tied to its bottom. Pull the cord sideways and the whole chain leans; let it go slack and the lamp swings as it likes.

A crane driver knows this problem from the cab. The hook hangs from the trolley, the load hangs from the hook, and the only thing the driver commands is the motor that moves the trolley. Every swing of the load has to be taken out through that one motor, by moving the trolley at the right moments. A dog on a lead, a kite on its line, a boat at anchor in a current: in each a body has its own motion, and we act on it only through something elastic and from one side.

Fig. 11 is the cleanest version of this situation that can be built. A double pendulum hangs from a fixed pivot and swings freely, exactly as in Fig. 2. Below it a cart rolls on a smooth rail. A strut joins the lower ball to the cart. It is made of two tubes, one sliding inside the other, with a spring inside them, so it can change length and pulls or pushes only along its own line. The cart carries the coil of Fig. 9, which sits in the gap of a long magnet and is pushed along the rail with the force \(F = \kappa I\) when a current \(I\) flows.

Nothing else touches the chain. The rods turn without friction, the floor is smooth, the tubes slide without friction. Left alone, the rig swings for ever. You can test that with your hand: switch the controller off, grab a ball or the cart, pull it anywhere, let go.

The aim of this page

The cart, and only the cart, brings the pendulum back to equilibrium. The controller measures where both balls and the cart are and how fast they move, sets the current in the coil, and so pushes the cart; through the strut the cart pulls the chain until all three bodies rest at the position you chose. Every section below serves this aim: the model says what the cart can do to the chain (§3–§6), the test of §7 says whether one push can reach every motion, §8 designs the push, and §9 shows the chain coming home in phase space.

02The rig and its coordinates

Count first. Three bodies move in a plane: two balls, which we treat as points, and the cart. A free point in a plane needs two numbers, so three free bodies would need six. The rigid rods remove one each, \(|\mathbf r_1| = l_1\) and \(|\mathbf r_2 - \mathbf r_1| = l_2\), and the rail removes one more, since the cart's height is fixed. These three are holonomic constraints, rules about positions alone, and each can push as well as pull. Six minus three leaves three degrees of freedom.

The strut is not a constraint. Its tubes are so light that we take them as massless, and a massless bar hinged at both ends can only push or pull along its own line: any sideways force on it would give an infinite angular acceleration. Such a bar is a two-force member. Its length is set by where ball 2 and the cart are, so it brings no coordinate of its own, and its spring enters through a potential energy. The tubes do impose two unilateral constraints, inequalities that act only when they are reached: the strut is never shorter than \(\ell_\text{min} = 0.5\ \mathrm{m}\) (inner tube pushed all the way in) and never longer than \(\ell_\text{max} = 0.95\ \mathrm{m}\) (its collar against the stop). Section 4 says how the page treats them.

We choose three generalised coordinates that fix the whole picture: the two absolute angles and the cart's position,

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf q = (\theta_1, \theta_2, X), \qquad \mathbf r_1 = l_1(\sin\theta_1, -\cos\theta_1), \\ \mathbf r_2 = \mathbf r_1 + l_2(\sin\theta_2, -\cos\theta_2), \qquad \ell = \left|\mathbf r_2 - (X, -H)\right| . \end{gathered} \tag{1} \]

On the page both balls weigh \(m_1 = m_2 = 0.5\ \mathrm{kg}\), both rods are \(l_1 = l_2 = 0.5\ \mathrm{m}\), the cart with its coil weighs \(M = 1\ \mathrm{kg}\), and its hinge runs \(H = 1.65\ \mathrm{m}\) below the pivot. When the chain hangs straight down over the cart, the strut has the length \(\ell_0 = H - l_1 - l_2 = 0.65\ \mathrm{m}\), comfortably between its stops.

03Lagrangian and equations of motion★

Newton's laws would make us find the tension in each rod, forces we do not care about. Lagrange's method uses the coordinates (1) and lets the constraints do their work silently. We need the kinetic and potential energies in terms of \(\mathbf q\) and \(\dot{\mathbf q}\). Differentiating (1), the velocity of ball 2 is the velocity of ball 1 plus a turn of the lower rod, and the cross term between them carries the cosine of the angle between the rods:

\[ T = \tfrac12 (m_1 + m_2)\, l_1^2 \dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2 l_2^2 \dot\theta_2^2 + m_2 l_1 l_2 \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\, \dot\theta_1 \dot\theta_2 + \tfrac12 M \dot X^2 . \tag{2} \]

The potential energy has gravity, with height measured upwards from the pivot, and the spring inside the tubes, of stiffness \(k_s\) and rest length \(L_0\):

\[ V = -(m_1 + m_2)\, g\, l_1 \cos\theta_1 - m_2\, g\, l_2 \cos\theta_2 + \tfrac12 k_s \left(\ell - L_0\right)^2 . \tag{3} \]

The coil adds the electromechanical terms we met in Fig. 9: with \(q_e\) the charge that has flowed through the winding, \(\dot q_e = I\), and \(\Lambda\) its inductance,

\[ \mathcal L = T - V + \tfrac12 \Lambda \dot q_e^2 + \kappa X \dot q_e , \tag{4} \]

where \(\kappa = B\ell_w = 10\ \mathrm{N/A}\) is the force constant: the field in the magnet's gap times the length \(\ell_w\) of wire in it. The term \(\kappa X \dot q_e\) is the whole coupling between the winding and the field. Its derivative with respect to \(X\), \(\kappa\dot q_e = \kappa I\), is the force on the cart; its time derivative of \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_e\) gives the back-emf \(\kappa\dot X\) in the circuit. The page drives the coil from a current source, which delivers the current it is told whatever the back-emf, so the electrical equation drops out and \(F = \kappa I\) becomes the input. The Euler–Lagrange equations \(\frac{d}{dt}\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i = \partial\mathcal L/\partial q_i\) then read, in matrix form,

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf M(\mathbf q)\,\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathbf C(\mathbf q, \dot{\mathbf q})\,\dot{\mathbf q} + \nabla V = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ F \end{pmatrix}, \\ \mathbf M = \begin{pmatrix} (m_1+m_2) l_1^2 & m_2 l_1 l_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) & 0 \\ m_2 l_1 l_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) & m_2 l_2^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & M \end{pmatrix} . \end{gathered} \tag{5} \]

The mass matrix \(\mathbf M\) is block diagonal: the cart's inertia does not mix with the pendulum's. The term \(\mathbf C\dot{\mathbf q}\) holds the centrifugal pulls, \(m_2 l_1 l_2 \sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\dot\theta_2^2\) on the upper rod and its mirror on the lower. Everything that ties the cart to the chain sits in \(\nabla V\), through the strut. The derivative of its spring energy is

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial q_i}\, \tfrac12 k_s (\ell - L_0)^2 = N\, \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial q_i}, \qquad N = k_s(\ell - L_0) , \tag{6} \]

the pull \(N\) of the strut times the rate at which each coordinate stretches it. On the cart this is \(N\,\partial\ell/\partial X = -N (x_2 - X)/\ell\): the strut's pull, projected onto the rail. That one term, and its partners in the two angle equations, are all the strut does. The code solves (5) for \(\ddot{\mathbf q}\) in accel(): the \(2\times2\) pendulum block by Cramer's rule, the cart by one division. As a check, the same accelerations come out of Newton's laws with the two rod tensions as unknowns; at a random state with the cart pushed, the two routes agree to 13 significant figures.

Key result · the equations of motion

Three coordinates, one input. The coil pushes only the cart, \(Q_X = F = \kappa I\); the strut couples cart and chain through its pull \(N\) and nothing else.

04The strut: two tubes and a spring

Picture the strut without its spring: two smooth tubes, one sliding inside the other, hinged at both ends. Pull on it and the inner tube simply slides out; push and it slides in. Between its stops it carries no force at all. The chain would swing without dragging the cart, and the coil could not reach the chain. Only when the collar reached the stop would the strut act, suddenly and like a rigid rod. So the spring is what makes the tubes an elastic link. Real versions exist: the gas strut that holds a car's tailgate open, the spring strut of a bicycle's suspension.

The page sets the spring by the pull it carries when the chain hangs straight over the cart, \(N_0\), and by its stiffness \(k_s\); the rest length follows, \(L_0 = \ell_0 - N_0/k_s\). With the defaults, \(N_0 = 3\ \mathrm{N}\) and \(k_s = 100\ \mathrm{N/m}\), so \(L_0 = 0.62\ \mathrm{m}\): at rest the tubes are pulled 3 cm past the spring's natural length.

A telescope of two tubes cannot stretch to twice its collapsed length: each tube is 0.5 m long, so the strut is 0.5 m when pushed together and 0.95 m when the collar, 5 cm from the end of the inner tube, meets the stop. The page models each stop as a stiff spring, \(k_\text{stop} = 2\times10^4\ \mathrm{N/m}\), with a damper, \(c_\text{stop} = 40\ \mathrm{kg/s}\), that acts only while the stop is touched. The damper turns the knock into heat, \(Q_\text{stop} = \int c_\text{stop}\dot\ell^2\, dt\), the only loss in the rig. Your hand cannot pull past a stop either: a pose that would stretch the strut beyond \(\ell_\text{max}\) is refused, and the chain stops following the pointer.

05Where the chain can rest

A rest, or fixed point, is a state with all speeds and accelerations zero. Set them to zero in (5): what remains is \(\nabla V = (0, 0, F)\). With the coil off, \(F = 0\), and the cart's equation says \(N(x_2 - X) = 0\): the strut must stand vertical under ball 2, since a tilted strut would pull the cart sideways and nothing would hold it. A vertical strut pulls ball 2 straight down, so the rods must point straight down or straight up, \(\theta_i \in \{0, \pi\}\). Of these four arrangements, only straight down fits the tubes; the others need a strut far longer than 0.95 m.

With the coil on, a steady force \(F^*\) can hold the cart anywhere along the rail. For each cart position \(X = r\) the chain then leans until gravity and the tilted strut balance:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta_1} = 0, \quad \frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta_2} = 0 \quad\text{at } X = r, \qquad F^* = \frac{\partial V}{\partial X} = -N^*\, \frac{x_2^* - r}{\ell^*} . \tag{7} \]

One input gives a one-parameter family of rests, and \(r\) is the parameter you choose. The page solves the two angle equations by Newton's method in equilibrium(). For the default target \(r = 0.30\ \mathrm{m}\): \(\theta_1^* = 5.78^\circ\), \(\theta_2^* = 8.36^\circ\), the strut is \(\ell^* = 0.681\ \mathrm{m}\) long and pulls with \(N^* = 6.12\ \mathrm{N}\), and the coil holds the cart with \(F^* = 1.59\ \mathrm{N}\). The chain leans towards the cart, but less than the cart has moved: the strut is a soft leash, and gravity pulls the chain back under its pivot. The pale pendulum on the page draws this rest.

06Small swings: the guitar string★

Near a rest, every smooth system behaves like a set of coupled springs. Expand \(V\) to second order about the straight-hanging rest, using \(\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2\) and \(x_2 \approx l_1\theta_1 + l_2\theta_2\). The strut's length changes only to second order when the chain swings, and SymPy reduces the potential to

\[ \begin{aligned} V \approx{} & \tfrac12 (m_1+m_2) g\, l_1 \theta_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2 g\, l_2 \theta_2^2 \\ & + \tfrac12 N_0 \left(l_1 \theta_1^2 + l_2 \theta_2^2\right) + \tfrac12 \frac{N_0}{\ell_0} \left(l_1\theta_1 + l_2\theta_2 - X\right)^2 . \end{aligned} \tag{8} \]

The spring stiffness \(k_s\) has dropped out. To first order in the motion, only the pull \(N_0\) matters, and each term has a picture. The first two are gravity. The third is the strut pulling ball 2 down, an extra weight \(N_0\) hung on it. The last is a guitar string: a string under tension \(N_0\) and of length \(\ell_0\) resists a sideways offset \(\delta\) of its end with the force \(N_0\delta/\ell_0\), and here the offset is ball 2's horizontal distance from the cart, \(x_2 - X\). With the defaults the string stiffness is \(N_0/\ell_0 = 4.62\ \mathrm{N/m}\), soft compared with the pendulum's own gravity stiffness.

The last term is the only one that contains \(X\). With no tension, \(N_0 = 0\), the cart vanishes from the chain's equations and the chain from the cart's. No push on the cart could reach the swing. Tension is everything.

The sign of \(N_0\) matters too. A strut that pushes, \(N_0 < 0\), makes both new terms negative: it lightens the chain, and it pushes the cart away from the point under ball 2 like a pencil balanced on its tip. Already at \(N_0 = -2\ \mathrm{N}\) the matrix of (8) has a negative eigenvalue, so the hanging rest is unstable on its own: a disturbance grows by a factor \(e\) every 0.43 s. The slider allows pushes down to 6 N, and the controller of §8 still holds the chain (its slowest decay is then 0.28 to 0.46 per second), but the page starts with a pull.

The free motions are the solutions of \(\mathbf M\ddot{\mathbf q} + \mathbf K\mathbf q = 0\), with \(\mathbf K\) the matrix of second derivatives of (8). Trying \(\mathbf q = \mathbf u\, e^{i\omega t}\) gives \(\det(\mathbf K - \omega^2\mathbf M) = 0\). With the defaults there are three normal modes, at \(\omega = 1.71\), \(5.05\) and \(10.00\ \mathrm{rad/s}\) (0.27, 0.80 and 1.59 Hz). In the slowest the cart and the chain sway together on the soft string, the cart moving most. In the middle one the chain swings as a whole while the cart moves a little against it. In the fastest the two rods swing against each other, as in the faster mode of a bare double pendulum. Without the strut the bare pendulum has only two, at 3.39 and 8.18 rad/s; the strut's pull stiffens the chain and adds the third mode through the cart.

07Can one push reach everything?★

Write the state as \(\mathbf y = (\theta_1, \theta_2, X, \dot\theta_1, \dot\theta_2, \dot X)\) and its distance from a rest as \(\tilde{\mathbf y} = \mathbf y - \mathbf y^*\), with \(\tilde F = F - F^*\). For small \(\tilde{\mathbf y}\), (5) becomes a linear system,

\[ \begin{gathered} \dot{\tilde{\mathbf y}} = \mathbf A\tilde{\mathbf y} + \mathbf B\tilde F, \\ \mathbf A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \mathbf 1 \\ -\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf K & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ \mathbf M^{-1}(0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} . \end{gathered} \tag{9} \]

The page builds \(\mathbf A\) and \(\mathbf B\) numerically in linearise(), by central differences of the full equations of motion at the rest you chose; at the straight-hanging rest they agree with (8).

Is one push enough to steer six numbers? The test is Kalman's: the system (9) is controllable, meaning that some force history can carry any state to any other, if and only if the matrix

\[ \mathcal C = \left[\,\mathbf B,\ \mathbf A\mathbf B,\ \mathbf A^2\mathbf B,\ \dots,\ \mathbf A^5\mathbf B\,\right] \tag{10} \]

has full rank, 6. The idea is a chain of influences. \(\mathbf B\) is what a push does at once: it accelerates the cart. \(\mathbf A\mathbf B\) is what that does a moment later: the cart moves, stretches the string sideways, and starts to tug ball 2. Each further power reaches one link further into the chain. If some combination of the state never appears in this chain, no push can ever touch it. With the defaults the rank is 6; with \(N_0 = 0\) it falls to 2, the cart's own two numbers, as Section 6 predicted. The page computes the rank in rankOf() and prints it under the phase portrait.

Controllable does not mean easy. The obvious first try is the controller of Fig. 9 on the cart alone, \(F = K_p(r - X) - K_d\dot X\). It damps the chain only through the soft string, and only by accident of timing. Searching \(K_p\) and \(K_d\) over a wide grid, the best choice leaves the slowest motion decaying at 0.009 per second, a time constant of nearly two minutes. The controller has to look at the chain itself.

08The controller

A controller that looks at everything is full-state feedback: the coil's force is the holding force plus a weighted sum of all six errors,

\[ F = F^* - \mathbf K\,(\mathbf y - \mathbf y^*), \qquad\text{clamped to}\quad |F| \le \kappa I_\text{max} . \tag{11} \]

Each gain is a part we can picture. The gain on \(X\) is a spring anchored at the target, as \(K_p\) was in Fig. 9. The gains on the angles say how hard to move the cart when the chain leans, much as you move your hand under a stick you balance. The gains on the speeds are dampers. With six gains to choose, guessing is hopeless. The linear–quadratic regulator chooses them by asking for the least of a cost,

\[ \begin{gathered} J = \int_0^\infty \left( \tilde{\mathbf y}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf Q\,\tilde{\mathbf y} + \rho\,\tilde F^2 \right) dt, \\ \mathbf Q = \operatorname{diag}(q_\theta, q_\theta, q_X, q_v, q_v, q_v) . \end{gathered} \tag{12} \]

The first term charges for being away from the rest, the second for pushing. The sliders set the weights: \(q_\theta\) for the angles, \(q_X\) for the cart's position, \(q_v\) for all three speeds, and \(\rho\) for the coil's effort. Raise \(\rho\) and the controller pushes more gently and takes longer; raise \(q_v\) and it damps harder. The answer is a quadratic bowl in the state, \(J_\text{min} = \tilde{\mathbf y}_0^{\mathsf T}\mathbf P\,\tilde{\mathbf y}_0\), where \(\mathbf P\) solves the algebraic Riccati equation, and the best gains follow from it:

\[ \mathbf A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf P + \mathbf P\mathbf A - \rho^{-1}\mathbf P\mathbf B\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf P + \mathbf Q = 0, \tag{13} \] \[ \mathbf K = \rho^{-1}\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf P . \tag{14} \]

The page solves (13) in lqr() by the matrix sign function of the \(12\times12\) Hamiltonian matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf A & -\rho^{-1}\mathbf B\mathbf B^{\mathsf T} \\ -\mathbf Q & -\mathbf A^{\mathsf T}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\): the iteration \(\mathbf Z \leftarrow \tfrac12(\mathbf Z + \mathbf Z^{-1})\) converges in a handful of steps, and its blocks give \(\mathbf P\). For the default rest (\(r = 0.30\ \mathrm{m}\)) and weights \(q_\theta = q_X = q_v = 5\), \(\rho = 0.02\), the gains are \(\mathbf K = (19.22,\ -201.70,\ 120.09,\ 0.04,\ 0.52,\ 22.14)\), in newtons per radian or metre and per radian or metre per second; SciPy's Riccati solver gives the same numbers. The large gain on \(\theta_2\) is the hand under the stick: when ball 2 swings right, the cart is pulled the other way at first, which sets the string against the swing.

The gains depend on the rest, because the linear model does: each new target is a new design, done in a millisecond when you press Solve, release the flag, or let go of the chain.

Key result · why not a PID on the cart

The coil pushes one of three bodies. A PID on the cart's position sees the chain only through its effect on the cart, and leaves its swings almost undamped (slowest decay 0.009 per second). Feedback on the whole state, designed by LQR, gives 0.60 per second at the straight-hanging rest and 1.13 per second at the default target.

09Phase space and the region of rescue

The state of the rig is a point in a six-dimensional phase space, and the run is a path through it. We cannot draw six dimensions, so the page draws three shadows: the angle against the angular speed for each rod, and the position against the speed for the cart. In each the rest is a cross, and a run that settles is a path that spirals into it.

Without control the rest is a centre: all six eigenvalues of \(\mathbf A\) are imaginary, \(\pm1.71i\), \(\pm5.05i\) and \(\pm10.00i\) per second at the hanging rest, and the energy (2) + (3) is conserved, so a disturbed chain circles for ever on a surface of constant energy. With the controller the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf A - \mathbf B\mathbf K\) move into the left half-plane. For the default target they are \(-14.98\), \(-1.83 \pm 6.58i\), \(-1.19 \pm 11.65i\) and \(-1.13\) per second: two decaying swings and two plain decays. The rest has become a stable focus, and the slowest decay, \(1.13\) per second, sets the time scale on which the chain comes to rest, about a second per factor of three.

The Riccati solution brings a gift: \(\mathcal V = \tilde{\mathbf y}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf P\,\tilde{\mathbf y}\) is a Lyapunov function for the linear closed loop, a bowl whose height falls along every path,

\[ \dot{\mathcal V} = -\tilde{\mathbf y}^{\mathsf T}\left(\mathbf Q + \rho\,\mathbf K^{\mathsf T}\mathbf K\right)\tilde{\mathbf y} < 0 . \tag{15} \]

For the real, nonlinear rig this proves stability only near the rest. Far from it two things break the guarantee: the coil's current limit, which cuts the push off at \(\kappa I_\text{max} = 30\ \mathrm{N}\), and the nonlinearity of (5), which grows with the angles. The set of starts from which the controller still brings the chain home is the region of attraction, and it has to be measured. In a test of 43 starts the tubes allow, with both rods up to about \(\pm60^\circ\) and the cart up to 0.8 m from the target, at five targets between \(-0.4\) and \(+0.4\ \mathrm{m}\), all 43 runs came within 0.01 of the rest; half within 5.7 s, nine in ten within 7.9 s. Outside the region there are starts that end in a whirl: after a hard yank, ball 1 can circle its pivot faster than the leash can follow, and the status line says so.

10Solve first, then play

Nothing is computed while you watch. When you press Solve, release the flag or let go of the chain, the page designs the controller, then integrates (5) with the feedback (11) by the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, step \(h = 0.5\ \mathrm{ms}\), for 30 s: 60 000 steps. The force is evaluated at all four stages of each step, as a continuous controller would act. Every twentieth state, one every 10 ms, is stored with the force, the current, the strut's pull and length, and the energy; the animation then plays the stored samples back.

The step is small because of the stops: a touched stop is a spring of \(2\times10^4\ \mathrm{N/m}\) on half a kilogram, a vibration of about 200 rad/s, and RK4 needs \(\omega h\) well below 2.8 to stay stable. To grade the run, the page solves it a second time with \(h/2\) and stores the largest difference; from rest it is about \(10^{-11}\), after a drag a few times \(10^{-10}\), and larger, around \(10^{-6}\), when the coil hits its limit, because the clamp in (11) has a kink that RK4 steps across. The energy books are a second check. The coil's work \(W_\text{coil} = \int F\dot X\,dt\) and the stops' heat are integrated with the same RK4 weights as the state, and at the end \(E(0) + W_\text{coil} - Q_\text{stop} - E(T)\) is a few times \(10^{-10}\ \mathrm{J}\).

12What the model leaves out

  • The balls are points and the rods, tubes and spring are massless. Real rods have moments of inertia, and a strut with mass carries sideways forces and swings on its own.
  • Nothing rubs. Real hinges, tubes and wheels have friction, which would damp the uncontrolled swing and would add a dead zone the controller must push through.
  • The controller measures all six numbers exactly and at once. A real one needs sensors on both rods and the cart; speeds are usually estimated by an observer such as a Kalman filter, and every sample of delay costs stability.
  • The current source is ideal. A real amplifier has a voltage limit, and the back-emf \(\kappa\dot X\) eats into it when the cart moves fast. A long stroke of 2 m also needs a commutated linear motor rather than one coil in one long gap.
  • The gains come from the linear model at the target. Far from the rest the nonlinear rig can escape, as the whirl shows. Swinging a chain up from far away needs other ideas, such as pumping energy in or out deliberately before handing over to LQR.

Problems

  1. From (8), show that with \(N_0 = 0\) the cart's equation is \(M\ddot X = \tilde F\) and that the chain's equations do not contain \(X\). Which two columns of (10) survive?
  2. A string of tension \(N_0\) and length \(\ell_0\) is pulled sideways at one end by \(\delta\). Show that the restoring force is \(N_0\delta/\ell_0\) to first order, and explain why the spring stiffness \(k_s\) does not appear.
  3. Set \(\rho\) ten times larger on the page and solve again. How do the gains, the slowest decay and the time at the current limit change? Explain with (12).
