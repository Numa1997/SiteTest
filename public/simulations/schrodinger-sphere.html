Lecture note · Fig. 14 · quantum mechanics

A wave packet meets a sphere: the Schrödinger equation in three dimensions

Numa Koudsie · for readers meeting quantum mechanics for the first time, from school to the second year of university

Abstract. A ball thrown at a wall comes back; a swell in a harbour runs round a pillar and closes up behind it. A quantum particle does some of each, and the figure above shows how much. In this note we write down the Schrödinger equation and the starting wave packet, follow a free packet as it moves and spreads, solve the textbook problem of a wall in one dimension, and then see why a sphere in three dimensions gives a completely different answer. We then take apart the method that computed the figure, the split-step Fourier method, show why it keeps the total probability fixed and how large its errors are, and finish with what each colour and curve on the screen means. Every number is measured from the stored runs or by running the page's own code, and every result is checked with the page's own values in a worked example. Paragraphs marked ★ carry extra mathematics; you can skip them, because their results are restated without the star.

01A ball, a wall and a pillar

Throw a tennis ball at a garden wall and it comes straight back. Watch a swell roll into a harbour and meet a stone pillar standing in the water: the wave is not thrown back as a whole. It wraps round the pillar, and a few pillar-widths behind it the crests have closed up again as if the pillar were hardly there.

These are the two pictures of motion that classical physics offers. A ball is at one place at a time and follows one path; whether it gets over an obstacle is decided by its energy. A wave is spread out, it is partly reflected and partly bent round an obstacle, and how much of each happens depends on how the size of the obstacle compares with the wavelength. Quantum mechanics says that an electron, a proton or an atom is neither of these and borrows from both. It is described by a wave, and the strength of that wave at a place tells us how likely we are to find the particle there when we look. The particle is always found whole, like a ball; but where it is found is decided by a wave, which can reflect, bend and interfere.

The figure above follows such a wave. A particle, spread out as a small cloud called a wave packet, flies along the \(x\) axis towards a sphere. Inside the sphere the particle's potential energy is raised by a fixed amount, as if the sphere were a round hill with a flat top and steep sides. With the page's default settings the particle does not have enough energy to climb onto that hill. A classical ball aimed at it would bounce off. In the one-dimensional problem of every textbook, where the hill is a wall across the whole path, the wave would be reflected almost completely: we will find that only 2.5 parts in ten million get through. Yet at the end of the stored run 63 % of the probability lies ahead of the sphere, 24 % behind it, and 13 % still beside it. This note explains why, and how the computer found these numbers.

02The model

2.1The wave function and its equation

The state of one particle is described by a complex number at every point \(\mathbf r = (x, y, z)\) of space and every time \(t\), the wave function \(\psi(\mathbf r, t)\). A complex number has a size and an angle, \(\psi = |\psi|\,e^{i\varphi}\). The size tells us where the particle is likely to be; the angle \(\varphi\), called the phase, is what lets two parts of the wave add up or cancel when they meet. The rule that connects the wave to what we observe is Born's rule: the probability of finding the particle inside a region \(\Omega\) is

\[ P_\Omega(t) = \int_\Omega |\psi(\mathbf r, t)|^2 \, \mathrm{d}^3 r , \qquad \int_{\text{all space}} |\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}^3 r = 1 . \tag{1} \]

The second statement, the normalisation, says that the particle is certainly somewhere. The quantity \(|\psi|^2\) is a probability density: probability per unit volume.

How the wave changes in time is fixed by the time-dependent Schrödinger equation,

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\nabla^2\psi + V(\mathbf r)\,\psi . \tag{2} \]

Here \(m\) is the particle's mass, \(\hbar = 1.054\,6\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\) is Planck's constant divided by \(2\pi\), \(V(\mathbf r)\) is the potential energy the particle would have at \(\mathbf r\), and \(\nabla^2 = \partial^2/\partial x^2 + \partial^2/\partial y^2 + \partial^2/\partial z^2\) is the Laplacian, which measures how much \(\psi\) at a point differs from its average over the neighbourhood. The right-hand side is the total energy acting on the wave: the first term is the kinetic energy and the second the potential energy. We will see in section 3 why the kinetic energy takes this form. Equation (2) cannot be derived from older physics; it is the basic law of non-relativistic quantum mechanics, in the same way as Newton's second law is the basic law of classical mechanics, and it is tested by every atom whose spectrum it predicts.

Two properties of (2) matter for everything that follows. It is linear: if \(\psi_1\) and \(\psi_2\) are solutions, so is any sum \(c_1\psi_1 + c_2\psi_2\). Waves therefore pass through each other and add, which is the origin of interference. And it keeps the normalisation (1): the total probability that is 1 at the start stays 1 for ever. Probability can flow from one region to another, but it is never created or destroyed. In section 6 we will see the numerical method inherit this property exactly.

2.2The sphere and the units

The obstacle is a sphere of radius \(R\) centred at the origin, inside which the potential energy is raised by \(V_0\):

\[ V(\mathbf r) = \begin{cases} V_0, & |\mathbf r| < R, \\ 0, & |\mathbf r| \ge R. \end{cases} \tag{3} \]

We measure lengths in a unit \(a\) of our choosing, the grid unit, and choose units of time and energy so that \(\hbar = 1\) and \(m = 1\). The unit of time is then \(m a^2/\hbar\) and the unit of energy \(\hbar^2/(m a^2)\). Every number on the page is in these units, and (2) becomes

\[ i\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{2}\,\nabla^2\psi + V(\mathbf r)\,\psi . \tag{4} \]

Nothing is lost by this choice. The equation looks the same for every particle and every size of sphere; only the meaning of one unit changes.

Example 2.1 · what one unit means for an electron

Take an electron, \(m = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}\), and let one grid unit be \(a = 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}\), about the radius of an atom. The unit of energy is \(\hbar^2/(m a^2) = 7.62\ \mathrm{eV}\) and the unit of time \(m a^2/\hbar = 8.64\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s}\). The default packet energy \(E = 2.5\) is then \(19.0\ \mathrm{eV}\) and the default barrier \(V_0 = 3\) is \(22.9\ \mathrm{eV}\). The sphere of radius \(4\,a\) is \(0.4\ \mathrm{nm}\) across its radius, and the whole stored run, \(t = 13.16\), lasts \(1.14\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{s}\), about one femtosecond. These are the scales of an electron scattering from an atom or a small molecule.

2.3The starting packet

At \(t = 0\) the particle is a Gaussian wave packet centred at \((x_0, 0, 0)\):

\[ \psi(\mathbf r, 0) = \left(2\pi\sigma_0^2\right)^{-3/4} \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x - x_0)^2 + y^2 + z^2}{4\sigma_0^2} + i k_0 x\right] . \tag{5} \]

Read it in two parts. The real exponential is a bell shape: \(|\psi|^2\) falls off as \(e^{-(x-x_0)^2/(2\sigma_0^2)}\) along each axis, so \(\sigma_0\) is the standard deviation of the particle's position, its width. The factor \(e^{i k_0 x}\) has size 1 and only turns the phase, by one full turn every \(2\pi/k_0\) along \(x\). That turning phase is what makes the packet move, as section 3 will show; \(k_0\) is called the mean wave number. The number in front makes the total probability exactly 1. The page sets \(x_0 = -14\,a\), \(\sigma_0 = 3\,a\) and \(k_0 = \sqrt{2E}\), where \(E\) is the packet energy chosen with the slider (we will see in section 3 where the square root comes from).

03A packet in free flight

Before the packet meets the sphere it flies through empty space, \(V = 0\). This free flight already shows the two facts that make a quantum particle different from a ball: its wave moves at one speed while its crests move at another, and it spreads.

3.1Plane waves

The simplest solution of (4) with \(V = 0\) is a plane wave running along \(x\), \(\psi = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}\), with a wave number \(k\) and an angular frequency \(\omega\) still to be related. Its time derivative is \(\partial\psi/\partial t = -i\omega\psi\), so the left side of (4) is \(i(-i\omega)\psi = \omega\psi\). Its second space derivative is \(\partial^2\psi/\partial x^2 = (ik)^2\psi = -k^2\psi\), so the right side is \(-\tfrac12(-k^2)\psi = \tfrac12 k^2\psi\). The two sides agree if

\[ \omega = \tfrac12 k^2 \qquad\text{or, with the units restored,}\qquad \hbar\omega = \frac{(\hbar k)^2}{2m} . \tag{6} \]

This relation between frequency and wave number is called the dispersion relation. With de Broglie's identification of momentum \(p = \hbar k\) and Planck's identification of energy \(E = \hbar\omega\), the second form of (6) is simply \(E = p^2/(2m)\), the kinetic energy of a free particle. That is why the kinetic term in (2) has the form it has, and it is why the page sets \(k_0 = \sqrt{2E}\): in our units a plane wave of energy \(E\) has \(k = \sqrt{2E}\).

A plane wave has the same size everywhere, so by (1) it describes a particle that could be anywhere: it cannot be normalised. A particle that starts in a definite region must be a sum of many plane waves with nearby wave numbers, arranged to add up in that region and cancel elsewhere. That sum is the wave packet.

3.2The packet as a sum of plane waves

Along \(x\) the packet (5) is a sum of plane waves, which we write as a Fourier integral,

\[ \psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,\mathrm{d}k, \qquad |\phi(k)|^2 = \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_0^2}{\pi}}\;e^{-2\sigma_0^2 (k-k_0)^2} . \tag{7} \]

The weight \(\phi(k)\) of each wave number is the Fourier transform of \(\psi(x,0)\); for a Gaussian it is again a Gaussian (Problem 10.1 asks you to check this). The weights are centred on \(k_0\), and comparing the exponent with \(e^{-(k-k_0)^2/(2\sigma_k^2)}\) gives the spread of wave numbers \(\sigma_k = 1/(2\sigma_0)\). So

\[ \sigma_x\,\sigma_k = \sigma_0 \cdot \frac{1}{2\sigma_0} = \frac12 , \tag{8} \]

and, with \(p = \hbar k\), \(\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2\). This is the smallest product that Heisenberg's uncertainty relation allows: a narrow packet needs a wide range of wave numbers, and a sharp wave number needs a long packet. In the \(y\) and \(z\) directions the packet has no \(e^{ik_0 x}\) factor, so there the wave numbers are centred on zero with the same spread \(1/(2\sigma_0)\).

The spread of wave numbers is a spread of energies. The mean of \(k_x^2\) over (7) is \(k_0^2 + \sigma_k^2\), and the means of \(k_y^2\) and \(k_z^2\) are \(\sigma_k^2\) each, so the mean energy of the packet is

\[ \langle E \rangle = \tfrac12\left(k_0^2 + 3\sigma_k^2\right) = \frac{k_0^2}{2} + \frac{3}{8\sigma_0^2} . \tag{9} \]

The slider's \(E = k_0^2/2\) is therefore the mean kinetic energy of the motion along \(x\); the true mean energy is larger by \(3/(8\sigma_0^2) = 0.042\). (We checked (9) on the page's own grid: the Fourier transform of the starting packet gives \(\langle k^2\rangle/2 = 2.541\,667\) for \(E = 2.5\).) More important than this small shift is the width of the energy distribution along \(x\): roughly \(k_0\sigma_k\), which is 0.37 for the default packet. Part of the packet carries more energy than the average and part less, and near a barrier that matters, as we will see in section 5.

3.3How the packet moves and spreads★

Each plane wave in (7) evolves on its own, because the equation is linear and the plane waves are solutions: the wave with wave number \(k\) simply acquires the factor \(e^{-i\omega t} = e^{-ik^2 t/2}\). So

\[ \psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int \phi(k)\,e^{i(kx - k^2 t/2)}\,\mathrm{d}k . \tag{10} \]

We write \(k = k_0 + q\) and expand the exponent: \(kx - k^2t/2 = (k_0x - k_0^2t/2) + q(x - k_0 t) - q^2 t/2\). The first bracket does not depend on \(q\) and comes out of the integral as a phase. Taking \(x_0 = 0\) for brevity, \(\phi \propto e^{-\sigma_0^2 q^2}\), and what remains is

\[ \psi(x,t) \propto \int e^{-\alpha q^2 + i\beta q}\,\mathrm{d}q = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}\;e^{-\beta^2/(4\alpha)}, \qquad \alpha = \sigma_0^2 + \frac{it}{2} = \sigma_0^2(1 + i\tau),\quad \beta = x - k_0 t , \]

where \(\tau = t/(2\sigma_0^2)\), and we used the Gaussian integral obtained by completing the square in \(q\). The probability density is the square of the size: \(|e^{-\beta^2/(4\alpha)}|^2 = e^{-\beta^2\,\mathrm{Re}(1/\alpha)/2}\). Multiplying top and bottom of \(1/\alpha\) by the complex conjugate \(\sigma_0^2(1 - i\tau)\) gives \(\mathrm{Re}(1/\alpha) = 1/[\sigma_0^2(1 + \tau^2)]\), so

\[ |\psi(x,t)|^2 \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x - k_0 t)^2}{2\sigma_0^2(1+\tau^2)}\right] . \]

This is again a Gaussian. Its centre has moved to \(x = k_0 t\), and its width has grown to \(\sigma_0\sqrt{1+\tau^2}\). (We checked with SymPy that the closed-form \(\psi(x,t)\) satisfies (4) and that its variance is \(\sigma_0^2 + t^2/(4\sigma_0^2)\).) The same calculation in \(y\) and \(z\), with \(k_0 = 0\), gives the same width and no motion.

Key result · the free packet

A free Gaussian packet keeps its Gaussian shape. Its centre moves with the group velocity \(v_g = \mathrm{d}\omega/\mathrm{d}k = k_0\) (with units, \(\hbar k_0/m = p/m\), the classical velocity), and its width grows as

\[ \sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{t}{2\sigma_0^2}\right)^{2}} \qquad\text{in every direction.} \tag{11} \]

The crests inside the packet move at the phase velocity \(\omega/k_0 = k_0/2\), half as fast: they appear at the back of the packet, run forward through it and fade at the front.

Why does the packet spread? Its waves have different wave numbers, and by (6) different wave numbers travel at different speeds: the fast components run ahead and the slow ones fall behind. The spreading is slow at first, because \(\sqrt{1+\tau^2} \approx 1 + \tau^2/2\) for small \(\tau\), and becomes a steady growth at the rate \(\sigma_k = 1/(2\sigma_0)\) once \(t\) exceeds the spreading time \(2\sigma_0^2\). A narrow packet has a wide spread of wave numbers and spreads fast; a wide one spreads slowly. In the limit \(\sigma_0\to\infty\) we recover the plane wave, which never changes shape.

Example 3.1 · the default packet

For \(E = 2.5\), \(k_0 = \sqrt5 = 2.236\) per \(a\), the wavelength is \(\lambda = 2\pi/k_0 = 2.81\,a\), and the packet moves at \(2.236\,a\) per unit time. The spreading time is \(2\sigma_0^2 = 18\). The stored run lasts \(t = 13.16\), so the packet travels \(29.4\,a\), from \(x = -14\) to \(x = 15.4\), and by (11) its width grows from 3 to \(3\sqrt{1 + (13.16/18)^2} = 3.716\,a\). In the stored run without the sphere (\(V_0 = 0\)) the measured width across the flight, \(\sigma_y\), is \(3.7163\,a\) at the end, the same to the four digits stored. Over all 45 stored runs with \(V_0 = 0\), \(\sigma_y\) follows (11) to within \(9.4\times10^{-5}\,a\) at every frame. A slow packet takes longer: for \(E = 0.5\) the run lasts \(t = 29.61\) and the width reaches \(5.78\,a\).

04A wall in one dimension

Before the sphere, we solve the problem every quantum mechanics course solves first: a wall of height \(V_0\) and thickness \(d\) across the whole path, so that nothing can go round it. Everything then depends on \(x\) alone. We look for waves of one definite energy \(E\), of the form \(\psi(x,t) = u(x)\,e^{-iEt}\). Putting this into (4), the time derivative brings down \(-iE\) and we are left with the time-independent Schrödinger equation,

\[ -\tfrac12\,u''(x) + V(x)\,u(x) = E\,u(x), \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad u'' = -2\,(E - V)\,u . \tag{12} \]

4.1Inside the wall: the evanescent wave

Where \(V\) is constant, (12) is the equation of a simple harmonic oscillator in space when \(E > V\), and its solutions are \(e^{\pm i k' x}\) with \(k' = \sqrt{2(E - V)}\): waves, shorter where the particle is faster. When \(E < V\) the sign flips, \(u'' = +\kappa^2 u\) with

\[ \kappa = \sqrt{2\,(V_0 - E)} , \tag{13} \]

and the solutions are the growing and decaying exponentials \(e^{\pm\kappa x}\). A classical particle with \(E < V_0\) can never be inside the wall: its kinetic energy would be negative. The wave, though, does not stop at the face of the wall. It enters as \(e^{-\kappa x}\), shrinking by the factor \(e\) every \(1/\kappa\), and its probability density shrinks twice as fast. Such a wave, which does not oscillate and only fades, is called evanescent. The distance \(1/\kappa\) is the penetration depth.

Two conditions join the pieces of \(u\) at a face of the wall. The function \(u\) is continuous, because a jump would make \(u''\) infinitely large. Its slope \(u'\) is continuous too: integrating (12) across the face over a tiny width, the left side changes by the jump of \(u'\), while the right side, \(-2(E-V)u\), is finite and contributes nothing as the width shrinks to zero. A jump in the potential produces only a jump in the curvature \(u''\).

4.2Through the wall★

Let the wall occupy \(0 < x < d\) and let a wave of wave number \(k = \sqrt{2E}\) arrive from the left. On the left there is the incoming wave and a reflected one, inside the two exponentials, on the right only a transmitted wave:

\[ u(x) = \begin{cases} e^{ikx} + r\,e^{-ikx}, & x < 0, \\ C\,e^{\kappa x} + D\,e^{-\kappa x}, & 0 < x < d, \\ t\,e^{ikx}, & x > d. \end{cases} \]

Continuity of \(u\) and \(u'\) at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = d\) gives four equations for the four unknowns \(r, C, D, t\):

\[ 1 + r = C + D, \quad ik(1 - r) = \kappa(C - D), \quad C e^{\kappa d} + D e^{-\kappa d} = t\,e^{ikd}, \quad \kappa\left(C e^{\kappa d} - D e^{-\kappa d}\right) = ik\,t\,e^{ikd} . \]

The last two give \(C\) and \(D\) in terms of \(t\): adding and subtracting them, \(C = \tfrac12 t\,e^{ikd}(1 + ik/\kappa)\,e^{-\kappa d}\) and \(D = \tfrac12 t\,e^{ikd}(1 - ik/\kappa)\,e^{\kappa d}\). From the first two we remove \(r\): the first gives \(1 - r = 2 - C - D\), and the second becomes \(2ik = (\kappa + ik)C - (\kappa - ik)D\). Inserting \(C\) and \(D\),

\[ 2ik = \frac{t\,e^{ikd}}{2\kappa}\left[(\kappa + ik)^2 e^{-\kappa d} - (\kappa - ik)^2 e^{\kappa d}\right] = \frac{t\,e^{ikd}}{\kappa}\left[2ik\kappa\cosh\kappa d - (\kappa^2 - k^2)\sinh\kappa d\right] , \]

where we expanded \((\kappa \pm ik)^2 = \kappa^2 - k^2 \pm 2ik\kappa\) and collected \(e^{\pm\kappa d}\) into \(\cosh\) and \(\sinh\). The transmission probability is \(T = |t|^2\), because the transmitted wave has the same wave number, and so the same speed, as the incoming one. Taking the square of the size and using \(\cosh^2 = 1 + \sinh^2\),

\[ T = \frac{4k^2\kappa^2}{4k^2\kappa^2 + (\kappa^2 + k^2)^2\sinh^2\kappa d} . \]

Finally \(\kappa^2 + k^2 = 2V_0\) and \(4k^2\kappa^2 = 16E(V_0 - E)\). For \(E > V_0\) the same algebra holds with \(\kappa\) replaced by \(ik'\), and \(\sinh(ik'd) = i\sin k'd\). (Both branches were checked against a direct numerical solution of the four matching equations and against the page's own function T1D; they agree to all printed digits.)

Key result · the rectangular barrier

A wave of energy \(E\) meeting a wall of height \(V_0\) and thickness \(d\) gets through with probability

\[ T = \left[1 + \frac{V_0^2\,\sinh^2(\kappa d)}{4E\,(V_0 - E)}\right]^{-1}\ (E < V_0), \qquad T = \left[1 + \frac{V_0^2\,\sin^2(k' d)}{4E\,(E - V_0)}\right]^{-1}\ (E > V_0), \tag{14} \]

with \(\kappa = \sqrt{2(V_0 - E)}\) and \(k' = \sqrt{2(E - V_0)}\). For a thick wall, \(\kappa d \gg 1\), \(\sinh\kappa d \approx \tfrac12 e^{\kappa d}\) and \(T \approx \dfrac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\,e^{-2\kappa d}\): passing through the wall, called tunnelling, falls off exponentially with the wall's thickness measured in penetration depths. Above the barrier, \(T\) is less than 1, except when a whole number of half-wavelengths fits into the wall, \(k'd = n\pi\).

Two limits check (14). When \(V_0 \to 0\), the fraction in the bracket vanishes and \(T \to 1\): no wall, no reflection. When \(E\) approaches \(V_0\) from either side, both branches tend to \(T = 1/(1 + V_0 d^2/2)\), which is what the page's code uses at \(E = V_0\) exactly. Note also that \(T < 1\) for \(E\) above the barrier: a classical ball with enough energy always passes, but a wave is partly reflected at every sudden change of the medium, as light is partly reflected by a window pane.

Example 4.1 · the page's barrier, flattened

The readout "Guide: 1D slab of thickness 2R" evaluates (14) with \(d = 2R\), the thickness of the sphere along its centre line. With the defaults, \(E = 2.5\), \(V_0 = 3\): \(\kappa = \sqrt{2\times0.5} = 1\) per \(a\), so the penetration depth is \(1\,a\), two grid points. The slab is \(d = 8\,a\) thick, eight penetration depths, and \(\sinh 8 = 1490\), so \(T = [1 + 9\times1490^2/(4\times2.5\times0.5)]^{-1} = 2.50\times10^{-7}\). The thick-wall formula gives \(2.22\times e^{-16} = 2.50\times10^{-7}\) as well. Searching all the page's combinations with \(E < V_0\), the largest value of (14) is \(1.34\times10^{-3}\), for \(E = 0.5\), \(V_0 = 1\), \(R = 2\). Above the barrier, \(E = 4.5\), \(V_0 = 4\), \(R = 2\) gives \(k' = 1\), \(k'd = 4\), \(\sin^2 4 = 0.573\) and \(T = 0.495\): half the wave reflected although the particle has energy to spare.

05A sphere in three dimensions

If (14) were the whole story, the figure would show the packet bouncing back almost entirely for every \(E < V_0\). It does not. With the defaults, 63 % of the probability ends up ahead of the sphere, against \(2.5\times10^{-7}\) for the slab. The difference is not a subtle quantum effect. It is geometry: the packet is about as wide as the sphere, and a sphere, unlike a wall across the whole path, can be passed on the side.

5.1What a classical ball would do

We first ask what a cloud of classical balls with the same spread of positions and velocities as the packet would do. Two numbers come from geometry alone. Across the flight, the packet's probability density is \(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_0^2}e^{-\rho^2/(2\sigma_0^2)}\), where \(\rho = \sqrt{y^2+z^2}\) is the distance from the axis. A ball hits the sphere only if its impact parameter, the distance \(b\) of its line of flight from the centre, is less than \(R\). Integrating the density over the disc \(\rho < R\) with the area element \(2\pi\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho\),

\[ f_\text{aimed} = \int_0^R \frac{e^{-\rho^2/(2\sigma_0^2)}}{2\pi\sigma_0^2}\,2\pi\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho = 1 - e^{-R^2/(2\sigma_0^2)} . \tag{15} \]

For \(R = 4\) this is 0.589: four balls in ten miss the sphere altogether. Those that hit it are reflected like light from a polished ball. A ball arriving with impact parameter \(b\) meets the surface where the normal makes the angle \(\alpha\) with the line of flight, \(\sin\alpha = b/R\). The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, so the ball is turned through

\[ \theta = \pi - 2\alpha = \pi - 2\arcsin(b/R) . \tag{16} \]

A head-on ball, \(b = 0\), comes straight back, \(\theta = \pi\). A grazing ball, \(b \to R\), is hardly deflected. The ball moves backwards, \(\theta > \pi/2\), only if \(\alpha < \pi/4\), that is, \(b < R/\sqrt2\). By (15) with \(R\) replaced by \(R/\sqrt2\), the fraction sent backwards is \(1 - e^{-R^2/(4\sigma_0^2)} = 0.359\) for \(R = 4\). Most balls that hit the sphere glance off sideways or forwards.

To compare with the computed wave in full we followed 400 000 classical balls. For a Gaussian packet this comparison is fair: positions are drawn with the spread \(\sigma_0\) around \((x_0, 0, 0)\) and wave numbers with the spread \(1/(2\sigma_0)\) around \((k_0, 0, 0)\), which is exactly the distribution of position and momentum that (5) describes. Each ball flies straight; at the sphere's surface it keeps the part of its momentum along the surface and enters if the part along the normal carries more kinetic energy than \(V_0\), slowing and bending like light entering glass, or is reflected otherwise. We counted the balls in the three slabs at the end time of the stored run.

For a packet about as wide as the sphere, the outcome is decided first by geometry: the fraction aimed at the sphere, \(1 - e^{-R^2/(2\sigma_0^2)}\), and the angle at which it meets the curved surface, (16). Tunnelling through the sphere is negligible for every stored run with \(E < V_0\) (at most \(1.3\times10^{-3}\) even for the flat slab). 06The split-step Fourier method

Equation (4) has no solution in closed form for a sphere and a packet, so it is solved on a computer. The page itself computes nothing: every frame was computed beforehand on Kaggle's graphics cards by tools/kaggle/schrodinger-sphere/solver.py, and the page plays the stored frames back (section 8). This section describes what the solver does.

6.1The grid

Space is a cube of side \(L = 64\,a\), cut into \(N = 128\) points along each axis, \(128^3 = 2\,097\,152\) points in all, spaced \(\Delta x = L/N = 0.5\,a\) apart, at \(x_j = (j - 64)\,\Delta x\) from \(-32\) to \(31.5\). The cube is periodic: a wave leaving through one face comes back through the opposite face, as on the screen of an old arcade game. On such a grid every function is a sum of the plane waves that fit the box a whole number of times, with wave numbers \(k_n = 2\pi n/L\) for \(n = -64, \dots, 63\). The fast Fourier transform (FFT) converts the \(N\) values at the grid points into the \(N\) weights of these plane waves, and back, in about \(N\log_2 N\) operations instead of \(N^2\).

The grid can only represent wave numbers up to

\[ k_\text{max} = \frac{\pi}{\Delta x} = 6.28\ \text{per}\ a . \tag{17} \]

The reason is that a wave sampled at grid points cannot be told apart from a wave whose wave number differs by \(2\pi/\Delta x\): at every grid point \(e^{i(k + 2\pi/\Delta x)x_j} = e^{ikx_j}e^{2\pi i(j-64)} = e^{ikx_j}\). A shorter wave would be mistaken for a longer one, an error called aliasing, like the wagon wheels that turn backwards in films. The grid is fine enough if every wave number the packet contains stays below \(k_\text{max}\). By (7), the weights fall to \(e^{-8} = 3\times10^{-4}\) of their peak four spreads from \(k_0\), at \(k_0 + 4\sigma_k = k_0 + 0.67\), which is 3.67 for the fastest packet, \(E = 4.5\): 58 % of \(k_\text{max}\), or 4.2 grid points per wavelength at \(k_0 = 3\).

6.2One step: half a push, a free flight, half a push

Write (4) as \(i\,\partial\psi/\partial t = \hat H\psi\) with the Hamiltonian \(\hat H = \hat T + \hat V\), the sum of the kinetic-energy operator \(\hat T = -\tfrac12\nabla^2\) and the potential-energy operator \(\hat V\), which multiplies by \(V(\mathbf r)\). (A hat marks an operator: a rule that turns one function into another.) Over a short time \(\Delta t\) the exact solution is \(\psi(t + \Delta t) = e^{-i\hat H\Delta t}\psi(t)\). Each half of \(\hat H\) alone is easy. The potential alone, \(i\,\partial\psi/\partial t = V\psi\), only turns the phase at each point: \(\psi \to e^{-iV\Delta t}\psi\). The kinetic energy alone is a free flight, which by section 3 only turns the phase of each plane wave: \(\psi \to \mathrm{FFT}^{-1}\big[e^{-ik^2\Delta t/2}\,\mathrm{FFT}[\psi]\big]\), with \(k^2 = k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2\). The difficulty is that \(\hat T\) and \(\hat V\) do not commute: doing one after the other is not the same as doing them together, \(e^{-i\hat H\Delta t} \ne e^{-i\hat V\Delta t}e^{-i\hat T\Delta t}\). Strang's symmetric splitting puts half of one on each side of the other:

\[ \psi(t + \Delta t) \approx e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/2}\;\mathrm{FFT}^{-1}\!\left[e^{-ik^2\Delta t/2}\,\mathrm{FFT}\!\left[e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/2}\,\psi(t)\right]\right] . \tag{18} \]

Read it as a recipe: give the wave half a kick from the potential, let it fly freely for the whole step, and give it the other half of the kick. This is what the solver's loop body does, line for line: p = halfV * p; p = jnp.fft.ifftn(kin * jnp.fft.fftn(p)); p = halfV * p, followed by the border mask of section 7. The page uses \(\Delta t = 0.01\), about 1300 steps for the default run. In the chemistry literature this scheme is called the split-step Fourier method, after Feit, Fleck and Steiger (1982), and the symmetric splitting first appeared in print in a paper by Strang (1968); both facts are as Blanes, Casas and Murua report them (reference 3).

Key result · the split step keeps the probability exactly

Each factor in (18) multiplies a complex number by \(e^{-i\theta}\) with a real angle \(\theta\), which turns it without changing its size; the Fourier transform, with its normalisation, only changes the description from points to plane waves and keeps the sum of \(|\psi|^2\) (Parseval's theorem). Every factor is therefore unitary, and so is their product: in exact arithmetic, \(\sum_j |\psi_j|^2\,\Delta x^3\) is the same after any number of steps, whatever \(\Delta t\). The method can never blow up, and any loss of probability must come from elsewhere.

We measured this with the page's own solver on this workspace's processor, with the border switched off. In 64-bit arithmetic the total changed by \(3.6\times10^{-13}\) in 1500 steps, which is rounding error. In 32-bit arithmetic, the precision used on Kaggle, it fell by \(3.3\times10^{-4}\) in 1500 steps, about \(2\times10^{-7}\) per step: rounding in single precision has a small bias. On Kaggle's T4 cards the stored runs lose about \(4.6\times10^{-7}\) per step before any wave reaches the border.

6.3The error of one step★

How far is (18) from the exact \(e^{-i\hat H\Delta t}\)? The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula combines a product of exponentials of operators into a single exponential, with corrections made of commutators \([\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A\). For the symmetric product the terms of order \(\Delta t^2\) cancel, because the product reads the same backwards, and the first correction is of order \(\Delta t^3\):

\[ e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/2}\,e^{-i\hat T\Delta t}\,e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/2} = \exp\!\Big\{-i\Delta t\Big[\hat H + \frac{\Delta t^2}{24}\Big([\hat V,[\hat V,\hat T]] + 2\,[\hat T,[\hat V,\hat T]]\Big) + O(\Delta t^4)\Big]\Big\} . \tag{19} \]

(We checked the coefficients with random \(6\times6\) matrices: the remainder after the \(\Delta t^2\) term shrinks as \(\Delta t^2\) relative to it, as it should.) Equation (19) has a pleasant reading. The bracket is a Hermitian operator, so the split step is the exact evolution under a slightly modified Hamiltonian \(\hat H_\text{eff}\). The method solves a neighbouring problem exactly, which is why it stays unitary. Each step differs from the true one by a term of size \(\Delta t^3\); over a fixed time there are \(t/\Delta t\) steps, so the error in \(\psi\) grows like \(\Delta t^2\): the method is of second order.

The commutators tell us where the error lives. For \(\hat T = -\tfrac12\nabla^2\) one finds \([\hat V,[\hat V,\hat T]] = -|\nabla V|^2\), and \([\hat T,[\hat V,\hat T]]\) also contains derivatives of \(V\). Where \(V\) is constant, both vanish. For the sphere, \(V\) jumps at the surface, so the whole splitting error sits on the sphere's surface, the one place where the wave is reflected.

We measured the order with the solver in 64-bit arithmetic, the border switched off, for \(E = 2\), \(V_0 = 3\), \(R = 4\) up to \(t = 7.28\), while the packet is on the sphere. Halving \(\Delta t\) from 0.04 to 0.0025, the change in the whole wave function, \(\big(\sum|\psi_{\Delta t} - \psi_{\Delta t/2}|^2\Delta x^3\big)^{1/2}\), was \(1.21\times10^{-3}\), \(2.99\times10^{-4}\), \(7.45\times10^{-5}\) and \(1.86\times10^{-5}\): each halving divides it by 4.0, the signature of second order. At the page's \(\Delta t = 0.01\) the error of the wave function is therefore about \(\tfrac43\times7.45\times10^{-5} = 1.0\times10^{-4}\), and the probability behind the sphere changes by \(6\times10^{-6}\) when \(\Delta t\) is halved.

In words: the split step is exact for a free particle and exact for a particle that does not move; the error comes only from their interplay, it is concentrated where the potential changes, and it shrinks four times when the step is halved.

Example 6.1 · is the step short enough?

In one step the fastest wave the grid can hold, \(k_\text{max} = 2\pi\), turns its phase by \(k_\text{max}^2\Delta t/2 = 0.197\) rad; the packet's own fastest components, \(k = 3.67\), by 0.067 rad, and the highest barrier, \(V_0 = 8\), by \(V_0\Delta t = 0.08\) rad. All are small compared with one radian, so each step changes the wave only a little. Unlike the explicit schemes of many textbooks, the split step stays stable for any \(\Delta t\); a longer step makes it less accurate, never unstable.

Two more comparisons fix the size of the remaining errors. Precision: for \(E = 2\), \(V_0 = 3\), \(R = 4\), the probability ahead at the end is 0.627 80 on Kaggle (32-bit), 0.628 02 with the same solver on this workspace's processor (32-bit) and 0.628 21 in 64-bit. Grid: the same run on \(256^3\) points, \(\Delta x = 0.25\,a\), computed here in 5 minutes, gives 0.624 83 ahead and 0.249 23 behind, against 0.628 02 and 0.248 87 on \(128^3\). Halving the grid spacing changes the probability ahead by \(3\times10^{-3}\), the largest of the numerical errors: the staircase of grid cubes that stands in for the sphere's surface is the coarsest part of the model. The stored default run, recomputed here with the unchanged solver, matches the stored curves to \(3.4\times10^{-4}\) at every frame.

07The border and the checks

7.1An absorbing border

The periodic box of section 6 has a price: whatever leaves at \(x = +32\) would come back at \(x = -32\) and run into the sphere again. The solver prevents this with an absorbing border. After every step it multiplies \(\psi\) by a mask \(M(\mathbf r) = m(x)\,m(y)\,m(z)\), which is 1 in the interior and falls to 0 over the outer \(W = 12\) points of each face, \(6\,a\):

\[ m(x_j) = \left[\sin\frac{\pi j}{2W}\right]^{1/8} \ \ (j = 0, \dots, W-1), \qquad m = 1 \ \text{further in}, \tag{20} \]

mirrored at the opposite face. The eighth root makes the mask rise steeply at the very edge and then creep up to 1: \(m = 0,\ 0.775,\ 0.845,\ \dots,\ 0.990,\ 0.996,\ 0.999\) at \(j = 0, 1, 2, \dots, 9, 10, 11\). A wave crossing the frame is thinned a little at every step instead of being cut off at once, because a sudden edge would itself reflect the wave, as any sudden change does (section 4.2). The mask is not part of the physics. It stands for the rest of an infinite space, into which waves leave and never return.

Because the mask is applied once per step, its strength per unit time depends on \(\Delta t\): halve the step and the frame absorbs a little more in the same time. With the border on, halving \(\Delta t\) from 0.01 to 0.005 raised the probability lost by \(t = 14.57\) by \(8\times10^{-4}\) and lowered the probability behind by \(5\times10^{-4}\) (\(E = 2\), \(V_0 = 3\), \(R = 4\), 64-bit). Late in a run, numbers at the \(10^{-3}\) level are therefore set by the border, not by the equation.

7.2What "lost" means

The page reads the probability that has left the box as

\[ P_\text{lost}(t) = 1 - \sum_{j} |\psi_j(t)|^2\,\Delta x^3 , \tag{21} \]

the sum running over all \(128^3\) points. It has two sources. The main one is the border: the front of the transmitted packet, and the waves scattered sideways, reach the frame towards the end of a run. The other is the 32-bit rounding of section 6.2. Before any wave reaches the frame, the rounding is all there is: in the default run \(P_\text{lost}\) is \(1.5\times10^{-4}\) after 336 steps, while the same run in 64-bit loses only \(3.5\times10^{-6}\) in 300 steps. At the end of the run \(P_\text{lost} = 0.0039\). For \(E = 2\), \(V_0 = 3\), \(R = 4\) the stored run ends with \(P_\text{lost} = 0.0047\) and the 64-bit run with 0.0040, so about 85 % of the final loss is real absorption at the border.

Over all 405 runs, \(P_\text{lost}\) at the end lies between \(7\times10^{-4}\) and 0.123. The largest loss belongs to the slowest packet at the largest and highest sphere, \(E = 0.5\), \(V_0 = 8\), \(R = 6\): its run lasts \(t = 29.61\), long enough for the reflected and side-scattered waves to reach the frame. In the first quarter of every run (frames 0 to 12), before anything reaches the frame, the total probability stays within \(5.2\times10^{-4}\) of 1.

Example 7.1 · the checks, in one place

Three tests tell us that the stored runs solve (4). First, the free packet: with \(V_0 = 0\) the measured width \(\sigma_y\) follows (11) to \(9.4\times10^{-5}\,a\) in all 45 such runs. Second, the books balance: \(P_\text{behind} + P_\text{inside} + P_\text{ahead} + P_\text{lost} = 1\) by construction, and \(P_\text{lost}\) stays below \(5.2\times10^{-4}\) until the waves reach the border. Third, convergence: the time step contributes about \(10^{-4}\) to \(\psi\), single precision a few \(10^{-4}\) to the probabilities, the border up to \(10^{-3}\) late in a run, and the grid up to \(3\times10^{-3}\). The probabilities on the page are good to about two parts in a thousand.

08Computed once, played back

A single run moves two million complex numbers through about 1000 to 3000 steps, each with two three-dimensional FFTs. That is too much to do in a visitor's browser at the speed of an animation, so the whole grid of parameters was computed once on Kaggle, a free service with graphics cards (GPUs), and stored. On one Tesla T4 card one step at \(128^3\) takes 1.61 ms in 32-bit arithmetic, 0.77 ns per grid point, and 2.6 ms with the measurements of each frame. The 405 runs, every combination of the 9 energies, 9 barrier heights and 5 radii in Table 1, took 880.8 s on two cards, between 2.3 s and 6.9 s each.

Each run takes \(n_\text{target} = \mathrm{round}\big(30/(k_0\Delta t)\big)\) steps as its target, the time for the packet's centre to fly \(30\,a\). It stores 48 frames, one every \(s = \lfloor n_\text{target}/47\rfloor\) steps, so it really takes \(47s\) steps: for the default packet \(n_\text{target} = 1342\), \(s = 28\), and the run ends after 1316 steps at \(t = 13.16\), one frame every \(\Delta t_\text{frame} = 0.28\). Each frame stores, from the whole three-dimensional \(\psi\): the total \(\sum|\psi|^2\Delta x^3\), the probability behind (\(x < -R\)) and ahead (\(x > R\)), and the width across the flight, \(\sigma_y = \big[\sum (y - \bar y)^2|\psi|^2 / \sum |\psi|^2\big]^{1/2}\). It also stores \(|\psi|\) in the plane \(z = 0\) at all \(128\times128\) points as whole numbers from 0 to 255 (section 9). On the website the 405 files take 46.4 MB; the phase picture computed with them stays with the Kaggle notebook. The browser checks every file it downloads against its SHA-256 fingerprint in the index, and the panel reports the result.

10What the model leaves out

The model is a single particle without spin, moving slowly enough for (2) to hold, in a potential with a perfectly sharp edge. Real scatterers are softer: the potential of an atom or a nucleus changes smoothly over a distance comparable to its size, and a smooth edge reflects less than a sharp one, as Problem 10.5 lets you explore. Electrons have spin, and at high speed they need the Dirac equation; many electrons repel each other and are identical, which (2) for one particle cannot describe. The model also stops at the wave: it does not describe what happens when a detector finds the particle in one place, which is where the probabilities of (1) are turned into counts.

The computation has its own limits, all measured above: a box of finite size whose border removes waves and so ends each run after a fixed time; a time step and a grid that leave errors of order \(10^{-4}\) and \(10^{-3}\); single precision; and a picture of one plane in 256 levels. At the end of a run the scattering is not finished: in the default run 12.6 % of the probability is still beside the sphere, moving outwards.

The next model is the stationary theory of scattering. Instead of following one packet, one sends in a plane wave of a single energy and asks for the outgoing spherical wave far away, whose strength in each direction is the differential cross-section. For a sphere the wave is expanded in partial waves of definite angular momentum; a wave with angular momentum \(\ell\) passes the centre at a distance of about \(\ell/k\), so for \(k_0R = 8.94\) about nine partial waves meet the sphere, too many for the simple low-energy results and too few for pure ray optics, which is why the computed pictures show both reflection and diffraction. This theory is the subject of the scattering chapters of the textbooks listed below.
