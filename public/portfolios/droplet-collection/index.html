How a warm cloud of small droplets grows its first drizzle by gravitational collection, built one effect at a time: one drop sweeping a cloud, the drag that sets its speed, the air that steers droplets around it, the bookkeeping of a whole population, and the luck of the first drops. 0 The question

Look at a summer cumulus. It is made of water, but nothing falls out of it: its droplets are about ten micrometres in radius, and they sink through the air at about a centimetre per second, slower than the air rises in the cloud. Twenty minutes later the same cloud may rain. Raindrops are a hundred times wider than cloud droplets and hold a million times more water each. Something has to gather a million droplets into one drop, and it has to do it in minutes.

Gravity does it. A drop that is a little larger than its neighbours falls a little faster, overtakes the droplets below it and swallows some of them; it grows, falls faster still and swallows more. These notes build that process from first principles, one physical effect at a time, and ask three questions of it:

  1. How long does it take until most of the water sits in large drops? (Observable 1.)
  2. How long until the very first drizzle drop appears in a given volume of cloud, and how much does that time vary from one volume to the next? (Observable 2.)
  3. If the cloud is cold and holds ice, do collisions make new ice particles? (Observable 3, the question of the thesis these notes grew from: N. Koudsie, Collisional Ice Multiplication Processes in Clouds, Bachelor thesis, Leipzig 2022.)

The cloud we study

The test case is the thesis's warm cloud at 10 °C and 850 hPa: 300 droplets per cm³ with radii spread around 8 µm, and one larger drop per cm³ around 40 µm, which we call a collector. Collector is a role, not a kind of particle: in any encounter the larger drop is the collector. Both groups are given as lognormal size distributions (section 5.1). The droplets hold 0.805 g of water per m³ of air, the collectors 0.311 g/m³. The cloud is a closed, well-mixed box: no air rises, nothing condenses, nothing falls out. That isolates collection, at the price of everything else a real cloud does (chapter 9 says what).

A word on size names, all radii: cloud droplets are below 20 µm; drizzle drops have r ≥ 100 µm and fall about 0.7 m/s; raindrops have r ≥ 250 µm. Observable 1 counts the water in drops of radius r ≥ 40 µm, the fraction F40​(t), and the times t50​ and t90​ at which it reaches one half and nine tenths. It is a measure of growth, not a rain gauge: the box has no ground.

An estimate before any model

A collector of radius R falling at speed v sweeps a cylinder of air of volume πR2v per second. With the simplest fall-speed law (Stokes, chapter 2) a 40 µm drop falls at 0.197 m/s, so it sweeps 9.9×10−10 m³ each second. One million collectors per m³ sweep 9.9×10−4 of the air per second: if every droplet they met stuck, the droplet water would be cleared on a time scale of 17 min. Growth speeds this up, a collision efficiency below one slows it down: expect a few minutes to ten minutes for t50​. Every later result is checked against this estimate.

Conventions
  • SI units; radii in µm in the text. r is a radius, d=2r a diameter, R the larger radius in a pair.
  • Air at 10 °C and 850 hPa unless stated: density ρa​=1.0458 kg/m³ (ideal gas), viscosity μ=1.771×10−5 Pa s (Sutherland's law with the thesis's constants), water density ρw​=1000 kg/m³, g=9.81 m/s².
  • "In our cloud" boxes give numbers computed by the model on this page; measured data and quoted yields name their source; the Checks tab says how each is tested.
  • The notes climb a ladder: L0 (chapter 1) is the simplest model, and each later chapter adds one effect, with a number that shows it matters.
Part IOne drop

1 One drop sweeping a cloud

We start with the simplest picture that still contains the mechanism: one collector falling through a uniform cloud of identical small droplets, every droplet in its path sticking to it, and the fall speed given by Stokes' law (derived in chapter 2).

Run through drizzle holding a sheet of cardboard in front of you. Each second the sheet sweeps a volume of air equal to its area times your speed, and catches the drops in that volume. A falling collector is such a sheet, with one difference: every droplet it catches makes it wider and heavier, so it falls faster and sweeps more.

The growth law

Two spheres touch when their centres come within R+r. So, seen from the collector, every droplet whose centre enters a tube of radius R+r is hit. The droplets fall too, at v(r), so the tube advances through them at the difference of the two speeds. With W the mass of droplet water per m³ of air (the liquid water content), the collector's mass M grows as

dtdM​=Eπ(R+r)2[v(R)−v(r)]W,M=34π​ρw​R3.
(1.1)

Here E is the fraction of droplets in the tube that really touch and merge; in this chapter E=1. This is the continuous collection model of Langmuir (1948). With Stokes' fall speed v(a)=ka2, k=2(ρw​−ρa​)g/(9μ), and dM/dt=4πρw​R2dR/dt:

dtdR​=4ρw​EkW​R2(R+r)3(R−r)​.
(1.2)

When the droplets are much smaller than the collector, r≪R, this is dR/dt=cR2 with c=EkW/(4ρw​). Separate the variables, dR/R2=cdt, and integrate from R0​:

R(t)=1−cR0​tR0​​​which is infinite att∗=cR0​1​.
(1.3)
Check
Units: k is m⁻¹ s⁻¹, W/ρw​ is a pure number, so c is m⁻¹ s⁻¹ and cR0​t is a pure number. Limit W→0: R=R0​ for all time, as it should be.

The radius runs away to infinity in a finite time, and counting powers of R shows why: the swept area grows as R2, the Stokes speed as R2, and the mass to be added for each micrometre of radius as R2. So dR/dt∝R2R2/R2=R2: the growth feeds on itself faster than linearly, and ∫dR/R2 converges, which is the condition for reaching infinity in finite time. In a real cloud three things stop it: above about 40 µm the drag is no longer Stokes' (chapter 2), the droplets run out, and millimetre drops break up.

With droplets of finite size the integral of (1.2) can still be done (partial fractions):

t(R)=EkW4ρw​​[F(R)−F(R0​)],F(R)=8r1​lnR+rR−r​−4(R+r)23R+2r​.
(1.4)

As r→0, F(R)→−1/R and (1.4) becomes (1.3).

In our cloud
With W=0.643 g/m³ (300 droplets of exactly 8 µm per cm³; chapter 0's 0.805 g/m³ is the same number spread over a range of sizes, whose mean cube of the radius is 1.25 times (8 μm)3), k=1.2296×108 m⁻¹ s⁻¹ and E=1, a 40 µm collector grows to 100 µm in 9.96 min by (1.4); with r≪R, (1.3) gives 12.64 min: the droplets' own size shortens the time by a fifth. By (1.3) the radius would blow up at 21.1 min; with the droplets' own size, (1.2), already at 17.8 min.

Where the time goes

Write (1.3) as t=t∗(1−R0​/R). Growing from 40 to 60 µm takes one third of the time to infinity, and from 40 to 100 µm, 60 % of it. The time is spent while the collector is small, slow and, as chapter 3 will show, clumsy at hitting droplets. So the speed of the whole process is decided at the start, not by the runaway at the end.

A closed box: the droplets run out

In the thesis cloud there is one collector for every 300 droplets, and a 40 µm collector already holds the water of (40/8)3=125 droplets. If all collectors were identical and shared the droplets equally, each would end at the radius Rm​ that holds its own water and its 300 droplets: Rm3​=R03​+300r3, so Rm​=60.15 µm. Mass conservation turns the droplet water into a function of R, W(R)=Nc​34π​ρw​(Rm3​−R3), and (1.2) remains a single equation for R(t).

In our cloud
Identical collectors, finite droplets, E=1, Stokes speeds: half of all the water is inside the collectors after 2.93 min and nine tenths after 9.19 min.

Do not read Rm​ as a ceiling. It is the share of an average collector. A collector that starts larger sweeps more, so it grows more: for r≪R all collectors see the same droplet water, so 1/R0​−1/R is the same for all of them, and a collector that starts at 44 µm ends near 70 µm while one that starts at 40 µm ends at 60 µm. Luck in the order of captures does the same (chapter 6). Chapter 5 follows the whole population.

2 How fast a drop falls

Every rate in these notes is a difference of fall speeds, so the fall speed has to be right. Chapter 1 used Stokes' law. Here we derive it, see where it fails, and replace it by a drag balance that holds at every size in the study.

Draw a teaspoon slowly through honey. The honey closes in behind it at once, and doubling the speed doubles the push you feel. Swish the same spoon through water: the water piles up in front and swirls away behind, and doubling the speed roughly quadruples the push. In honey the fluid's viscosity sets the push; in water the momentum of the fluid thrown aside sets it. A cloud droplet lives in the honey world. A growing collector leaves it.

Stokes' drag, derived

Hold a sphere of radius a at rest and let air stream past it at speed U. When the air's inertia is negligible (we will check when), the flow obeys the creeping-flow equations ∇p=μ∇2u, ∇⋅u=0. For a flow symmetric about the stream direction, a stream function ψ(r,θ) (with θ measured from the stream direction) gives the velocity, ur​=∂θ​ψ/(r2sinθ), uθ​=−∂r​ψ/(rsinθ), and conserves volume automatically. Taking the curl of the momentum equation removes the pressure and leaves E2(E2ψ)=0, where E2=∂r2​+r2sinθ​∂θ​(sinθ1​∂θ​).

Try ψ=sin2θf(r): then E2ψ=sin2θ(f′′−2f/r2), and the equation becomes an ordinary one whose solutions are powers, f=Ar4+Br2+Cr+D/r. Far away the flow must be uniform, ψ→21​Ur2sin2θ, so A=0 and B=U/2. On the surface the air must not slip, ur​=uθ​=0 at r=a, so f(a)=f′(a)=0. Two conditions fix C and D:

ψ=21​Usin2θ(r2−23​ar+2ra3​).
(2.1)

The momentum equation then gives the pressure, p−p∞​=−23​μUacosθ/r2, higher on the upstream face; on the surface the normal viscous stress vanishes and the shear stress is τrθ​=−23​μUsinθ/a. Adding up the stream-wise components over the sphere:

F=pressure2πμaU​​+shear4πμaU​​=6πμaU.
(2.2)
Check
Dimensions alone give F∝μaU; the factor 6π needs the solution. SymPy confirms E4ψ=0, the no-slip conditions, the pressure, the shear and the two parts of (2.2) (Checks tab).

A falling drop reaches its terminal speed when the drag balances its weight less its buoyancy: 6πμav=34​πa3(ρw​−ρa​)g, so

vS​(a)=9μ2(ρw​−ρa​)g​a2=ka2.
(2.3)

Stokes' law undermines itself

We dropped the air's inertia. Its size relative to the viscous force is the Reynolds number Re=2aρa​v/μ (by custom with the diameter). Put Stokes' own speed into it: Re=4ρa​(ρw​−ρa​)ga3/(9μ2), growing as a3.

In our cloud
Stokes' speed has Re = 1 at a = 41.0 µm. Our collectors start at 40 µm. At 100 µm Stokes gives 1.23 m/s and Re = 15: far outside the law's own assumption.

A drag balance for every size

For a rigid sphere the drag can depend only on a, v, ρa​ and μ: with the force that is five quantities in three units, so by Buckingham's theorem two dimensionless groups, the Reynolds number and the drag coefficient CD​=F/(21​ρa​v2πa2). The drag law is a curve CD​(Re). The balance of weight and drag then reads

34​πa3(ρw​−ρa​)g=21​ρa​v2πa2CD​(Re).
(2.4)

The unknown v sits on both sides, once inside Re. Multiply by Re2 and it cancels: the Davies number

NDa​≡CD​Re2=3μ24ρa​(ρw​−ρa​)g​d3
(2.5)

contains only the drop and the air. So the recipe is: compute NDa​, find the Re that belongs to it, and v=μRe/(ρa​d). With Stokes' drag, CD​=24/Re, it gives NDa​=24Re and returns (2.3) exactly.

The curve Re(NDa​) for 0.01≲Re≲300 cannot be derived in closed form. These notes take it from Beard (1976), who fitted a polynomial in lnNDa​ to the drag of rigid spheres: for Re up to 20, the numerical solutions of the full Navier–Stokes equations by LeClair, Hamielec and Pruppacher (1970), which those authors found in close agreement with measured drag on spheres and on water drops; above that, the wind-tunnel measurements of water drops by Beard and Pruppacher (1969). Below a diameter of 19 µm Beard uses Stokes' law with a small correction for the slip of air molecules (+1.2 % on the speed of an 8 µm droplet); above 1.07 mm a separate fit lets the drop flatten. The curve is a fit and must not be extrapolated outside its range.

Check against measurement
Against Gunn and Kinzer's 34 measured speeds (in their 50 % humid air), the drag balance is within 0.49 % above 1.2 mm (where Beard fitted it to these very data), and 0.2 to 1.9 % below the data between 0.3 and 1.0 mm, always on the same side; the largest gap from 0.3 mm up is 2.07 % in dry air (the old study quoted 2.1 %). At 0.1 and 0.2 mm the gaps are −7.5 and −3.5 %.
In our cloud
The drag balance gives 0.177 m/s at 40 µm, 0.348 m/s at 60 µm and 0.728 m/s at 100 µm. Stokes is too fast by 11 %, 27 % and 69 %. Near 100 µm the drag grows as v1.29: 29 % of the way from the honey world (F∝v) to the water world (F∝v2).

What the better speed changes

For one collector the change is modest. In both settings of chapter 1 the time is t=∫w(R)dR/[v(R)−v(r)] with the same positive weight w for both speed laws, so the ratio of the two times is a weighted mean of the ratio of the relative speeds: it must lie between that ratio's smallest and largest value over the radii crossed.

In our cloud
One collector, 40 → 100 µm: 9.96 min with Stokes, 13.02 min with the drag balance, a factor 1.31 (bounds 1.12 and 1.70). Closed box: the times to put half and nine tenths of the water into the collectors grow by factors 1.14 and 1.20.

For the whole cloud the change is much larger, and that was a surprise. In chapter 5 the full collection equation, with every drop allowed to meet every other, takes 4.51 min to put 90 % of the water into drops above 40 µm with Stokes speeds and 7.96 min with the drag balance: a factor 1.76, outside the single-collector bounds. The reason: in a population, collectors also meet each other and droplets meet droplets, and those rates depend on the difference of fall speeds between drops of similar size. Stokes' a2 law exaggerates that difference far more than it exaggerates the speed of one collector.

3 Which droplets touch

Chapter 1 let every droplet in the swept tube stick. It does not.

Drive through heavy rain and every drop ahead hits the windscreen. Drive through fine mist and much of it never touches the glass: the air pushed aside by the car carries it round. A falling drop pushes the air below it aside in the same way, and only droplets heavy enough to keep going while the air turns reach its surface.

The droplet's equation of motion

Sit on the collector: the air far away rises past it at U=v(R), and near it the air has some velocity field u(x). The small droplet, the partner, feels the drag on its velocity relative to the local air, and its own weight (ez​ is the unit vector pointing up):

dtdx​=v,τdtdv​=u(x)−v−τg∗ez​,g∗=g(1−ρw​ρa​​).
(3.1)

τ is the partner's response time: in Stokes drag τ=2ρw​r2/(9μ), and we use τ=v(r)/g∗ so that the partner falls at the speed of chapter 2 when the air is still. Measure lengths in R, speeds in U and time in R/U (the time the air takes to pass the collector), and write u^=u/U for the air's velocity in these units. Then

dsdy​=w,Stdsdw​=u^(y)−w−Sez​,St=RτU​,S=Uv(r)​.
(3.2)

One number decides most of the story. The Stokes number St is the partner's stopping distance τU in collector radii: how far it would coast if the air stopped turning it. A partner with St ≪ 1 follows the air round; one with St ≫ 1 goes straight and hits. S is its own settling speed in units of U. (Langmuir called the same number K; with Stokes' τ, K=St.) The partner touches when its centre comes within 1+p of the collector's, p=r/R.

In our cloud
An 8 µm droplet meeting a 40 µm collector: St = 3.60, S = 0.045.

Two model flows

The air around a sphere is known exactly only in two limits. Creeping flow (Re → 0) is (2.1): the air sticks to the surface and its disturbance reaches far, decaying as 1/r. Potential flow (Re → ∞, no boundary layer) is a uniform stream plus a dipole, ψ=21​Usin2θ(r2−a3/r): the air slips along the surface and its disturbance decays as 1/r3, so droplets are turned only close by and more of them hit. Our collectors have Re between 0.8 and 9, in between. Langmuir (1948) joined the two efficiencies with an empirical weight, E=(ES​+EP​Re/60)/(1+Re/60); we use it and call it what it is, an interpolation.

The critical Stokes number

Take a point partner without weight (p=0, S=0) straight below the collector, on the axis. Let η be its distance from the surface in collector radii. In potential flow the air on the axis approaches the surface at 1−(1+η)−3≈3η, so near the surface (3.2) becomes

Stη′′+η′+3η=0,η∝eκs,κ=2St−1±1−12St​​.
(3.3)

A damped oscillator. If 12St<1 it is overdamped and creeps toward the surface for ever; if 12St>1 it overshoots zero, which means the partner reaches the surface. So in potential flow

Stc​=121​.
(3.4)

In creeping flow the air's approach vanishes as 23​η2, the linear argument fails, and the threshold must be computed along the whole axis. Our solver gives Stc​ = 1.21194, the value Michael and Norey (1970) give "correct to five decimal places" (Langmuir 1948 had 1.214). Below the critical number a point droplet never touches; a real droplet can still graze the collector with its own radius.

From the grazing path to the efficiency

Start partners far below at a sideways offset b and integrate (3.2). Those starting inside some bc​ hit; those outside miss. Partners far upstream are spread evenly and move together, so the hit rate is πbc2​ times their flux, and

E=(1+p)2bc2​​.
(3.5)

The real capture area over the geometric one. (In creeping flow the disturbance reaches so far that we start at 200 radii and count the partners crossing the start disc with the stream function; starting at 400 radii instead changes E(40, 8) by less than 10−4.)

In our cloud
E(40 µm, 8 µm) = 0.344, against 0.50 in Hall's table. The old study's stored table, made by a program that is lost, had 0.343.

How far to trust it

Three simplifications sit inside these numbers, and a reader should know their size.

The flow between the two limits. At 40 µm the collector's Reynolds number is 0.84. Creeping flow is right only within the distance where viscosity still beats inertia, about ν/U=2R/Re=2.4 collector radii (ν=μ/ρa​, the air's kinematic viscosity); the partner coasts 3.6 radii (St=3.6), so it crosses air that neither model flow describes. Langmuir's weight decides how much potential flow enters, and its 60 is an analogy with the drag curve, not a property of the flow. One more comparison bears on our pair. For a point droplet without weight, our creeping-flow efficiencies agree with Langmuir's own table within 3.2 % for K≥5, but run 8 to 10 % below it at K = 3 to 3.6, which is where 40/8 µm sits (St = 3.60). Our number is confirmed by an independent integration (Checks tab: 0.3442 against 0.3444), so the gap is between our solution and Langmuir's 1948 approximation, and it is not resolved here.

One-sided flow. Only the collector moves the air; the partner's own flow is left out. That is good when the partner is much smaller (at 40/8 µm its flow at the collector is under 1 % of U) and poor when they are nearly equal. Above p=0.6 we hold E at its value at p=0.6; chapter 5 shows these pairs carry under 2 % of the water's growth.

Small collectors. A partner without inertia reaches the surface only if the air carries it in faster than it sinks. In creeping flow the air approaches the surface at 1−2(1+p)3​+2(1+p)31​ (in units of U), and for every pair of our cloud the partner's settling ratio S is larger: at 40/8 µm, 0.045 against 0.039. Without inertia it would hover in front of the collector and never arrive; inertia carries it in. Below about 25 µm the collector is too slow to give its partners enough inertia, and our efficiency drops to nearly zero, where Hall's table, which lets the two drops' flows interact (Davis 1972, Jonas 1972), has 0.01 to 0.08. Putting Hall's values in for collectors below 30 µm changes t90​ by 0.2 %: the artefact is real but harmless here. It would not be harmless if every small drop stuck to every other: with E = 1 for collectors below 30 µm, t90​ falls to 12.7 min, because droplets then merge among themselves. How sticky the smallest drops are is a question the answer is sensitive to, and one the one-sided model cannot settle.

In our cloud
The efficiency is the largest single change of the ladder. In the full collection equation of chapter 5, 90 % of the water is in drops above 40 µm after 7.96 min with E = 1 and the drag balance, after 13.47 min with this chapter's efficiencies, and after 11.38 min with Hall's.

4 Touching is not merging

Watch rain on a puddle. Most drops vanish into it at once; some bounce, or skate across on a film of air before they sink. Two drops in a cloud face two hazards. Before they touch, the thin film of air between them must drain away, or they bounce. After they touch, a hard hit can stretch the merged drop so far that it tears apart again. The law below describes only the second hazard.

Only part of the drops' energy can do the tearing. Two drops of masses m1​, m2​ with velocities v1​, v2​ have kinetic energy 21​m1​v12​+21​m2​v22​. Write each velocity as the velocity V of the common centre of mass plus a part of the relative velocity Δv=v1​−v2​: v1​=V+m1​+m2​m2​​Δv, v2​=V−m1​+m2​m1​​Δv. Square and add; the cross terms cancel, and

21​m1​v12​+21​m2​v22​=21​(m1​+m2​)V2+21​μred​Δv2,μred​=m1​+m2​m1​m2​​.
(4.1)

The centre of mass keeps moving whatever happens (momentum is conserved), so only the second part, the collision kinetic energy CKE=21​μred​Δv2, can deform the drops. Compare it with the surface energy of the merged drop of diameter dc​=(d13​+d23​)1/3, with σ the surface tension of water (0.0742 N/m at 10 °C); their ratio is a Weber number,

We=πσdc2​CKE​,Ecoal​=e−1.15We.
(4.2)

The exponential law is the thesis's, and the form Straub et al. (2010) fitted to simulations of raindrop pairs; it is a fit, not a consequence of energy conservation, and it was made for drops of 0.35 mm and more.

In our cloud
For an 8 µm droplet hitting a 40 µm collector the collision energy is 3.0×10−14 J and We = 2.0×10−5: the surface is 50 000 times stiffer than the hit, and E_coal = 0.99998. For two collectors of 60 and 50 µm, We = 2.9×10−4. Only for drizzle and rain does the law bite: a 500 µm drop meeting a 250 µm drop has We = 0.52 and E_coal = 0.55.

So for the growth that decides observable 1, coalescence is a stated check: setting E_coal = 1 changes t90​ by 0.25 %. It matters in two other places. For millimetre drops, where measured laws differ among themselves (Low and List 1982 give 0.34 where (4.2) gives 0.55 for 500/250 µm). And for the thesis kernel, which used Stokes speeds in (4.2): at 500/250 µm Stokes' relative speed is 23 m/s instead of 2.1 m/s, We = 61, and the merger is switched off. That is why the thesis kernel's large drops pile up below a millimetre.

Check against measurements
The law (4.2) gives 1 to within 4×10−4 for every pair that decides observable 1, but it knows nothing of the air film. What experiments say: for cloud drops of 55–105 µm radius colliding with each other (size ratio 0.5–1), the coalescence efficiency is "certainly greater than 91 % and likely greater than 95 %" (Beard et al. 2002), so collector–collector mergers do merge. For small droplets hitting drizzle-sized collectors, bouncing has been seen: Beard and Ochs (1984) find that, averaged over a collector's growth from 50 to 500 µm by catching 30 µm droplets, only half of the collisions lead to a merger. So taking Ecoal​≈1 is supported for the pairs that make the first drizzle, and is an assumption the law cannot test for drizzle drops sweeping up cloud droplets.

5 A whole population: the collection equation

Keep a ledger with one line per size. When a 10 µm and a 20 µm droplet merge, cross out one entry on each of their lines and write one on the line of 20.8 µm. Number falls by one per merger; mass is moved, never made.

5.1 Gains and losses

Let n(m,t)dm be the number of drops per m³ of air with mass between m and m+dm, and K(m,m′) the volume of air one drop of mass m clears of partners of mass m′ per second, the kernel of chapters 1–4: K=π(R+r)2∣v(R)−v(r)∣EEcoal​. The ½ counts each pair once: as m′ runs from 0 to m, the pair (m′, m−m′) is met twice. This is Smoluchowski's coagulation equation; in cloud physics it is called the stochastic collection equation (SCE). It is an equation for the mean: it assumes that the numbers of drops in different size classes are uncorrelated (Gillespie 1972). Chapter 6 tests that.

5.2 The thesis's bookkeeping as a special case

The thesis kept two fixed groups: collectors that grow smoothly by (1.1) and never meet each other, and droplets that only disappear. We call this the gravitational collection equation (GCE). It follows from (5.1) if only collector–droplet pairs are allowed and each collector's jumps (one droplet at a time) are replaced by their average drift: a collector of mass M then grows at A(M)=∫mK(M,m)n(m)dm, which is (1.1) summed over droplet sizes. What it leaves out: collectors meeting collectors, droplets meeting droplets, and the spread between collectors that are lucky or unlucky in their captures.

5.3 Solving it on a grid

Sizes span more than three decades of radius (1 µm to 4 mm) and eleven of mass, so the bins are spaced logarithmically: bin k holds drops of mass xk​=x0​2k/s, with x0​ the mass of a 1 µm sphere and s bins per doubling of mass. In a time step Δt, bins i<j produce Cij​=Kij​Ni​Nj​Δt mergers per m³, and a bin with itself Cii​=21​Kii​Ni2​Δt (each pair counted once). Each product has mass x∗=xi​+xj​, which almost never falls on a grid mass; it lies between xk​ and xk+1​. Put the fraction f of the products in bin k+1 and 1−f in bin k, and demand that both their number and their mass come out right:

qk​+qk+1​=Cij​,xk​qk​+xk+1​qk+1​=x∗Cij​⇒f=xk+1​−xk​x∗−xk​​.
(5.3)

A lever rule. A step may never take more drops from a bin than it holds (counting what the product returns to the same bin), and products above the last bin (4 mm) go to an overflow store that keeps their mass. The cost of (5.3): a product is smeared over two neighbouring bins, a numerical diffusion that only an exact test can measure.

Check: Golovin's exact solution
For the artificial kernel K=b(m+m′) and an exponential start (5.1) has an exact solution (Golovin 1963; the Checks tab shows how a Laplace transform turns (5.1) into a solvable equation for this kernel, and gives the solution). With b = 1.5 m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹, a mean drop mass equal to that of a 10 µm sphere, and 1 g/m³, after 60 min the scheme's spectrum error falls from 0.428 at 2 bins per doubling to 0.150, 0.051 and 0.018 at 4, 8 and 16: an observed order of about 1.5. At 8 bins per doubling its mass error is −4.58×10−7, which is exactly the water the exponential puts below the grid's first bin; the scheme itself changes the mass by 2×10−13.

6 Luck: the first drizzle

The collection equation follows the average cloud. Rain begins with the luckiest drops.

Two identical collectors start side by side. One happens to meet another collector early: at a stroke it has twice the mass, falls faster and sweeps more; the other waits. Like savings started a few days apart, the early lead never closes. Among a million collectors, a few are lucky in this way, early and more than once, and they are the first drizzle.

6.1 The process behind the equation

In a well-mixed volume V, each pair of drops (i,j) merges in the next dt with probability K(ri​,rj​)dt/V, whatever happened before. The cloud is a random process that jumps from one list of drop sizes to the next. The probability of every possible list obeys a master equation; its average obeys (5.1) only if the counts in different size classes are uncorrelated, which becomes exact as V→∞ (Gillespie 1972). In a small volume it is not: when one drop gets lucky, it has also used up droplets that its neighbours can no longer have.

6.2 Sampling the process exactly

Gillespie (1975) showed how to draw the history of such a process without any time step. If the total rate of all pairs is a0​, the waiting time to the next merger is exponential, tw​=−lnu1​/a0​ with u1​ uniform on (0, 1], and the pair is chosen in proportion to its rate. Computing all N2/2 pair rates after every merger is too slow for a million drops, so we use his second device, thinning: propose pairs at a larger rate that is easy to compute, and accept each with the probability true rate over proposed rate. We sort drops into size classes and give each pair of classes an upper bound K∗ on the kernel. Proposals then arrive at the rate ∑K∗na​nb​/V; a proposed pair is accepted with probability K/K∗. The accepted events are exactly the coalescence process, because a Poisson process thinned with probability K/K∗ is a Poisson process of rate K/V for each pair.

Check
The bound is checked at every proposal (zero violations in 430 runs); 50 to 59 % of proposals are accepted, depending on the volume. Each run conserves water exactly. Section 6.4 compares the averages with the collection equation.

The drops are drawn from the two lognormal modes, but cut at three standard deviations (σg3​): no collector starts above 69 µm. Without the cut, the lognormal tail itself would put 0.25 drops of 100 µm in every m³ at the start, and in a large volume the first drizzle would be decided by the initial spectrum, which comes from aerosol and condensation physics outside these notes.

6.3 The first 100 µm drop

Run the process many times in volumes of 1, 10, 100 and 1000 cm³ (200, 100, 100 and 30 runs) and note when the first drop reaches 100 µm.

In our cloud
In 1 cm³ (one collector and 300 droplets) no drop ever reaches 100 µm: there is not enough water (a 100 µm drop holds the water of 3.75 cm³ of this cloud). The median time to the first 100 µm drop is 605 s in 10 cm³, 286 s in 100 cm³ and 175 s in 1000 cm³. In every one of the 230 runs that made one, the drop got there by merging with another drop of 30 µm or more: collectors meeting collectors, not droplet capture alone, make the first drizzle here. Capture alone would be far too slow: the largest collector the cloud starts with (69 µm), sweeping 8 µm droplets with all of the cloud's droplet water and none used up, would need 1312 captures and 448 s to reach 100 µm, and the number of captures in a given time varies by only a few percent, so no amount of capture luck brings that down to three minutes.

The larger the volume, the sooner its luckiest drop arrives. To reach a cubic metre, which we cannot simulate drop by drop, use the collection equation's tail. A drop above 100 µm stays above 100 µm, so the first one has arrived by time t if the volume holds at least one. If such drops are rare and independent, their number in V is Poisson-distributed with mean Vn100​(t), where n100​ is the concentration of drops above 100 µm in the mean field:

P(no 100 µm drop in V by t)=e−Vn100​(t),median: Vn100​=ln2.
(6.1)
In our cloud
(6.1) predicts medians of 280 s in 100 cm³ and 175 s in 1000 cm³; the exact runs gave 286 and 175 s. Trusting it for a cubic metre: the first 100 µm drop in 1 m³ appears after about a minute (55, 57 and 61 s with 8, 16 and 32 bins per doubling: (6.1) leans on the far tail of the collection equation, which converges slowly), the first 1 mm drop after about 18 min (untested by exact runs: a 1 mm drop holds the water of 3.75 litres of cloud). Half of the water is in drops above 100 µm only after 585 s.

In 10 cm³ the argument fails, and the way it fails is instructive. The mean field says that at most 32 % of such volumes ever hold a 100 µm drop; every one of the 100 exact runs made one, after a median of 605 s. Ten cm³ hold about ten collectors and the water for one drop of 139 µm, no more. Once two collectors merge, the merged drop is the largest in the volume by far and sweeps up the rest: every box ends with one drop of 100 to 140 µm. The collection equation describes a cloud so large that its large drops keep growing on water from far away: at 605 s it already has 54 % of the water above 100 µm, but packed into drops of 155 µm (mass-weighted mean radius), only 0.39 of them per 10 cm³. Counting those few large drops, (6.1) misses the many boxes whose single drop is just over 100 µm. (6.1) needs large drops that are rare and independent of the volume's own water, which in a volume this small they are not.

So in a cubic metre the first drizzle drop appears about ten times earlier than the time at which half the water has become drizzle. That rain starts with a few lucky drops is Telford's (1955) point, and Kostinski and Shaw (2005) estimated a factor of about ten for the luckiest one in a million collectors when the luck lies in droplet captures. Here the factor is similar but the luck is different: it lies in meeting another collector early. Note what carried the luck: the collection equation's tail already contains it, because (5.1) moves drops one merger at a time. What does not contain it is the smooth-growth bookkeeping of the thesis (5.2), in which identical collectors grow identically.

In our cloud

7 Turbulence, checked by a number

Stir a cup of tea with tea leaves in it. The leaves ride the swirls, but the heavier ones lag behind the flow and bump into the lighter ones. A cloud's turbulence does the same to drops: its shear moves neighbours past each other, and drops of different inertia answer its accelerations differently.

How strongly a drop feels the smallest eddies is measured by its response time against the eddies' turnover time, the Kolmogorov time τK​=ν/ε​, with ε the rate at which the turbulence dissipates energy per kg of air. Clouds have ε from about 10−3 to 10−1 m²/s³.

In our cloud
At ε=10−2 m²/s³, τK​ = 41 ms. An 8 µm droplet (response time 0.8 ms) follows the eddies almost perfectly; a 40 µm collector (18 ms) has a Stokes number of about 0.4.

Ayala, Rosa and Wang (2008) worked out the mean relative speed of two sedimenting drops in turbulence for weak inertia. For the pair that governs observable 1, 40 µm on 8 µm, their formula raises the kernel by 0.4 %, 5.6 % and 28 % at ε = 10, 100 and 400 cm²/s³. The gain comes from two effects of similar size: the fluid's accelerations, which the light droplet follows and the heavy collector does not (the tea leaves again), and the coupling of the drops' settling to the shear of the eddies. The second is the larger at 10 and 100 cm²/s³, the first at 400. Used inside its range (size ratio below 0.6, radii up to 60 µm, Stokes number below 1) in the collection equation, it shortens t90​ by 1.7 % at 100 cm²/s³: far less than the 3.0 min by which the collision efficiency moves t90​ (10.5 to 13.5 min, chapter 5).

That 1.7 % is a lower bound, for two reasons a student should see. The formula is switched off where it is not valid, for collectors above 60 µm, and at 400 cm²/s³ for every collector above 43 µm, whose Stokes number passes 1; that is why the stronger turbulence shortens t90​ by only 1.1 %, an artefact of the cut, not physics. And only the geometric part of the kernel is raised: simulations find that turbulence also raises the collision efficiency itself, by up to about a factor of 2 for size ratios like ours (Wang et al. 2008), and studies with the full turbulent kernel find a marked speed-up of warm rain (Grabowski and Wang 2013). So turbulence stays a stated check here, and t90​ in still air is an upper bound on the time in a turbulent cloud.

For observable 2 it is different. The first drizzle grows through mergers of nearly equal collectors (chapter 6), whose gravitational relative speed is small, and that is exactly where turbulence helps most: simulations find the collision rate of 18 and 20 µm drops doubled at 100 cm²/s³ and raised sevenfold at 400 (Wang et al. 2008). No calculation was found for drops of 50 to 60 µm, so these notes cannot give a number. Turbulence would make the first drizzle earlier than chapter 6 says; chapter 6's times are an upper bound for a turbulent cloud.

8 Ice: new particles from collisions

Shake ice cubes in a glass and small chips break off: the number of pieces rises, the mass of ice does not. Now picture a large supercooled drop, colder than 0 °C but still liquid, that touches a speck of ice. It freezes from the outside in; the shell traps the liquid core, the core expands as it freezes, and the shell may crack and spit splinters. Here the ice mass does grow (the drop's water becomes ice), and so may the number of pieces.

8.1 Counting new ice

The thesis asked whether collisions multiply ice. Its cold case is the same cloud at −10 °C and 600 hPa, with all 301 drops per cm³ of both modes split by the thesis's fit for the share that stays liquid when supercooled, 44.55 % at −10 °C: 134.1 liquid drops and 166.9 ice spheres (density 900 kg/m³) per cm³. Whatever the mechanism, new ice is counted the same way: how often a kind of event happens, times how many fragments each event makes,

dtdNnew​​=pairs∑​events per m3 and sKn1​n2​​​×fragments per eventF​​.
(8.1)

The event rates come from the kernel of chapters 1–4, with each phase's density, liquid freezing on contact with ice and ice sticking to ice. The fragments per event cannot be derived here: they come only from experiments. So the question becomes which experiments apply to these particles.

8.2 Three candidates

Rime splintering (Hallett and Mossop 1974): an ice particle collecting droplets throws off splinters while the rime freezes. They measured from −2 to −16 °C and found production peaking near −5 °C and stopping below about −8 °C (Korolev and Leisner 2020, Table 2). At −10 °C it is zero.

Shattering of freezing drops. In our cloud almost every such event (2.799 of 2.803 per cm³ and minute with drops of 25 µm radius or more) is a large supercooled drop sweeping up a small ice sphere and freezing, the picture above. That is the geometry of the laboratory experiments, which nucleated their drops with tiny ice crystals. The only free-fall study with drops of our size that reports both how often drops shatter and how many pieces they make, Brownscombe and Thorndike (1968, drops of 50–90 µm diameter), saw at most 14 % of drops shatter, with at most 12 fragments, both maxima over −5 to −15 °C. So a freezing drop makes between 0 and 0.14 × 12 = 1.7 fragments; both numbers are upper-leaning.

Breakup in ice–ice collisions. Phillips, Yano and Khain (2017) fitted a law to collisions of ice particles from 0.5 mm to 5 mm (coefficients as printed by Sotiropoulou et al. 2021). With K0​=21​μred​Δv2 the collision kinetic energy of chapter 4 and α=πD2 the surface of the smaller particle,

F=αA[1−exp(−(αACK0​​)γ)],γ=0.3,
(8.2)

where A is the number of breakable bumps per m² of ice surface and C (J⁻¹) how fragile they are. For dense, graupel-like ice (the nearest of the law's categories to our ice spheres) A=a0​[31​+max(32​−∣T−258 K∣/9 K, 0)] with a0​=3.78×104(1+0.0079/D1.5) m⁻² (D in metres; 0.428 a0​ at −10 °C), and C=6.3×106ψ J⁻¹, where ψ runs from 3.5×10−3 (a correction for sublimation, after Vardiman 1978) to 1 (Sotiropoulou et al. 2021, Appendix B). Read it as a saturation law: a hard hit breaks every bump, F→αA; a soft hit breaks a fraction. For soft hits, CK0​≪αA, the bracket is about (CK0​/αA)0.3, and the fragments grow only as the 0.3 power of the energy. That weak power is the danger: it keeps producing fragments however small the energy, so the answer depends on how far one trusts it below the experiments.

In our cloud: one collision worked through
A 40 µm ice sphere falls at 0.1726 m/s, an 8 µm one at 0.0076 m/s. Their collision energy is K0​=2.6×10−14 J. With D held at the law's lower limit of 0.5 mm inside A, as its users recommend (α stays the true surface of the 8 µm sphere), αA=9.2×10−3 and CK0​=1.6×10−7 (at C=6.3×106 J⁻¹), so F=3.4×10−4 fragments: one new splinter per three thousand such collisions. The old study's rule gave this collision 0.51 fragments.

Over all pairs, the ice–ice collisions in our cloud carry 1.3×10−14 to 2.3×10−13 J (5 % and 95 % of events). Two solid ice spheres of 0.5 and 1 mm diameter, the smallest particles (8.2) was fitted to, fall at 2.21 and 4.47 m/s and collide with K0​=1.3×10−7 J: our collisions carry 5.8 to 7.0 orders of magnitude less energy than even that gentlest case. Every number from (8.2) here is an extrapolation of that size.

9 What the model says, and what it does not

The three questions of chapter 0, answered for the thesis cloud in a closed box at 10 °C and 850 hPa:

  1. Most of the water sits in drops above 40 µm after 5.8 min (half) and 13.5 min (nine tenths), with t90​ anywhere from 10.5 to 13.5 min depending on the collision efficiency (11.4 min with Hall's table). The kernel decides the speed, and within it the collision efficiency first; the bookkeeping changes t90​ by 2.3 to 5.1 min.
  2. The first drizzle drop (100 µm) appears in a cubic metre after about a minute, in a litre after about three, and in 10 cm³ after about ten, in every exact run through mergers of collectors with each other. That is about ten times earlier than the time at which half the water is drizzle. Small volumes grow more slowly than the mean field.
  3. Ice: collisions of small ice spheres make 50 to 1600 times less new ice than the old study's rule assumed; shattering of freezing drops could make a few particles per cm³ per minute, or none, because the measured yields scatter that much.
What these notes leave out
  • No condensation: a rising cloud condenses 0.06 to 0.25 g/m³ of new water per minute at 0.5 to 2 m/s updraft (saturated air cooling along the moist adiabat, about 2 g/m³ per km of rise), as much as collection moves at the start. The closed box isolates collection; it is not a forecast.
  • No fall out of the box, no updraft, no cloud depth: F40​ is a growth measure, not rain at the ground.
  • The collision efficiency of nearly equal drops, which makes the first drizzle, is the least known ingredient; turbulence would raise it.
  • The initial spectrum's far tail decides the first drizzle in large volumes; we cut it at three standard deviations and say so.

Each of these is a further study in its own right.

1The runaway drop. With Stokes' law and E = 1, when does a 40 µm collector in 0.643 g/m³ of small droplets reach infinite radius, and why does no real drop do that?
At 21.1 minutes, and only on paper

From (1.3), t∗=1/(cR0​) with c=kW/(4ρw​). With k=1.2296×108 m⁻¹ s⁻¹, W=6.43×10−4 kg/m³ and R0​=40 µm, c=19.8 m⁻¹ s⁻¹ and t∗ = 21.1 min.

The blow-up comes from dR/dt∝R2, and that R2 comes from Stokes' speed. Above about 40 µm the drag balance replaces it (chapter 2) and the speed grows more slowly than R2, as R1.33 at 100 µm and roughly R1 near 0.3 mm, so the growth slows to about exponential; the droplets also run out, and millimetre drops break up. The lesson is not the infinity but its cause: growth that feeds on its own size.

2The smallest droplet that can hit. A 40 µm collector falls at 0.177 m/s. Ignoring the droplet's own size and weight, what is the smallest droplet radius that can reach its surface in potential flow, and in creeping flow?
1.2 µm in potential flow, 4.7 µm in creeping flow

A point droplet hits when St=τU/R exceeds the critical value: 1/12 in potential flow (3.4), 1.21194 in creeping flow. So τ≥Stc​R/U; in potential flow τ≥1.88×10−5 s. With Stokes' τ=2ρw​r2/(9μ), r=9μτ/(2ρw​)​ = 1.22 µm. In creeping flow the same steps give 4.67 µm.

The real air around a 40 µm collector (Re = 0.84) is much closer to creeping flow, which is why 8 µm droplets, only twice the creeping-flow limit, hit with an efficiency of just 0.344. Their own radius helps them: they touch when their centre is 8 µm from the surface.

3Why the thesis kernel stalls. A 500 µm drop meets a 250 µm drop. Compute the Weber number (4.2) with Stokes speeds and with drag-balance speeds.
We = 61 against 0.52: Stokes switches the merger off

The relative speed is 23.1 m/s with Stokes' law and 2.12 m/s with the drag balance. The collision energy grows as the square of the relative speed, so We grows by (23.1/2.12)2≈120: from 0.52 to 61. Then Ecoal​=e−1.15We is 0.55 against practically zero.

A wrong fall speed enters twice: once in the rate at which drops meet, once squared in whether they merge. With Stokes speeds the large drops of the thesis kernel stop merging with each other below a millimetre.

4Golovin's clock. For the sum kernel K=b(m+m′) the number of drops obeys dN/dt=−bLN. With b = 1.5 m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹ and L = 1 g/m³, what fraction of the drops is left after one hour?
0.45 %: the test spectrum is pushed far

From (5.2) with ϕ=1, the bracket is −1 and the double integral of b(m+m′)nn′ is 2bLN, so dN/dt=−bLN and N=N0​e−bLt. Here bLt = 5.40 and N/N0​ = 0.00452. The test is hard on the scheme: the mass peak moves from about 10 µm to about half a millimetre, and the scheme follows it with a spectrum error of 0.051 at 8 bins per doubling.

5A litre of cloud. Using (6.1), what concentration of drops above 100 µm must the mean field reach for half of all 1000 cm³ volumes to hold one?
693 per m³, reached after about three minutes

The median is where Vn100​=ln2, so n100​ = ln 2/(10⁻³ m³) = 693 per m³. The collection equation reaches it after 175 s; the 30 exact runs in 1000 cm³ gave a median of 175 s. In a whole cubic metre only 0.69 per m³ is needed, which the mean field reaches after about a minute: the first drizzle in a large volume comes from one-in-a-million luck.

One of these tests taught something worth passing on. The second, independent solver of the collection equation first gave t90​ = 18.3 min instead of 13.5. The physics was the same; the difference was one line of bookkeeping. A step may not take more drops out of a bin than it holds, and the first version counted every merger of a large drop as a loss to its bin. But a 1 mm drop that sweeps up an 8 µm droplet stays a 1 mm drop: it almost never leaves its bin, so its bin loses almost nothing. Counting the gross loss throttled exactly the largest, fastest-growing drops. Counted net, the two solvers agree to 0.013 min.

Golovin's exact solution

Chapter 5 tests the grid against the one kernel for which (5.1) can be solved exactly, the sum kernel K=b(m+m′). With it the gain term of (5.1) is 21​bm∫0m​n(m′)n(m−m′)dm′ (because m′+(m−m′)=m), and the loss term is bn(m)(mN+L), with N(t)=∫ndm the number of drops and L=∫mndm the water, which is constant. Take the Laplace transform F(p,t)=∫0∞​n(m,t)e−pmdm. A convolution becomes a product, and a factor m becomes −∂p​, so

∂t∂F​=−2b​∂p∂F2​+bN∂p∂F​−bLF.
(C.1)

At p=0, F=N, and (C.1) gives dN/dt=−bLN: the number falls as N=N0​e−bLt. Subtract that from (C.1) and write G=F−N:

∂t∂G​+bG∂p∂G​=−bLG.
(C.2)

This is Burgers' equation without viscosity, with a damping term: each value of G travels in p at the speed bG while it decays as e−bLt. Along such a characteristic, G=G0​(p0​)e−bLt and p=p0​+G0​(p0​)(1−e−bLt)/L. For the exponential start n0​=(N0​/mˉ)e−m/mˉ, G0​(p)=−N0​pmˉ/(1+pmˉ). Eliminating p0​ and transforming back to m is the step Golovin (1963) carried out; the result, with θ=1−e−bLt, is

n(m,t)=mθ​N0​(1−θ)​e−(1+θ)m/mˉI1​(mˉ2mθ​​),
(C.3)

with I1​ the modified Bessel function. It satisfies (5.1) (checked by substituting it numerically, and its number and water reproduce N0​e−bLt and L to 10−6). The spectrum error quoted in chapter 5 is ε=∑k​∣Mk​−Mkexact​∣/L, the summed difference of the water in each bin, as a fraction of all the water.
