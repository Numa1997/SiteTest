Project 07 · Electron optics

The Electron Beam, from Tip to Measurement

Numa Koudsie · lecture notes and simulations · every number computed

Question. Why does anyone build an electron beam, and what must the machine deliver? Chips have features a few nanometres wide and one defect can kill a chip, so every step is inspected and measured. Light cannot resolve such features; the electron wavelength at kilovolt energies is picometres. The beam is a probe scanned over the wafer, and what comes back gives edge position, height and material. The machine must give a spot thinner than the feature, enough current for a clean picture, a beam gentle enough for fragile resist, and speed, and these demands trade against each other. The chapters expose those trade-offs one by one.

Chapter 2The source

The chapter starts at the metal. It derives the barrier an electron meets outside a tungsten surface (applied field and image charge), the probability of tunnelling through it (WKB), the supply of electrons from the Fermi–Dirac gas, and from the three the Fowler–Nordheim, Richardson–Dushman and Schottky formulas, the energy spread and the reduced brightness of each source. It then follows the vacuum physics (Hertz–Knudsen flux, coverage kinetics, flash cleaning) that makes a cold tip bright and fragile at once. A published tungsten-tip data set and a published measurement of current decay against pressure are laid over the model and the differences are stated. Four numbers go to chapter 3: emission current, reduced brightness, energy spread, virtual source size. Assumed, not measured: the field factor k, the work functions, the desorption energy and the effect of an adsorbate; each is a control of the simulation.

2.1A needle, a wall and a leak

Every electron beam in this project starts at a piece of metal. We want to know what that piece of metal has to look like, what is applied to it, and what comes out, in numbers the next chapters can use.

Anyone who has touched a door handle on a dry winter day has met the main idea. The spark jumps from a fingertip and not from the flat of the hand, and a lightning rod is a pointed rod and not a plate. Charge on a conductor crowds where the surface curves sharply, and the electric field next to the surface grows with that crowding. A needle tip is the extreme case: a few tens of nanometres across, it turns a modest voltage into a field that is enormous on the scale of atoms.

2.1.1Work function and the wall

Inside a metal the conduction electrons fill states up to an energy called the Fermi level. We take that level as the zero of energy in this chapter. An electron at the Fermi level is not free: to take it to rest just outside the surface costs a definite energy, the work function φ. We picture a bowl of water whose surface is the Fermi level and whose rim is φ higher; φ is the height of the rim above the water. For clean tungsten we use φ=4.5 eV, the value Dolan and Dyke called "the accepted average value for clean tungsten" in 1954. One electronvolt is 1.602176634×10−19 J. We shall treat φ as a parameter that can be changed, because the whole story of this chapter depends on how sensitive the current is to it.

There are three ways to get an electron over or through the rim. Heat the metal until enough electrons have the energy to climb it (thermionic emission). Apply a field that lowers the rim a little (Schottky emission). Or apply a field so strong that the rim becomes a thin wall and the electrons tunnel through it (field emission). The cold source that Applied Materials describes publicly for its electron-beam metrology column is of the third kind; the Schottky source of the second kind is its main competitor. The three are one calculation, and we do it once.

2.1.2The field at the apex

Take a conducting sphere of radius r carrying charge Q and held at potential V relative to far away. Outside it the potential is that of a point charge, Q/(4πε0​ρ) at distance ρ from the centre, so V=Q/(4πε0​r). The field at the surface is Q/(4πε0​r2), and eliminating Q gives F=V/r. A real tip is a sphere sitting on a cone or a wire, with an anode at a finite distance, and the shank takes some of the potential drop. This is folded into one dimensionless factor k, of order unity to several, so that

F=krV​(2.1)

with k=1 for the isolated sphere. The value k=5 is used for tungsten tips by Chapman (1971) and by Ayari et al. (2023), whose data we use in section 2.9; we take it as an assumption, and the chapter says where it enters.

Example 2.1 · The field at a tip

A tip of radius r=50 nm at V=1000 V with k=5 has F=1000/(5×50×10−9)=4.00×109 V/m=4.0 V/nm. For scale, the field of a proton at one Bohr radius is 5.14×1011 V/m, so this is 1/129 of the field inside a hydrogen atom: a field that changes the energy of an electron by 4.0 eV over each nanometre. An electron at the Fermi level, in this field, has to climb a wall of height φ that falls by eF per unit length, which would reach the Fermi level after φ/(eF)=1.125 nm. That is the thickness of the wall it must tunnel through, a few atomic diameters.

Key result · the field of a needle

F=V/(kr): halving the radius doubles the field at the same voltage. With r in nanometres and a kilovolt applied, fields of several volts per nanometre are reached.

2.2The wall an electron sees

Outside the metal, an electron feels two things: the applied field, which pulls it away from the surface, and its own image, which pulls it back. We add the two energies.

2.2.1The image force

Put an electron at distance x from the plane surface of a conductor. The conduction electrons rearrange until the field just outside the surface is perpendicular to it. For the region outside the metal this arrangement is exactly what a single positive charge +e at distance x inside the metal, the mirror image of the electron, would produce. The force on the electron is therefore that of a charge +e at distance 2x:

Fim​(x)=−4πε0​(2x)2e2​=−16πε0​x2e2​(2.2)

The potential energy is minus the work needed to take the electron from x out to infinity against this attraction,

Uim​(x)=−∫x∞​16πε0​x′2e2​dx′=−16πε0​xe2​(2.3)

(One might expect e2/(8πε0​x), the energy of the real pair at separation 2x. It is twice as large, because as the electron moves away the induced charge in the metal rearranges, and only half of the pair's energy is stored in the field outside the surface. Integrating the force, as above, takes this into account.)

2.2.2The Schottky–Nordheim barrier

The applied field F, directed so that it pulls electrons out, contributes −eFx. Measuring all energies from the Fermi level, an electron at the Fermi level sees outside the surface

U(x)=φ−eFx−16πε0​xe2​(2.4)

This is the Schottky–Nordheim (SN) barrier. For F=0 it is the work function wall rounded off by the image force. For F>0 it falls linearly and is cut by the image term near the surface. Writing Q0​=e2/(16πε0​), the energy U is zero where eFx2−φx+Q0​=0, that is at

x1,2​=2eFφ​(1∓1−f​),f=φ24eFQ0​​=4πε0​φ2e3F​(2.5)

The dimensionless number f is the scaled field. The barrier exists as long as f<1; at f=1 the two roots merge and the top of the barrier touches the Fermi level. The field at which this happens, FR​=4πε0​φ2/e3, is 14.06 V/nm for φ=4.5 eV, so f=F/FR​. Setting dU/dx=0 gives the top of the barrier at xm​=Q0​/(eF)​, where Umax​=φ−Δφ with the Schottky lowering

Δφ=4πε0​e3F​​=φf​,Δφ=1.2000 eV×F/(V/nm)​(2.6)

The derivation of x1,2​, of f, and of Δφ was checked symbolically (checks C1 and C2 in section 2.13).

Example 2.2 · The barrier at F = 4 V/nm

Here f=0.2844, the roots are x1​=0.087 nm and x2​=1.038 nm, and the top of the barrier is Δφ=2.40 eV below the zero-field rim, at 2.10 eV above the Fermi level. A Fermi-level electron meets a barrier x2​−x1​=1.04−0.087≈0.95 nm wide.

Where the model is not trusted. The image term diverges at the surface, and at x1​ of less than a tenth of a nanometre the picture of a smooth classical image charge is no longer meaningful. The standard treatment, which we follow, simply uses the barrier between its two roots; the fact that the result depends only on the region x1​<x<x2​, where the barrier is above the electron, hides the problem. It is one of the reasons the current in section 2.9 is predicted only to a factor, not to a percent.

2.3Leaking through: the WKB transmission

A wave that meets a barrier higher than its energy does not stop at the edge. Inside the barrier it dies away exponentially, and what is left at the far side leaves as an electron. We now compute how much is left.

Let an electron approach the surface with energy w of motion normal to it. We write the time-independent Schrödinger equation for that motion,

−2mℏ2​ψ′′(x)+U(x)ψ(x)=wψ(x)(2.7)

In the barrier, U>w, so ψ′′=κ2ψ with κ(x)=2m(U(x)−w)​/ℏ. If κ were constant the solution that decays towards larger x would be e−κx. When κ varies slowly, ∣dκ/dx∣≪κ2, the WKB approximation replaces κx by ∫κdx:

ψ(x)≈ψ(x1​)exp(−∫x1​x​κ(x′)dx′)(2.8)

The probability of finding the electron, ∣ψ∣2, falls by the square of that factor between the two turning points x1​ and x2​. The transmission probability is taken as this ratio:

D(w)≈e−G(w),G(w)=ℏ2​∫x1​x2​​2m(U(x)−w)​dx(2.9)

The approximation fails close to the turning points, where κ→0, so D is only reliable when G is large, and the prefactor that belongs to the exact solution is dropped; for the barriers that give currents we care about, G is of order 10 (the exponent in Example 2.2 is 10.7) and the missing prefactor is a factor of order one that is absorbed in the uncertainty named in section 2.9. Allaham et al. (2022) give the same step in their equations (3) and (4), with the constant 2κe​=10.246 eV−1/2nm−1 which is 22m(1eV)​/ℏ; we recomputed it.

2.3.1The triangular barrier

Without the image term the barrier is U=φ−eFx, which meets the Fermi level (w=0) at x=φ/(eF). Then

G△​=ℏ2​∫0φ/eF​2m(φ−eFx)​dx=3ℏe42m​​Fφ3/2​≡bFφ3/2​(2.10)

The integral is 32​φ3/2/(eF) times 2m​ times 2/ℏ. The constant b=42m​/(3ℏe) is the second Fowler–Nordheim constant; it equals 6.8309 eV−3/2Vnm−1 (Forbes quotes 6.830 890; check C3). In words: G grows as the wall gets higher (φ3/2) and shrinks as the field gets stronger (1/F). With the example numbers G△​=16.30.

2.3.2With the image term: the function v(f) ★

With the image term, U−w=eF(x−x1​)(x2​−x)/x between the roots (the roots depend on the energy through φ−w in place of φ). We put x=x1​+(x2​−x1​)sin2ϑ with 0≤ϑ≤π/2, so that (x−x1​)(x2​−x)=Δ2sin2ϑcos2ϑ, dx=2Δsinϑcosϑdϑ, Δ=x2​−x1​:

G=ℏ22m​​eF​∫0π/2​x1​+Δsin2ϑ​2Δ2sin2ϑcos2ϑ​dϑ≡bFφ3/2​v(f)(2.11)

which has no singularity and is evaluated by Gauss–Legendre quadrature (48 points, in ref/ch02.py and physics/ch02.js). The right-hand side defines v(f), a correction factor that depends only on f: it equals 1 for f→0 (no image term) and falls to 0 at f=1, where the barrier has disappeared. In the older literature it is written with the elliptic integrals of Nordheim; Forbes (2006) found that v≈1−f+61​flnf is good to 0.0024 everywhere on 0≤f≤1. Our quadrature agrees: the largest difference on a grid of 199 values is 0.00238 (check C7).

v(f)≈1−f+6f​lnf(2.12)

For the working point, v=0.6538 by quadrature and 0.6560 from the approximation.

Example 2.3 · The transmission at the working point

G=bφ3/2v/F=16.30×0.6538=10.66 and D=e−G=2.35×10−5. The image force changes G from 16.30 to 10.66 and therefore multiplies the transmission by e16.30−10.66=283. Of every 42532 electrons that arrive at the wall one gets out.

2.4Counting the electrons that arrive: the supply function

The transmission is a probability per electron. To get a current we need to know how many electrons strike the surface per second, with which normal energy, and that is a count in the metal.

We treat the conduction electrons as a gas of free particles with the Fermi–Dirac occupation F(E)=[1+e(E−μ)/kT]−1, with μ=0 the Fermi level. A momentum cell of size d3p holds 2d3p/h3 electron states (the 2 is the spin). Electrons with velocity component vx​ normal to the surface, directed at it, reach unit area of surface per unit time at the rate vx​ times their density. So the current density of electrons that arrive with the momenta in d3p is

dJin​=eh32​F(E)vx​d3p(2.13)

Split E=w+εt​ into the normal energy w=px2​/2m and the transverse energy εt​=(py2​+pz2​)/2m. Then vx​dpx​=dw, and the transverse momentum element is dpy​dpz​=πd(pt2​)=2πmdεt​. A fraction D(w) of what arrives is transmitted. Adding up,

J=e∫dwD(w)h34πm​∫0∞​F(w+εt​)dεt​=e∫N(w)D(w)dw(2.14)

(spin 2 times 2πm gives 4πm). The inner integral is elementary, because ∫w∞​FdE=kTln(1+e−w/kT). It defines the supply function, the number of electrons per unit area, time and energy that arrive with normal energy w:

N(w)=h34πmkT​ln(1+e−w/kT)(2.15)

The check C4 verifies the derivative that defines it. For w≪−kT, N→4πm(−w)/h3: at low temperature the number of arriving electrons grows linearly with how far below the Fermi level they sit, and N drops to zero above it on a scale kT.

Key result · the master formula of this chapter

J=e∫N(w)D(w)dw, with N from (2.15) and D=e−G from (2.9). Fowler–Nordheim, Richardson–Dushman and Schottky emission are three limits of it; the code of this page evaluates it numerically, without limits, wherever it needs to.

One assumption is hidden here and we state it: D depends only on the energy w of the normal motion, not on εt​. That holds for a plane, smooth surface in an isotropic free-electron metal. It fails for a real crystal face, where the band structure and the surface potential differ from face to face (this is why different tungsten planes emit differently).

2.5The Fowler–Nordheim equation

At low temperature the master formula can be done by hand. The result is the equation that the experimenters of the 1950s used to count nothing but volts and amperes.

2.5.1Linearising the exponent

At T→0 only electrons with w<0 exist and N=4πm(−w)/h3. Because D=e−G changes so fast with w, only energies within a fraction of an electronvolt of the Fermi level matter, and we expand G there to first order:

G(w)≈GF​−dw​,d1​=−dwdG​​w=0​(2.16)

with GF​=G(0), the exponent computed in section 2.3. The slope: raising w by a small amount lowers the barrier height seen from the electron by the same amount. Because the image term does not depend on w, that is the same as lowering φ at fixed F, so dG/dw=−∂G/∂φ. With G=bφ3/2v(f)/F and f∝φ−2 (so df/dφ=−2f/φ),

∂φ∂G​=Fb​[23​φ1/2v−2fφ3/2φv′​]=2F3bφ​​t(f),t(f)≡v−34​fv′(f)(2.17)

For the triangular barrier v=1, t=1, and d=2F/(3bφ​)=ℏeF/(22mφ​), which is the decay length of the electron's states in energy: d is an energy, 0.175 eV at the working point. Inserting Forbes' v gives

t(f)≈1+9f​−18f​lnf(2.18)

(check C6 does the algebra symbolically; check C8 confirms ∂G/∂φ numerically by differencing the quadrature of section 2.3: the formula with the exact t agrees to 2.5×10−11, and Forbes' t(f) is 0.32 % off).

2.5.2The integral

At T=0, J=eh34πm​e−GF​∫−∞0​(−w)ew/ddw, and ∫−∞0​(−w)ew/ddw=d2 (integration by parts, check C5). So

J0​=h34πme​d2e−GF​(2.19)

Inserting d=ℏeF/(22mφ​t), ℏ=h/2π, and GF​=bφ3/2v/F, the constants combine (check C5):

J0​=8πhe3​φt2(f)F2​exp(−v(f)Fbφ3/2​)(2.20)

Example 2.4 · Fowler–Nordheim current density at the working point

At F=4 V/nm and φ=4.5 eV: f=0.2844, v=0.6538, t=1.0515, bφ3/2/F=16.30. Then J0​=(1.5414×10−6/4.5)×42/1.0512×e−10.66 A/nm², which is 1.13×108 A/m2. The full integral of section 2.4, without any approximation, gives 1.16×108 A/m2, at 300 K.

2.5.3Finite temperature ★

With the linearised D and the full N, the integral is J=e(4πmkT/h3)e−GF​∫ln(1+e−w/kT)ew/ddw. Integrating by parts (the boundary terms vanish for d>kT) turns it into kTd​∫ew/d/(1+ew/kT)dw, and ∫−∞∞​eau/(1+eu)du=π/sinπa for 0<a<1, with u=w/kT and a=kT/d. Hence

J(T)=J0​sin(πkT/d)πkT/d​(2.21)

(check C9 evaluates both sides numerically at 30 digits). At the working point, 300 K, πkT/d=0.464 and the factor is 1.037, a 4 % correction. The factor diverges when πkT/d→π, that is when kT→d, and is a poor description well before that: electrons far above the Fermi level, where the barrier is thinner, then dominate and the linearisation of G fails. That is the transition to thermal-field emission; there we integrate numerically.

2.5.4The Fowler–Nordheim plot

Experimenters measure current I at voltage V, not J at F. If the emitting area A is constant and F=V/ζ (so ζ=kr is the voltage conversion length), I=AJ0​ gives

lnV2I​=lnφt2ζ2Aa​−bφ3/2ζVv(f)​(2.22)

Plotted against 1/V, the right-hand side is a straight line to a good approximation. Its slope is d[v(f)/V]/d(1/V) times −bφ3/2ζ, and because f∝V the derivative is v−fv′=s(f). For Forbes' v this is exactly s(f)=1−f/6 (check C6b). So

SFN​=−bφ3/2ζ(1−6f​)(2.23)

A measured slope gives ζ if φ is known, and then r=ζ/k if k is. Section 2.9 does this with real data.

2.6Heat helps: Richardson–Dushman and Schottky emission

Warm the same metal and the supply of energetic electrons in the tail of the Fermi function takes over from tunnelling. The master formula gives that limit in one line.

For electrons with w above the top of the barrier, D≈1, and ln(1+e−w/kT)≈e−w/kT when w≫kT. With no field the top is at w=φ:

J=e∫φ∞​h34πmkT​e−w/kTdw=h34πmek2​T2e−φ/kT≡AR​T2e−φ/kT(2.24)

with AR​=1.20173e+06 Am−2K−2 (check C10; the textbook value is 1.2017×106). This is the Richardson–Dushman law. A field lowers the top of the barrier by the Schottky lowering Δφ of (2.6) and the emission is that of a work function φ−Δφ:

J=AR​T2exp(−kTφ−Δφ​)(2.25)

For our Schottky preset we choose φ=2.9 eV for the zirconium-oxide-coated surface (an assumed value; the Hitachi page cited below says only that the coating lowers the work function), T=1800 K, and a field F=0.5 V/nm at the apex (we choose k so that this holds; this is an assumed operating point, not a quoted specification). Then kT=0.155 eV and Δφ=0.849 eV. The field raises the current by a factor eΔφ/kT=238 over plain Richardson emission (2.96×104 to 7.02×106 A/m2). The full integral gives 8.14×106 A/m2, 16 % above the Schottky formula; the excess is tunnelling through the top of the lowered barrier.

2.6.1One formula, three regimes

Dolan and Dyke (1954) did the integral of the master formula numerically, for a wide range of temperature, field and work function, and showed that it contains both the Fowler–Nordheim equation (cold, strong field) and the Richardson formula (hot, weak field). Our code does the same calculation (check C11: against the analytic Fowler–Nordheim formula the full integral is within 2.4 % at 4 and 5 V/nm; against Richardson–Schottky at 2800 K and 0.1 V/nm it is within 0.5 %). The regime is read from where the peak of the emitted electrons' energy distribution lies (section 2.7): below the Fermi level the source is a cold field emitter; between the Fermi level and the top of the barrier it is in the thermal-field regime; at the top, thermionic or Schottky.

2.7How wide is the energy: the spread ΔE

Lenses bend electrons of different energies by different amounts. The energy spread of the source therefore sets a blur in the probe (chapter 5), and it is the first of the four numbers we hand over.

Take electrons of total energy ε=w+εt​ leaving the surface. For each total energy, any normal energy w≤ε is allowed (the transverse part ε−w cannot be negative). Using F(w+εt​)=F(ε) in the integral (2.14) and changing variables from εt​ to ε,

P(ε)=F(ε)∫−∞ε​D(w)dw,J=eh34πm​∫P(ε)dε(2.26)

(the second relation, which the code checks, follows from integrating (2.14) by parts; check C12: ratio of the two currents 1+2.3e−05). With the linearised barrier, D∝ew/d, the inner integral is deε/d times constants and

P(ε)∝1+eε/kTeε/d​(2.27)

In words: the electrons that leave have energies spread from below the Fermi level (rising as eε/d: lower-lying electrons see a thicker barrier) to a cut-off at the Fermi level, smeared by kT. The peak is at ε∗=kTln[kT/(d−kT)] when d>2kT, which is −0.046 eV at the working point, just below the Fermi level. The full width at half maximum ΔE follows from the two roots of P=21​Pmax​: with d=0.175 eV and kT=0.0259 eV it is 0.218 eV from (2.27) and 0.215 eV from the full integral (check C13).

Because d∝F/φ​, the width grows with the field: ΔE=0.184, 0.215 and 0.245 eV at 3, 4 and 5 V/nm. A cold source gains in current by running at higher field and pays in energy width. For the Schottky preset the full integral gives ΔE=0.43 eV: the distribution is broad because it is a thermal tail above the barrier top, and its peak lies 0.15 eV above the top.

Against published values. A public Hitachi High-Tech page (8 May 2026), written for buyers of electron microscopes, quotes "around 0.2–0.3 eV" for cold field emission and "around 0.6–1.1 eV" for Schottky sources. Our cold value, 0.21 eV, lies in the first range and was not fitted to it. Our Schottky value, 0.43 eV, lies below the second range. The model contains no electron–electron interaction in the beam and no distribution of fields over the emitting area. Both widen the measured distribution (inference, not tested here); the vendor page is a marketing source and gives no conditions, so we use it only to show that the model is in the right range for the cold source and incomplete for the Schottky one.

2.8Brightness and the virtual source

A tip that emits a large current into a huge angle is useless for a probe, because only the electrons that go through a small aperture count. The quantity that matters is the current per unit area and per unit solid angle, the brightness.

The brightness B of a source is the current dI that comes from the area dA into the solid angle dΩ: B=dI/(dAdΩ). Chapter 3 shows (Liouville) that B/V does not change as the beam is accelerated through V (non-relativistically), so we give the reduced brightness Br​=B/V, in A m−2 sr−1 V−1.

2.8.1From the energy of the sideways motion to the angle

An electron with transverse energy εt​ that has been accelerated to the energy eV moves at a small angle α to the axis with α2≈εt​/(eV) (ratio of the transverse to the total kinetic energy). Electrons with transverse energy between εt​ and εt​+dεt​ therefore fill the solid angle dΩ=πd(α2)=πdεt​/(eV). Counting those electrons as in (2.14) but without integrating over εt​ gives the current density per unit transverse energy,

j(εt​)=eh34πm​∫D(w)F(w+εt​)dw(2.28)

so B=jπeV​ at εt​→0, where the direction is along the axis, and

Br​=πe​j(εt​=0)(2.29)

with j per joule, so ej is per electronvolt. For the cold limit, F is a step and j(εt​)=(J/d)e−εt​/d (integrate e−GF​ew/d up to w=−εt​ and compare with (2.19)). The mean transverse energy is then d, and

Br​=πdJ​(d in eV)(2.30)

At 4 V/nm this gives 2.11×108, the full integral 2.17×108 Am−2sr−1V−1 (check C14). The model's mean transverse energy is 0.168 eV (cold) and 0.155 eV (Schottky preset). The reduced brightness of the Schottky preset is 1.67×107, a factor 13 below the cold preset: the cold source wins by its current density, 1.16×108 against 8.14×106 A/m2, at similar transverse energy.

These are numbers for the emitting patch itself. A published reduced brightness of an operating source also includes everything between the patch and the measurement, and is dealt with in chapter 3, where we put published values next to ours. The vendor page of section 2.7 gives a brightness "up to about 109 A/cm²·sr" for cold field emission and "high, but generally lower" for Schottky, with no voltage; it is not a reduced brightness and we do not compare it with ours.

2.8.2The virtual source

The electrons do not all seem to come from the same point. Follow the backward extension of the straight path each electron has far away: these lines pass through a small disc, the virtual source, which is the object the lenses image. A first estimate comes from conservation of angular momentum. In a field that points along the radius from the apex centre, an electron leaving the sphere of radius r with transverse momentum pt​=2mεt​​ keeps L=pt​r for ever. Far away its momentum is p∞​=2meV​ and the extended path passes the centre at the distance

b=p∞​L​=reVεt​​​(2.31)

(check C17: a Runge–Kutta integration of the trajectory in a 1/r potential, to 2 mm, finds 0.70687 nm against 0.70686 nm). We define the virtual source diameter as dv​=2r⟨εt​⟩/(eV)​, with the mean transverse energy of the model. For the cold preset (r=50 nm, V=1000 V) it is 1.30 nm; for the Schottky preset (r=500 nm, V=2000 V) it is 8.8 nm. The Hitachi page quotes typical virtual source sizes of 2.5 to 5 nm (cold) and 15 to 30 nm (Schottky). Ours are lower for both; the model has a perfectly radial field, no extractor lens and an emitting patch of zero size. All three would increase dv​ (inference). We treat dv​ as a lower bound.

2.9Checking the model against a published tungsten tip

A model that has not met a measurement is a story. Here it meets one: the current–voltage curve of a tungsten tip measured by Ayari, Vincent, Perisanu, Poncharal and Purcell in 2023, whose raw numbers are public.

The measurement (their section III). A ⟨111⟩ tungsten tip, electrochemically etched from a 125 µm wire, initial radius about 15 nm, heated several times for 30 s at 1700 K. Base pressure 3×10−10 Torr (4×10−8 Pa). The current was collected by a microchannel plate and phosphor screen, in the range from femtoampere to picoampere; the voltage was swept five times up and down between 1300 V and 1630 V in steps of 10 V. We use their sweep "run 4, down" (33 points; data in data/ch02/ayari2023_w111_iv.csv, licence CC BY 4.0, provenance in the README of that folder).

What we did. (i) A straight line through ln(I/V2) against 1/V gives slope −41339 V and intercept 4.578, which reproduces the authors' own fit (41 339 ± 73 V, 4.57 ± 0.05; check C19) and so confirms that we read the units of the file correctly (current in nanoampere). (ii) With φ=4.5 eV, which the authors also assume, (2.23) with s(f)=0.974 at the middle of the range gives ζ=651 nm. (iii) The full fit of I=AJ(V/ζ) to all 33 points, with the temperature factor and 300 K, gives ζ=657.5 nm, an emitting area A=781 nm2, and a root-mean-square deviation of lnI of 1.8 %, largest deviation 5.8 %. With k=5 the radius is r=ζ/k=132 nm. The authors' own analysis with the Murphy–Good model has 653 nm and 131 nm for the same sweep (their file paramIV4d.txt), so the two calculations agree.

2.9.1Where model and data differ

The data do not fix the work function. The fit quality is the same, rms 0.0185 in lnI, for any φ between 4.0 and 5.5 eV; only ζ changes, because the slope fixes the product φ3/2ζ. For φ=4.0, 4.5, 5.0 and 5.5 eV the fit gives ζ=788, 658, 560 and 484 nm, so the apex radius deduced from a slope inherits the full uncertainty of φ and of k. The authors of the data make this point more strongly in their title and abstract. The model is not wrong here; the measurement is not sensitive enough to test it for φ.

The residuals. The model describes the curve to within 1.8 % rms over a range in which the current changes by a factor 752. The deviations look irregular. The authors report that the current at a fixed voltage drifts by about 8 % per hour (their Fig. 4); in the data file the current at 1470 V in the ten sweeps in time order is 0.000161, 0.000154, 0.00016, 0.000158, 0.00015, 0.00015, 0.000134, 0.000126, 0.000145, 0.000135 nA. Our residuals are of the size of that drift and the noise they quote (1.8 % standard deviation about their line), so the model has no deviation to explain that the measurement does not already contain (inference).

The radius. The fit gives a tip of 130 nm radius if k=5, against 15 nm initially. The authors expect the radius to increase because of the repeated heating; the field-emission measurement alone cannot say. It does not contradict the model, but it shows what the model cannot do: it does not predict the geometry.

The scaled field. The fit places the data at f=0.142 to 0.176 (F=1.99 to 2.48 V/nm), at the lower edge of the range that Forbes and co-workers call physically reasonable for a tungsten tip, 0.15≤f≤0.45, and well above the "clearly unreasonable" limit 0.10 (Allaham et al. 2022, their Table 1). The classical tungsten data set of Dyke and Trolan (1953, emitter X89), which Forbes (2013) re-analysed, covers 0.20<f<0.34; our working point f=0.28 lies inside that range. We did not open the paper of Dyke and Trolan (only its abstract is public), so no number from it enters this chapter.

2.10Vacuum and contamination

The current of a cold tip depends on φ so steeply that a few foreign atoms on the apex change it. How fast they arrive, and whether they leave again, is decided by the pressure and the temperature.

2.10.1How many molecules arrive: Hertz–Knudsen

The residual gas at temperature Tg​ and pressure p has density n=p/kTg​ and Maxwell–Boltzmann velocities, f(v)=(mg​/2πkTg​)3/2e−mg​v2/2kTg​. The molecules that hit a surface of unit area per second are those moving towards it, weighted by their normal speed:

Φ=n∫vx​>0​vx​f(v)d3v=n2πmg​kTg​​​=2πmg​kTg​​p​(2.32)

(check C15 does the three integrals symbolically). This is the Hertz–Knudsen flux. For hydrogen (mg​=2.016 u) at 300 K and 10−9 Pa, Φ=1.07×1014 m−2s−1; for carbon monoxide (28 u) 2.87×1013 m−2s−1. A surface of tungsten carries about Ns​=1/a2 atoms per unit area; with the lattice constant a=0.3165 nm this is 9.98×1018 m−2, and we use Ns​=1019 m−2. Dividing, the time to deposit one monolayer if every molecule sticks is Ns​/Φ:

2.10.2The rate equation

Let θ be the fraction of the apex sites that carry an adsorbed atom. Molecules arrive at Φ, a fraction s of those that hit a free site stick, and an adsorbed atom leaves at the rate kd​ per second. We allow a factor κ≥1 for gas that is collected on the shank and diffuses to the apex (κ=1 in the default). Then

dtdθ​=a(1−θ)−kd​θ,a=κsNs​Φ​,kd​=ν0​e−Ed​/kT(2.33)

This is first-order Langmuir kinetics: no interaction between adatoms, no precursor state, one adsorbate per site. The desorption rate has the Arrhenius form with an attempt frequency ν0​=1013 s−1 and a desorption energy Ed​; both are assumptions, discussed below. The equation has the solution (check C16, SymPy)

θ(t)=θ∞​+(θ0​−θ∞​)e−(a+kd​)t,θ∞​=a+kd​a​(2.34)

and the time to reach a coverage θ∗<θ∞​ from a clean surface is t∗=−ln(1−θ∗/θ∞​)/(a+kd​). When kd​ is negligible, t∗≈θ∗Ns​/(κsΦ) and, because Φ∝p, pt∗ is a constant. This is the prediction we test.

2.10.3Emission as a function of coverage (a model assumption)

Model assumption

We assume that the emitting surface is a patchwork: the fraction 1−θ with the clean work function φ, the fraction θ with φ+Δφads​. The current is the sum of the two contributions, I/I0​=(1−θ)+θρ with ρ=J(φ+Δφads​)/J(φ) at the same field. This is a choice made for simplicity; nothing in the derivation forces it. We take Δφads​=1.0 eV, which gives ρ=0.0021 at the working point; it is not a measured value. What is measured (Cho 2014, below) is that adsorption of residual gas, mostly hydrogen, lowers the current and that this is attributed to an increase of the work function.

How strongly the current reacts to the work function is a result of the Fowler–Nordheim equation, not of this assumption. The logarithmic derivatives at the working point are ∂lnJ/∂lnF=17.5 and ∂lnJ/∂lnφ=−26.6. A rise of φ by 0.1 eV (2.2 %) cuts the current density by the factor 0.55; a rise by 1 eV cuts it by 0.0021. A field higher by 10 % raises it by 4.9.

2.10.4The rate equation against a measurement

Cho (2014) measured the probe current of a W(310) tip in an extreme-high-vacuum chamber, after flash heating, while the pressure was changed with a getter pump. The residual gas was more than 99 % hydrogen (his Fig. 5). The probe current stays near its initial value for a time τ90​, until it has dropped by 10 %, and then falls quickly. He reports τ90​=15, 105 and 450 min at 2×10−8, 2–3×10−9 and 6–8×10−10 Pa (gauge readings; we take 2.5 and 7×10−10 Pa), and that the damping rate is proportional to the pressure. The products pτ90​ are 3.00×10−7, 2.63×10−7 and 3.15×10−7 Pa·min: constant within ±9 % over a factor 29 in pressure, as (2.33) with the Hertz–Knudsen flux says. Taking θ∗=0.1 for the 10 % drop (patch model with ρ≈0) and mg​=2.016 u, the product κs that each point needs is 0.52, 0.59 and 0.49, mean 0.54. This mean is what we use as the default of the simulation.

What this does and does not show. The data confirm the scaling τ∝1/p over one and a half decades. The size of the constant fixes one number, κs≈0.5, and so does not test the model independently: any combination of sticking, dissociation of H2 into two atoms, and θ∗ that multiplies to the same product fits. Cho notes also that the "stable region" is specific to the (310) plane and disappears when gases other than hydrogen are present; the model has no plane dependence and no species dependence beyond the flux. His own constant CCH​=1.93×10−7 Pa·min refers to a 50 % drop of the total emission current and is a different quantity.

2.10.5The flash-cleaning cycle

At room temperature kd​ is negligible: for Ed​=1.9 eV, kd​=1.2×10−19 s−1, so adsorbed atoms stay as long as the experiment lasts. This is why a cold tip cannot clean itself. Heating the tip for a few seconds changes the rate by many orders of magnitude: at 700 K, kd​=0.21 s−1; at 1000 K, 2657 s−1; at 2000 K, 1.6×108 s−1. A flash of 5 s at 1000 K therefore removes everything (e−kd​t≈0). The values Ed​=1.9 eV and u0​=1013 s−1 are assumptions, chosen so that hydrogen-like adsorbates leave a hot tip in about a second near 700 K and stay at room temperature; they are not measured for this tip. The cycle is then: run until I/I0​ falls below a threshold, flash, run again. With the threshold 0.7 the model flashes after 104 min at 10−8 Pa and hydrogen, and for the other pressures (same assumptions) after:

One atom. Applied Materials states publicly that "a single atom" on the tip can partly block the emission. In our patch model an adsorbed atom blocks only its own site, which at our working point is one of 8201 sites in the effective emitting area of 820 nm2 (current divided by current density), so a single atom changes the current by 1.2×10−4. The model does not reproduce the claim for a tip of this size. It does for a tip whose emission is concentrated on a few atoms, where one atom is a large fraction of the emitting area, and when an adsorbate changes φ over a region larger than its own footprint. The model has neither; this is a limit of the model that we do not hide (see section section 2.13).

2.11Why the cold tip is bright but fragile

The two properties come from the same exponent, and it is easier to see them together.

The tip is bright because of two concentrations. The field concentrates: F=V/(kr) turns a kilovolt into 4 V/nm. And the current concentrates: lnJ varies as F17 locally, so electrons come out of a tiny patch (our effective emitting area is 820 nm², 5 % of the hemisphere, 15708 nm2) with a small transverse energy (0.17 eV). A large current density on a tiny area, with a narrow distribution of angles, is a high brightness.

The tip is fragile because of the same exponent seen from the other side. The current depends on the work function with the logarithmic derivative −27: one adsorbed atom that raises the local work function by 0.1 eV removes 45 % of the current from where it sits, and by 1 eV removes 99.8 %. At 10−9 Pa a surface of tungsten receives a monolayer of hydrogen in 25.9 h (all molecules sticking) and the current falls by 10 % in a few hours. A Schottky emitter is hot, the adsorbates leave as fast as they come, and the current is steady; it pays with a lower brightness and a larger energy spread. A cold emitter has to be flashed.

2.13.2What the model leaves out

Free-electron metal, no band structure and no crystal face dependence. A planar barrier with a classical image charge, and the WKB transmission without its prefactor. A hemispherical cap with F∝cosϑ, and k as a free number; no field solver (a proper one is outside this project). Contamination as one species, first-order kinetics, no surface diffusion, with Ed​, ν0​, κ and Δφads​ assumed, and s obtained from one data set. No tip blunting by the high-temperature flash, no ion bombardment of the tip, no emission-pattern change. Each omission is a place where a real column's source module differs; none is a claim about any particular product.
