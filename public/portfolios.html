Bachelor thesis, Leipzig 2022 · remodelled

Droplet Collection

How cloud droplets become rain

A large drop falls faster than a small one and sweeps it up; that is how a cloud of tiny droplets can turn into rain within minutes. Numa Koudsie’s Bachelor thesis, Collisional Ice Multiplication Processes in Clouds (Leipzig, 2022), modelled this collection and the ice splinters it can produce, with Stokes’ law for the fall speed and the geometric cross-section for the chance of a hit. The remodel replaces each ingredient with physics that holds at the sizes that matter: a drag balance for the fall speed, collision efficiencies from the trajectory of the small droplet in the air flow around the large one, and the full stochastic collection equation for the bookkeeping. Every step is derived on the theory page and tested on the checks page, and the solver runs in your browser.

1.1The warm cloud

The test case is a warm cloud of \(300\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) droplets of radius \(8\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) with \(1\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) collectors of \(40\ \mathrm{\mu m}\), at \(10\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and \(850\ \mathrm{hPa}\). Growth is measured by the mass fraction in drops of radius \(r \ge 40\ \mathrm{\mu m}\): a diagnostic of growth, not a rainfall prediction. With the thesis kernel the mass piles up near \(500\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) and stops, because the Weber number computed with Stokes speeds switches coalescence off; with the extended kernel the drops keep growing to millimetres. The kernel matters more than the bookkeeping.

1.2What changed, one line each

  1. Fall speed. Stokes’ law gives \(\mathrm{Re}\approx 150\) for a \(200\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) drop, outside its own validity. A force balance with Beard’s drag coefficient \(C_D(\mathrm{Re})\), solved through the Davies number, reproduces the measurements of Gunn and Kinzer within 2.1 % from 0.3 mm up.
  2. Collision efficiency. The small droplet obeys \(\tau\,\dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf u - \mathbf v - \tau g^{*}\mathbf e_z\) in the flow around the collector; scaled by \(R\) and \(U\) this is the Stokes number \(\mathrm{St} = \tau U/R\). The grazing trajectory gives \(E\); the solver reproduces the critical Stokes numbers \(1/12\) and \(1.21\).
  3. Bookkeeping. The thesis’s two fixed populations become one spectrum obeying the Smoluchowski collection equation; the thesis equations are recovered as its limit of rare collectors.
  4. Numerics. A logarithmic mass grid, pair fluxes computed in place, a deposit that conserves number and mass, and a limiter that is derived rather than guessed; tested against Golovin’s exact solution, with a mass error of \(10^{-7}\) and a spectrum error of 5 % at 8 bins per doubling.
  5. Ice. Splinter fragments join the interacting population. The app’s fragmentation is event-based: a fixed mass fraction \(\lambda\) per ice–ice collision and a fixed fragment mass. The thesis’s code drains mass at a random rate per second instead, so the cold runs test the app’s closure, not the thesis’s model. With fragments in the race the ice number grows from 167 to 1 700–2 100 cm⁻³ in an hour and the mass above 40 µm falls to 0.2–4 %: the closure, not the solver, drives that result.

rotating mechanics · energy harvesting · nonlinear dynamics

Rotor Pendula

Can a swinging weight on a turbine blade power a sensor?

Hang a 100 g weight on a 20 cm rod from a point on a turning blade. Each time the blade goes round, gravity pulls the weight first one way and then the other, so it swings once per turn, like a child on a swing pushed once per pass; a generator at the pivot brakes that swing and turns it into electricity. The study builds everything from the pendulum’s Lagrangian: the equations of motion, the energy books that say the rotor pays for every milliwatt, the driven-oscillator resonance at \(r_p = L\) with the best generator setting \(\kappa = |R-1|\), real turbine sizes from the tip-speed ratio, the double pendulum, chaos, and a sensor budget from two datasheets. An independent audit rebuilt the model from scratch and reproduced every headline number; the solver runs in your browser. Sol’s whole report is included, word for word, with its nine tables rebuilt from the data and all 181 files of the delivery to download.

2.1The answer in four lines

  1. There is enough power. All 18 studied mountings give 9.55–57.1 mW of mechanical power; after an assumed 50 % conversion that is at least 127 times the 37.6 µW a low-duty sensor node draws.
  2. The rotor pays for it. At fixed rotor speed the generator’s energy comes from the rotor’s drive; the pendulum cannot raise the turbine’s net output, and costs it at most \(7.2\times10^{-5}\) of it.
  3. One rod beats two. With the same mass and the same reach, a double pendulum gives 0.64–0.74 of the single one’s power at every mounting: half its mass sits at half the reach.
  4. Chaos does not help. 2 of 54 scanned double pendula are chaotic; at matched forcing the regular orbit harvests about 0.379 W, the chaotic one about 0.24–0.25 W. The catch is force, not power: a fast rotor’s pivot sits in 18–215 g, beyond a ±8 g accelerometer.

2.2What the audit corrected

An independent model, written from scratch, re-ran all 36 mapping cases and all 54 chaos cases; every headline number reproduced, to about one part in \(10^{11}\). Eight things in the report needed correcting, and none changes a conclusion. The main ones: the turbine-size formula is \(56\ \mathrm{m/s}/\Omega\), not mm/s; the centrifugal factor \(\Gamma\) rises with rotor speed, not falls; the chaotic power’s error bar is about \(\pm0.01\ \mathrm{W}\), not \(\pm0.003\ \mathrm{W}\); one mounting puts the bob 66 mm past the blade tip; and one figure is blurred by single-precision storage. The project shows each correction where it occurs, with its evidence.

Hydraulic Press Study

How a digital controller makes 12 MPa of oil press with 60 kN, and hold it

Push a small piston and a large one lifts a car: Pascal’s principle, the oldest trick in hydraulics. A modern press adds a servo valve and a computer, and the trick becomes a control problem. This study builds one such press from first principles: a pump held at 12 MPa, a four-way servo valve, two metres of hose, a cylinder carrying a 160 kg ram, and a specimen to squeeze, in a nonlinear model with eight state variables. A PID controller, sampled every millisecond, drives the ram to the specimen in position control, switches to force control at contact, ramps to 60 kN, holds and lets go. You watch every step with the oil coloured by its pressure, change any setting and see the whole cycle recomputed, and ask the linearized loop, with Bode, Nyquist and z-plane plots, whether it survives. A 23-chapter companion text derives every equation the animation integrates, starting from Pascal’s press.

3.1One press cycle, 4.4 seconds

  1. Approach. From 0.1 s the position law moves the ram up along a smooth path, the spool opening to 94 % on the way; at 1.1 s it slows to a 10 mm/s creep for the last 2 mm.
  2. Contact and press. At 1.316 s the load cell feels the specimen and the controller hands over to its force law, which ramps to 60 kN in 0.25 s, overshoots to 63.6 kN and settles while it holds for 0.5 s.
  3. Release and retract. From 2.066 s the force ramps back down; at 2.316 s the position law takes the ram home, and the cycle is done at 4.116 s.
  4. Where the energy goes. The pump does 26.3 kJ of work; the valve turns 24.4 kJ of it into heat, because a valve throttling a constant-pressure supply is a brake. The energy books close to \(2\times10^{-14}\) of the pump’s work.

3.2What the model keeps

Every block is a physical law with its own derivation. The valve’s metering edges are sharp-edged orifices whose jet contracts to 0.61 of the opening, as the free-streamline theory of Helmholtz and Kirchhoff predicts; the spool answers the controller as a mass on a spring at 60 Hz. The two metres of hose are a slug of oil with inertia and friction, laminar and turbulent. The oil itself is a spring whose stiffness depends on its pressure, through the 0.5 % of air it carries as bubbles. The cylinder has two chambers of different area, seal friction and end stops; the specimen is a spring with damping. The controller sees the world only once a millisecond, through a zero-order hold, and the model is integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at 10 µs, a hundred steps per sample. An independent Python model, with SciPy for the numbers and SymPy for the derivatives, recomputes the equilibria, the linearization, the closed-loop poles, the margins and the whole nonlinear cycle, and agrees with the page.

An interactive study · English and Arabic · 2026

impact mechanics · interatomic bonds · fibre networks

Iron, Cotton and the Head

Why an iron ball hurts and a cotton ball of the same mass does not

Drop two balls of exactly 1 kg from 10 m onto a head: one of iron, one of cotton. They bring the same energy, and on the head’s scale they settle to the same weight, 9.81 N. The blow is another matter. The iron ball does not give way, so the head must stop it within the few millimetres a skull can yield, and a short stop needs a large force. The cotton ball gives way itself and burns the energy inside its own fibres. The study builds both balls from their bonds: iron as a lattice of atoms held by Morse potentials with iron’s shape, cotton as a tangle of fibres that stretch, bend, cling where they cross and rub. Newton’s law moves every particle, a peak-hold force gauge reads the impact, and the energy books show where every joule goes. The text derives each step, from the fall with air drag to van Wyk’s law for fibre masses and the way real force sensors work, and compares the model with a real skull.

4.1One drop, 9 seconds

  1. The fall. Both balls are held 10 m above the head and fall under gravity alone; the first touch comes at 1.428 s, as Newton’s \(\sqrt{2h/g}\) predicts, at 14.0 m/s.
  2. The blow. The iron ball barely deforms and the head’s spring takes the whole stop: 639 N within about 50 ms, and the ball bounces. The cotton ball crushes through much of its size and peaks at 188 N.
  3. The weight. When the shaking has died away, both scales read 9.81 N, which is 1.000 kg: same mass, very different blows.
  4. Where the energy goes. The iron ball turns about 98 J into heat in the head; the cotton ball turns about 104 J into heat inside its own fibres. The energy books close to within half a joule.

4.2What the model keeps

Iron is 91 atoms on a triangular lattice, each bonded to its neighbours by a Morse valley with the shape Girifalco and Weizer fitted to iron in 1959. Cotton is 18 fibres of 34 beads: stretch springs along each fibre, bending springs on the angle between segments, breakable junctions where fibres cross, and soft contact with friction where they touch, which gives the stiffening of van Wyk’s law. The head is a plate on a spring and a damper and is also the scale. The model is two-dimensional, leaves out air and is tuned for clarity, so its force ratio is about 3.4, where real iron against real cotton gives about 120; the text says so and gives the real-world numbers beside the model’s.
