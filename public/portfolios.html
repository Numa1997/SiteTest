Droplet Collection

How cloud droplets become rain

A cloud droplet is ten micrometres across and sinks a centimetre a second; a raindrop holds a million of them. Gravity gathers them: a drop a little larger than its neighbours falls a little faster, overtakes the droplets below it and swallows some of them. These lecture notes rebuild Numa Koudsie’s Bachelor thesis, Collisional Ice Multiplication Processes in Clouds (Leipzig, 2022), from first principles, one physical effect at a time: Stokes’ drag derived from the stream function, a drag balance for every size, droplet trajectories around a falling drop, the energy that decides whether touching drops merge, the collection equation for a whole population, the exact random process behind it, turbulence, and ice. Every number not attributed to a source is computed by the model that runs in the page, and checked.

1.1The cloud and the three questions

The test case is the thesis’s warm cloud: \(300\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) droplets around \(8\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) and \(1\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) collectors around \(40\ \mathrm{\mu m}\), at \(10\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) and \(850\ \mathrm{hPa}\), in a closed, well-mixed box. The notes ask how long until most of the water sits in large drops, how long until the very first drizzle drop appears in a given volume, and whether collisions in a cold cloud make new ice. A ladder of models answers them, each rung adding one effect with a computed number that shows it matters.

1.2What the rebuild found, one line each

  1. Fall speed. Stokes’ law breaks its own assumption at \(41\ \mathrm{\mu m}\), where its Reynolds number reaches 1. A drag balance through the Davies number matches the measurements of Gunn and Kinzer within 2.1 % from 0.3 mm up, and in the full population it slows growth by a factor 1.76, far more than for a single drop.
  2. Collision efficiency. Only a third of the droplets in a \(40\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) drop’s path touch it (\(E = 0.344\)); the rest ride round on the air it pushes aside. The trajectory solver reproduces the critical Stokes numbers \(1/12\) and \(1.21194\). The efficiency is the largest single change of the ladder, and the least certain: it moves the time to nine tenths of the water from 13.5 to 10.5 min.
  3. Coalescence. For cloud droplets the collision energy is \(10^{-5}\) of the surface energy, so touching drops merge; with Stokes speeds, large drops hit so hard that the thesis kernel stops merging them below a millimetre.
  4. Luck. The exact random process, sampled drop by drop in volumes up to a litre, makes the first \(100\ \mathrm{\mu m}\) drop through mergers of collectors with each other, in every one of 230 runs; a Poisson argument carries it to a cubic metre.
  5. Ice. At \(-10\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) rime splintering is off, ice–ice breakup of the thesis’s small ice spheres makes almost nothing, and only the shattering of freezing drops could matter, between none and a few new particles per cm³ and minute: the measurements scatter that much.

Rotor Pendula

Can a second blade, hinged at the tip of a turbine blade, power a sensor, and can chaos or the wind help?

Hinge a light second blade, 1 kg and 1 m long, at the 63 m tip of a turning wind-turbine blade, free to turn in the rotor plane. The turning holds it straight out like a stiff spring, and gravity, which seen from the blade goes round once per turn, pushes it first one way and then the other, like a child on a swing pushed once per pass; a generator in the hinge brakes that swing and turns it into electricity. The lecture notes tell it as a double pendulum whose first arm the wind holds to a steady turning, and build everything from its Lagrangian: the chaos that holding the arm takes away, the best brake \(\kappa^* = R-1\), the trail that is the rotor’s bill, where the mass sits, a real motor and gearbox from a catalogue, then a turbulent wind over the rotor, gusts, the air on the second blade, the blade’s sag and shaking, and a ceiling on random shaking. Every number in the notes is computed by the models; a simulation page solves the full equations in your browser, and a wind page swings the second blade in a turbulent wind.

2.1The answer in five lines

  1. There is enough power. The second blade gives 161–267 mW of mechanical power at steady winds of 10 and 6 m/s, more in weak wind, and the rotor pays for every joule through the blade’s trail, at most \(3.4\times10^{-7}\) of the turbine’s output.
  2. Where the mass sits matters. The power grows with the second blade’s first moment, its mass times the distance of its centre of mass from the hinge: the same mass in a lump at the end would give 2.0 times as much.
  3. Chaos does not help. Every start, at every wind and brake tried, ends on one regular swing: the centrifugal spring is 3 to 9 times stronger than gravity’s push, and resonance is out of reach.
  4. The wind’s disorder hardly helps. Random shaking has a hard ceiling set by the blade’s mass; gusts change the power by a few per cent; the mother blade’s sag at its tip adds 2.2–3.5 %; its shaking adds a little at a tip vibration of 5 mm.
  5. A real generator still delivers. A catalogue motor behind its gearhead’s largest ratio, 492 : 1, brings 57–106 mW to the wires, at least 1 522 times the sensor’s need, and still 811 times if the gearbox is twice as draggy and only 50 % efficient. The air on the second blade, left out of these notes, is the open problem.

2.2What was checked

The equations are derived twice, by Lagrange with the kinetic energy integrated along the blade and by Newton summed over it, and solved with an implicit solver that agrees with an independent one to \(2\times10^{-12}\). A referee checked each derivation, and the notes say where a model was corrected: a smoothing band in the geared generator, set on gravity’s scale, was a thousand times too wide and made the power the rotor pays 16 % low; it now sits on the gear’s own power scale. An independent script, with every constant typed again, re-derives the main formulas in SymPy and re-integrates the runs with SciPy.

Hydraulic Press Study

How a digital controller makes 12 MPa of oil press with 60 kN, and hold it

Push a small piston and a large one lifts a car: Pascal’s principle, the oldest trick in hydraulics. A modern press adds a servo valve and a computer, and the trick becomes a control problem. This study builds one such press from first principles: a pump held at 12 MPa, a four-way servo valve, two metres of hose, a cylinder carrying a 160 kg ram, and a specimen to squeeze, in a nonlinear model with eight state variables. A PID controller, sampled every millisecond, drives the ram to the specimen in position control, switches to force control at contact, ramps to 60 kN, holds and lets go. You watch every step with the oil coloured by its pressure, change any setting and see the whole cycle recomputed, and ask the linearized loop, with Bode, Nyquist and z-plane plots, whether it survives. A 23-chapter companion text derives every equation the animation integrates, starting from Pascal’s press.

3.1One press cycle, 4.4 seconds

  1. Approach. From 0.1 s the position law moves the ram up along a smooth path, the spool opening to 94 % on the way; at 1.1 s it slows to a 10 mm/s creep for the last 2 mm.
  2. Contact and press. At 1.316 s the load cell feels the specimen and the controller hands over to its force law, which ramps to 60 kN in 0.25 s, overshoots to 63.6 kN and settles while it holds for 0.5 s.
  3. Release and retract. From 2.066 s the force ramps back down; at 2.316 s the position law takes the ram home, and the cycle is done at 4.116 s.
  4. Where the energy goes. The pump does 26.3 kJ of work; the valve turns 24.4 kJ of it into heat, because a valve throttling a constant-pressure supply is a brake. The energy books close to \(2\times10^{-14}\) of the pump’s work.

3.2What the model keeps

Every block is a physical law with its own derivation. The valve’s metering edges are sharp-edged orifices whose jet contracts to 0.61 of the opening, as the free-streamline theory of Helmholtz and Kirchhoff predicts; the spool answers the controller as a mass on a spring at 60 Hz. The two metres of hose are a slug of oil with inertia and friction, laminar and turbulent. The oil itself is a spring whose stiffness depends on its pressure, through the 0.5 % of air it carries as bubbles. The cylinder has two chambers of different area, seal friction and end stops; the specimen is a spring with damping. The controller sees the world only once a millisecond, through a zero-order hold, and the model is integrated with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at 10 µs, a hundred steps per sample. An independent Python model, with SciPy for the numbers and SymPy for the derivatives, recomputes the equilibria, the linearization, the closed-loop poles, the margins and the whole nonlinear cycle, and agrees with the page.

impact mechanics · interatomic bonds · fibre networks

Iron, Cotton and the Head

Why an iron ball hurts and a cotton ball of the same mass does not

Drop two balls of exactly 1 kg from 10 m onto a head: one of iron, one of cotton. They bring the same energy, and on the head’s scale they settle to the same weight, 9.81 N. The blow is another matter. The iron ball does not give way, so the head must stop it within the few millimetres a skull can yield, and a short stop needs a large force. The cotton ball gives way itself and burns the energy inside its own fibres. The study builds both balls from their bonds: iron as a lattice of atoms held by Morse potentials with iron’s shape, cotton as a tangle of fibres that stretch, bend, cling where they cross and rub. Newton’s law moves every particle, a peak-hold force gauge reads the impact, and the energy books show where every joule goes. The text derives each step, from the fall with air drag to van Wyk’s law for fibre masses and the way real force sensors work, and compares the model with a real skull.

4.1One drop, 9 seconds

  1. The fall. Both balls are held 10 m above the head and fall under gravity alone; the first touch comes at 1.428 s, as Newton’s \(\sqrt{2h/g}\) predicts, at 14.0 m/s.
  2. The blow. The iron ball barely deforms and the head’s spring takes the whole stop: 639 N within about 50 ms, and the ball bounces. The cotton ball crushes through much of its size and peaks at 188 N.
  3. The weight. When the shaking has died away, both scales read 9.81 N, which is 1.000 kg: same mass, very different blows.
  4. Where the energy goes. The iron ball turns about 98 J into heat in the head; the cotton ball turns about 104 J into heat inside its own fibres. The energy books close to within half a joule.

4.2What the model keeps

Iron is 91 atoms on a triangular lattice, each bonded to its neighbours by a Morse valley with the shape Girifalco and Weizer fitted to iron in 1959. Cotton is 18 fibres of 34 beads: stretch springs along each fibre, bending springs on the angle between segments, breakable junctions where fibres cross, and soft contact with friction where they touch, which gives the stiffening of van Wyk’s law. The head is a plate on a spring and a damper and is also the scale. The model is two-dimensional, leaves out air and is tuned for clarity, so its force ratio is about 3.4, where real iron against real cotton gives about 120; the text says so and gives the real-world numbers beside the model’s.
