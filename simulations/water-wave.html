01 · Physical picture

A cork on the sea

Watch a cork on a swell: it rises with the crest, drifts forward, sinks into the trough, drifts back, and ends where it began. The water under the surface does the same on smaller circles, and a few metres down it barely moves at all. The wave you see racing to the shore is a pattern of motion, not a current. In the figure every dot is a parcel of water; the burgundy column follows one vertical line of parcels from the surface down.

02 · Model

Linear waves on deep water

Let x run along the surface and z upward, with z = 0 the calm surface. Assume the water is inviscid, incompressible and its flow irrotational. Then the velocity is the gradient of a potential, u = ∇φ, and incompressibility ∇·u = 0 becomes Laplace's equation:

∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂z² = 0   for z < η(x, t)

For small waves the surface conditions can be applied at z = 0:

kinematic: ∂η/∂t = ∂φ/∂z   (the surface moves with the water)dynamic: ∂φ/∂t + g η = 0   (Bernoulli, with air pressure constant)deep water: ∇φ → 0 as z → −∞

  • Amplitude small compared with wavelength (linear theory, also called Airy theory).
  • Depth greater than about half a wavelength.
  • No surface tension, wind, viscosity or currents.

03 · Derivation

From Laplace to circles

A travelling solution

Try a surface η = a cos(kx − ωt) and a potential that decays with depth:

φ = (a ω / k) ek z sin(k x − ω t)

Laplace: ∂²φ/∂x² = −k²φ and ∂²φ/∂z² = +k²φ, so their sum is zero. The factor ekz is forced: the other solution, e−kz, would grow without bound with depth. Kinematic condition at z = 0: ∂φ/∂z = aω sin(kx − ωt) = ∂η/∂t. ✓

The dispersion relation

The dynamic condition at z = 0 gives −(aω²/k) cos(kx − ωt) + g a cos(kx − ωt) = 0, hence

ω² = g kc = ω / k = √(g / k) = √(g λ / 2π),   cg = dω / dk = ½ √(g / k) = c / 2

Long waves are fast: a 2.6 m swell moves at 2.01 m/s with period 1.29 s; a 100 m ocean swell at 12.5 m/s. The energy travels at the group velocity cg, half the crest speed, which is why crests in a group appear at the back, run through it and vanish at the front. The figure draws a single wave, so cg is a prediction here rather than something you can watch; and with dispersion switched off, cg = c/2 no longer applies.

Velocity and particle paths

u = ∂φ/∂x = a ω ekz cos(k x − ω t),   w = ∂φ/∂z = a ω ekz sin(k x − ω t)

In linear theory we evaluate this at the parcel's rest position (x₀, z₀) and integrate in time. That is the step that defines linear theory: the parcel moves only a small distance compared with λ, so the velocity where it actually is differs from the velocity at its rest position only at second order in ak, and dropping that difference is what closes the orbits (the Stokes-drift card below keeps it). Using d/dt sin(kx₀ − ωt) = −ω cos(kx₀ − ωt) and d/dt cos(kx₀ − ωt) = ω sin(kx₀ − ωt):

x = x₀ − a ek z₀ sin(k x₀ − ω t)z = z₀ + a ek z₀ cos(k x₀ − ω t)

(x − x₀)² + (z − z₀)² = a² e2kz₀: each parcel moves on a circle of radius a ekz₀. At a crest (phase 0) it is at the top, moving forward at speed aω; in a trough, at the bottom, moving backward. The circulation is clockwise when the wave moves to the right.

Stokes drift: why the orbits do not quite close

The circles above use the velocity at the rest position (x₀, z₀). A real parcel feels the velocity where it actually is, displaced by ξ = −A sin θ horizontally and ζ = A cos θ vertically, with A = a ekz₀ and θ = kx₀ − ωt. Expanding the horizontal velocity to first order in the displacement:

u(x₀ + ξ, z₀ + ζ) ≈ u + ξ ∂u/∂x + ζ ∂u/∂zξ ∂u/∂x = (−A sin θ)(−aωk ekz₀ sin θ) = a²ωk e2kz₀ sin²θζ ∂u/∂z = (A cos θ)(aωk ekz₀ cos θ) = a²ωk e2kz₀ cos²θ

The first term, u itself, averages to zero over a period. The two corrections add to a constant, because sin²θ + cos²θ = 1:

US = a²ωk e2kz₀ = c (ak)² e2kz₀c = ω/k · second order in the steepness ak

The picture: under a crest the parcel is at the top of its circle, where the forward flow is stronger (ekz is larger); under a trough it is at the bottom, where the backward flow is weaker. Forward wins, so each orbit ends slightly ahead of where it began. Per period the parcel advances UST = (ak)² e2kz₀ λ, since cT = λ. At ak = 0.2 and λ = 2.6 m (c ≈ 2.0 m/s) that is 4 % of a wavelength, about 10 cm per wave at the surface, and it fades twice as fast with depth as the orbits do.

The simulation draws the first-order circles and leaves this drift out on purpose. The exact Gerstner wave mentioned below has closed orbits and no drift; the drift belongs to irrotational waves like the ones derived here.

Decay with depth

At z₀ = −λ/2 the radius is e−π ≈ 4.3 % of the surface value, at z₀ = −λ only 0.19 %. The Deepest orbit readout gives this fraction for the lowest layer shown.

The shape of the surface

The surface parcels (z₀ = 0) trace the curve (x₀ − a sin θ, a cos θ) with θ = kx₀ − ωt. This is a trochoid: crests sharper and troughs flatter than a sine. The steepness ak controls the sharpening; at ak = 1 the crest would become a cusp, so the simulation caps ak at 0.9. Real waves break well before that, near the Stokes limit H/λ ≈ 0.142 (H = 2a), i.e. ak ≈ 0.45. This trochoidal form is also the exact nonlinear Gerstner wave, although that solution carries vorticity.

TryRaise the amplitude to 50 px at λ = 260 px: ak reaches 1.2, the cap applies and the readout shows (cap). Look at how the crests narrow and the troughs flatten.

04 · Numerical method

Closed form at every frame

The particle positions are known analytically, so each frame evaluates them exactly, with no time stepping; only the phase ωt accumulates, by 2πf Δt, so a change of f is smooth. With dispersion on, the frequency is computed exactly from the wavelength, f = √(g k)/(2π) with 100 px = 1 m. The slider then displays that value, but the simulation uses the unrounded number rather than the slider's 0.01 Hz steps.

05 · On screen

Parcels, orbits and a tinted sea

The world is 800 px wide (8 m). The calm surface sits at 28 % of the figure height, and the depth layers are 56 px = 0.56 m apart. The surface is drawn as a polyline through parcels every 3 px of rest position, and the region under it is filled with a rose tint fading with depth. For every parcel, a dashed circle shows its orbit and a dot shows its present position; the followed column is burgundy, with a radius drawn from each orbit's centre.

06 · Limits

What the model leaves out

  • Stokes drift, derived in step 03, is not drawn: the circles on screen close exactly.
  • Shallow water (depth < λ/2): orbits become flattened ellipses and ω² = gk tanh(kh).
  • Surface tension matters for λ below about 1.7 cm (capillary waves).
  • No wind forcing, viscosity or breaking.
