01 · Physical picture

Many voices on one string

Two tuning forks a little out of tune produce a sound that swells and fades, the beat. A violin string vibrates in many harmonics at once, and their mixture is the timbre you hear. In both cases the medium simply carries the sum of the separate waves. This workbench lets you build such sums one component at a time: the lower plot shows the ingredients, the upper plot the result, and the spectrum below lists what is in the mix.

02 · Model

Travelling sine waves

Each component i is

yi(x, t) = Ai sin( 2π fi (x/200 − si t) + φi )

Reading off the wavenumber and angular frequency, ki = 2π fi/200 px⁻¹ and ωi = 2π fi si. So:

  • wavelength λi = 200/fi px, and the view spans 800 px, i.e. 4fi wavelengths;
  • phase speed vi = ωi/ki = 200 si px/s, set by the Speed slider alone;
  • temporal frequency fi si. The Frequency slider is therefore the true frequency in Hz only at speed 1×; in general it counts cycles per 200 px.

03 · Derivation

Superposition and what it builds

Why adding is allowed

Every component solves its wave equation ∂²y/∂t² = vi² ∂²y/∂x². With equal speeds, all solve the same linear equation 𝓛y = 0 with 𝓛 = ∂²/∂t² − v² ∂²/∂x². Linearity means 𝓛(a y₁ + b y₂) = a 𝓛y₁ + b 𝓛y₂ = 0: any weighted sum of solutions is a solution. The sum then moves at v without changing shape; the medium is non-dispersive.

Beats

Take two components with equal amplitude A and phases a = k₁x − ω₁t, b = k₂x − ω₂t. The sum-to-product identity gives

A sin a + A sin b = 2A · cos((a − b)/2) · sin((a + b)/2)

A fast carrier with the mean wavenumber, inside a slow envelope with wavenumber Δk/2. Its magnitude |cos| repeats every 2π/Δk in space: for the preset f = 1.00 and 1.25, that is 200/0.25 = 800 px, exactly one beat across the view. In time, at any fixed point, the loudness swells every 1/|f₁s₁ − f₂s₂| = 1/0.25 = 4 s.

The envelope moves at Δω/Δk and the carrier at ω̄/k̄. With equal speeds both equal v. Give the waves different speeds and the envelope travels at

venv = Δω / Δk = 200 (f₁ s₁ − f₂ s₂) / (f₁ − f₂) px/s

which in general differs from both phase speeds. In the limit of close frequencies this is the group velocity dω/dk, the speed at which energy and information travel.

Fourier series of a square wave

A square wave sq(x) of period 2π, equal to +1 on (0, π) and −1 on (−π, 0), is odd, so it has only sine terms, bn sin nx. To pick out one coefficient, multiply both sides by sin nx and integrate over a period: the sines are orthogonal, ∫ sin mx sin nx dx = π δmn, so every other term drops out and

bn = (1/π) ∫−ππ sq(x) sin(nx) dx = (2/π) ∫0π sin(nx) dx = (2/(nπ)) (1 − cos nπ)bn = 4/(nπ) for n odd,   0 for n even

sq(x) = (4/π) [ sin x + sin 3x / 3 + sin 5x / 5 + sin 7x / 7 + … ]

I checked the first coefficients by numerical integration: 1.2732 = 4/π and 0.4244 = 4/(3π), with the even ones zero. The Square wave preset uses f = 0.4, 1.2, 2.0, 2.8 (n = 1, 3, 5, 7) and amplitudes 30/n px, which approximates a square wave of height (π/4)·30 ≈ 23.6 px. Near each jump the partial sums overshoot; this Gibbs overshoot tends to about 9 % of the jump and never vanishes, however many terms you add. It only gets narrower.

How big can the sum get?

By the triangle inequality, |Σ yi| ≤ Σ |Ai|: the Sum of amplitudes readout is a strict bound, reached only where all components peak together. When all frequencies differ, the cross terms average to zero and the mean square of the sum is ½ Σ Ai² (Parseval). Two components with the same frequency and speed merge into one sine of amplitude √(A₁² + A₂² + 2A₁A₂ cos(φ₁ − φ₂)), and the spectrum shows them as a single stem of that amplitude.

TryLoad Beats, then set the second wave's speed to 0.5×: the envelope now slides against the carrier. Load Square wave and remove wave 4: the corners round off and the Gibbs ripples widen.

04 · Numerical method

Direct evaluation per column

Every frame, each component is evaluated exactly at one point per screen pixel of plot width, and the sum is accumulated at the same points: a cost of N × (plot width) sines per frame, a few thousand at most. The time t advances by the real elapsed time, so the waves move at the stated speeds on every screen. The peak readout is the largest |y| among the sampled points in the current frame.

05 · On screen

Three plots, one scale rule

The upper plot (56 % of the height) shows the sum in ink with a soft burgundy fill to the axis. Its vertical range is Σ Ai rounded up to the next 10 px (at least 20), so by the bound above the curve can never leave it. The lower plot shows each component in its own colour, scaled to the largest single amplitude. The spectrum is the temporal line spectrum at x = 0: each stem sits at ν = fisi, the frequency you would hear at a fixed point, and components with the same ν are first added as phasors, C = Σ Ai eiφi, so the stem height is |C|: two equal waves in phase give 2A, in antiphase nothing. Heights are scaled to 40 px or to the tallest stem, whichever is larger. Each wave's panel and sliders carry the same colour as its curve and stem; a stem that combines several waves is drawn in ink.

06 · Limits

What the model leaves out

  • An infinite, lossless medium; the 800 px view is a window onto it.
  • Different speeds imitate dispersion, but each component's speed is set by hand, not by a physical relation ω(k).
  • The spectrum is computed analytically from the components you entered (at x = 0), not by a Fourier transform of the plotted curve.
