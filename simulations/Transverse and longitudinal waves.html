From physics to pixels

Why a rope and a spring carry the same mathematics, where that mathematics comes from, and how it becomes two rows of dots.

01 · Physical picture

A rope and a slinky

Shake the end of a stretched rope up and down: a hump runs along it, but every bit of rope only moves up and down. That is a transverse wave. Push and pull the end of a slinky along its length: bunched and stretched regions run along it, but every coil only moves back and forth. That is a longitudinal wave, the way sound travels through air. The two rows in the figure are the same wave shown both ways; the burgundy particle in each row makes the difference visible.

02 · Model

A displacement field on a line

Label every particle by its rest position x. The displacement ξ(x, t) says how far it has moved: sideways in the transverse row, along the line in the longitudinal row. The simulation uses the harmonic travelling wave

ξ(x, t) = A sin(k x − ω t),   k = 2π/λ,   ω = 2π f

  • Small amplitudes, so the medium responds linearly.
  • No damping and no dispersion: every wavelength travels at the same speed.
  • An infinite medium: nothing reflects from the ends of the picture.

03 · Derivation

One wave equation for both

The transverse wave on a string

Take a string under tension F with mass per length μ. A short piece between x and x + dx is pulled by the tension at both ends; for small slopes the vertical components are F ∂ξ/∂x. The net vertical force is the difference between the two ends, and Newton's second law for the piece reads

μ dx · ∂²ξ/∂t² = F [ ∂ξ/∂x |x+dx − ∂ξ/∂x |x ] = F ∂²ξ/∂x² dx⇒   ∂²ξ/∂t² = v² ∂²ξ/∂x²,   v = √(F/μ)

The longitudinal wave in a rod or a gas

Now let the particles move along x. A piece of rest length dx is stretched to dx(1 + ∂ξ/∂x), so the strain is ∂ξ/∂x and, by Hooke's law, the stress is σ = E ∂ξ/∂x with E the elastic modulus. The net force on the piece is the difference of stress across it, times the area; per unit volume, with density ρ,

ρ ∂²ξ/∂t² = ∂σ/∂x = E ∂²ξ/∂x²  ⇒  v = √(E/ρ)for a gas, E is replaced by γp: v = √(γp/ρ) ≈ 343 m/s in air at 20 °C

Different physics, the same equation. That is why one ξ(x, t) can drive both rows.

Solutions and the speed

Any function f(x − vt) + g(x + vt) solves the wave equation (d'Alembert): a shape moving right plus one moving left. Inserting the harmonic wave, ∂²ξ/∂t² = −ω² ξ and ∂²ξ/∂x² = −k² ξ, so −ω² = −v²k², and

v = ω / k = f λ

A crest, kx − ωt = π/2, therefore sits at x = (π/2 + ωt)/k and moves at v. The small ochre triangles above the upper row ride exactly on these crests.

Particle motion is not wave motion

Each particle's velocity is ∂ξ/∂t = −Aω cos(kx − ωt), at most Aω. Compared with the wave speed, Aω/v = Ak: for the defaults (A = 26 px, λ = 220 px) that is 0.74, so the particles move slower than the pattern and never go anywhere on average.

Compressions in the longitudinal row

A piece of rest length dx now occupies dx(1 + ∂ξ/∂x), so mass conservation gives the local density

ρ / ρ₀ = 1 / (1 + ∂ξ/∂x) ≈ 1 − A k cos(k x − ω t)

The medium is compressed where ∂ξ/∂x < 0, that is around kx − ωt = π. There the displacement is zero and the particles move forward at full speed. Since kx − ωt grows with x, the compressions sit a quarter wavelength ahead of the transverse crests, in the direction of travel: a detail that is easy to get wrong and easy to see in the figure. The shading shows max(0, −cos(kx − ωt)); the triangles above the lower row mark the compression centres.

If Ak ≥ 1 somewhere, 1 + ∂ξ/∂x ≤ 0: a particle would overtake its neighbour, which is impossible. So the longitudinal amplitude is capped at 0.8/k, and the Longit. A readout says when the cap is active.

Energy carried

For the string, the energy per length is ½μ(∂ξ/∂t)² + ½F(∂ξ/∂x)². Averaged over a period, both terms are equal, giving ½μω²A², and the average power carried along the string is ⟨P⟩ = ½ μ v ω² A²: it grows with the square of amplitude and of frequency.

Where this comes from: multiply the wave equation μ ∂²ξ/∂t² = F ∂²ξ/∂x² by ∂ξ/∂t and use the product rule. The result is a local conservation law, the same shape as the continuity equation for charge:

∂e/∂t + ∂P/∂x = 0,   e = ½μ(∂ξ/∂t)² + ½F(∂ξ/∂x)²,   P = −F (∂ξ/∂x)(∂ξ/∂t)

For a wave moving right, ∂ξ/∂x = −(1/v) ∂ξ/∂t, so P = (F/v)(∂ξ/∂t)² ≥ 0: the energy flux points along the direction of travel at every instant, and since F/v = μv its average is the ⟨P⟩ = ½μvω²A² above.

04 · Numerical method

Exact evaluation, continuous phase

Because the solution is known in closed form, there is no time-stepping error at all: every frame evaluates ξ exactly at every particle. Only the phase ωt is accumulated, by 2πf Δt per frame with Δt the real elapsed time. Accumulating the phase rather than computing 2πf·t means a change of f changes the speed smoothly, instead of making the wave jump.

05 · On screen

Two rows on one canvas

Both rows use a world line 640 px long, scaled to the width of the figure. The transverse row sits at 30 % of the height: particles at (x, y₀ − ξ), with rose lines showing each displacement, a thin burgundy line tracing the full sine curve, and tick marks at the rest positions. The longitudinal row sits at 77 %: particles at (x + ξ, y₀), each drawn as a short vertical bar so that crowding reads as density, above a band whose burgundy tint follows the compression, sampled at 65 points along the line. One particle in the middle is followed in both rows, with a rose line from its rest position in the lower row.

06 · Limits

What the model leaves out

  • Large amplitudes make both media nonlinear: string tension changes with stretch, sound waves steepen into shocks.
  • No damping, no reflections at the ends, no dispersion.
  • The lengths are in px of the drawing; only the ratio A/λ matters for the shape.
