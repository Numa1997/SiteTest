01 · Physical picture

A pendulum hanging from a pendulum

Hang a rod from a pivot, and a second rod from the end of the first. Let it go from a small angle and it rocks gently, the two arms trading energy back and forth like two coupled swings. Let it go from high up and the lower arm starts whipping over the top, reversing without warning. Nothing random happens: the motion is completely fixed by the starting state. Yet two starts that differ by less than you could ever measure end up in unrelated places within seconds. That is deterministic chaos.

02 · Model

Two point masses on rigid, massless rods

The pivot is the origin, y points up, and each angle θi is measured from the downward vertical, counter-clockwise positive. The positions of the two masses are

x₁ = l₁ sin θ₁,   y₁ = −l₁ cos θ₁x₂ = x₁ + l₂ sin θ₂,   y₂ = y₁ − l₂ cos θ₂

Two numbers, θ₁ and θ₂, fix the whole configuration: the system has two degrees of freedom, and its state is the four numbers (θ₁, θ₂, ω₁, ω₂) with ωi = dθi/dt.

  • Rods are rigid and massless; masses are points; the motion stays in one plane.
  • No friction or air resistance, so total energy is conserved.
  • Here m₁ = 1 kg, l₁ = 1 m, g = 9.81 m/s²; the sliders set the ratios m₂/m₁ and l₂/l₁.

03 · Derivation

Lagrangian mechanics, step by step

Kinetic and potential energy

Differentiating the positions in time:

ẋ₁ = l₁ ω₁ cos θ₁,   ẏ₁ = l₁ ω₁ sin θ₁ẋ₂ = ẋ₁ + l₂ ω₂ cos θ₂,   ẏ₂ = ẏ₁ + l₂ ω₂ sin θ₂

Squaring and adding, the cross term uses cos θ₁ cos θ₂ + sin θ₁ sin θ₂ = cos(θ₁ − θ₂):

T = ½ m₁ l₁² ω₁² + ½ m₂ [ l₁² ω₁² + l₂² ω₂² + 2 l₁ l₂ ω₁ ω₂ cos(θ₁ − θ₂) ]V = m₁ g y₁ + m₂ g y₂ = −(m₁ + m₂) g l₁ cos θ₁ − m₂ g l₂ cos θ₂

The Lagrangian is L = T − V. Write δ = θ₁ − θ₂ and αi = dωi/dt for the angular accelerations.

Where the Euler–Lagrange equation comes from

Among all paths θ(t) with fixed start and end, the real motion makes the action S = ∫ L dt stationary (Hamilton's principle). Vary one angle by a small δθi(t) that vanishes at both ends: δS = ∫ [∂L/∂θi δθi + ∂L/∂ωi δωi] dt, with δωi = d(δθi)/dt. Integrating the second term by parts moves the time derivative onto ∂L/∂ωi, and the boundary term vanishes because δθi does:

δS = ∫ [ ∂L/∂θi − d/dt(∂L/∂ωi) ] δθi dt = 0 for every δθi  ⇒  d/dt(∂L/∂ωi) − ∂L/∂θi = 0the rods are built in: only θ₁ and θ₂ are varied, so no constraint forces appear

The quantity pi = ∂L/∂ωi is the generalised momentum of θi. For the upper angle, p₁ = (m₁ + m₂) l₁² ω₁ + m₂ l₁ l₂ ω₂ cos δ: an angular momentum that includes the lower bob's motion, not simply m₁ω₁.

Euler–Lagrange equation for θ₁

Why these three lines, in this order. The equation d/dt(∂L/∂ω₁) − ∂L/∂θ₁ = 0 uses two different kinds of derivative. A partial derivative such as ∂L/∂ω₁ treats L as a function of four independent numbers (θ₁, θ₂, ω₁, ω₂) and varies only one of them, holding the other three fixed. The time derivative d/dt then follows the actual motion, in which all four change together. By the chain rule, d/dt cos δ = −sin δ · dδ/dt = −sin δ (ω₁ − ω₂): that is where the −ω₂(ω₁ − ω₂) sin δ term below comes from. Its ω₁ω₂ part then cancels against ∂L/∂θ₁, which picks up its own ω₁ω₂ sin δ from differentiating cos δ with θ₁ alone varying.

∂L/∂ω₁ = (m₁ + m₂) l₁² ω₁ + m₂ l₁ l₂ ω₂ cos δd/dt(∂L/∂ω₁) = (m₁ + m₂) l₁² α₁ + m₂ l₁ l₂ α₂ cos δ − m₂ l₁ l₂ ω₂ (ω₁ − ω₂) sin δ∂L/∂θ₁ = −m₂ l₁ l₂ ω₁ ω₂ sin δ − (m₁ + m₂) g l₁ sin θ₁

Setting d/dt(∂L/∂ω₁) − ∂L/∂θ₁ = 0, the two ω₁ω₂ terms cancel, and dividing by l₁:

(m₁ + m₂) l₁ α₁ + m₂ l₂ α₂ cos δ = −m₂ l₂ ω₂² sin δ − (m₁ + m₂) g sin θ₁   (E1)

Euler–Lagrange equation for θ₂

∂L/∂ω₂ = m₂ l₂² ω₂ + m₂ l₁ l₂ ω₁ cos δd/dt(∂L/∂ω₂) = m₂ l₂² α₂ + m₂ l₁ l₂ α₁ cos δ − m₂ l₁ l₂ ω₁ (ω₁ − ω₂) sin δ∂L/∂θ₂ = +m₂ l₁ l₂ ω₁ ω₂ sin δ − m₂ g l₂ sin θ₂

Again the ω₁ω₂ terms cancel; dividing by m₂ l₂:

l₁ α₁ cos δ + l₂ α₂ = l₁ ω₁² sin δ − g sin θ₂   (E2)

Solving for the accelerations

Why solve before integrating: each equation contains both α₁ and α₂, because each rod pushes on the other. A time-stepping method needs each acceleration on its own, as a function of the current state, so the next move is to solve this 2 × 2 system. (E1) and (E2) are linear in α₁ and α₂. The determinant of the coefficient matrix is

D = (m₁ + m₂) l₁ l₂ − m₂ l₁ l₂ cos² δ = l₁ l₂ (m₁ + m₂ sin² δ)never zero, since m₁ + m₂ sin² δ ≥ m₁ > 0

By Cramer's rule, with the right-hand sides r₁ of (E1) and r₂ of (E2),

α₁ = l₂ [ r₁ − m₂ cos δ · r₂ ] / D   = [ −m₂ sin δ (l₂ ω₂² + l₁ ω₁² cos δ) − (m₁ + m₂) g sin θ₁ + m₂ g sin θ₂ cos δ ] / [ l₁ (m₁ + m₂ sin² δ) ]

Two identities bring this to the compact form. Multiply top and bottom by 2 and use 2 sin² δ = 1 − cos 2δ in the denominator. In the numerator, 2 sin θ₂ cos(θ₁ − θ₂) = sin θ₁ − sin(θ₁ − 2θ₂), so the gravity terms become −g(2m₁ + m₂) sin θ₁ − m₂ g sin(θ₁ − 2θ₂). For α₂, the same rule gives a gravity combination sin θ₁ cos δ − sin θ₂, and since sin θ₂ = sin(θ₁ − δ) = sin θ₁ cos δ − cos θ₁ sin δ, it equals cos θ₁ sin δ. Collecting:

α₁ = [ −g(2m₁ + m₂) sin θ₁ − m₂ g sin(θ₁ − 2θ₂) − 2 m₂ sin δ (ω₂² l₂ + ω₁² l₁ cos δ) ] / [ l₁ (2m₁ + m₂ − m₂ cos 2δ) ]α₂ = [ 2 sin δ ( ω₁² l₁ (m₁ + m₂) + g (m₁ + m₂) cos θ₁ + ω₂² l₂ m₂ cos δ ) ] / [ l₂ (2m₁ + m₂ − m₂ cos 2δ) ]

I checked this closed form against a direct numerical solve of (E1)–(E2) at 10 000 random states and parameter values: they agree to 5 × 10⁻¹⁴, i.e. to rounding error.

Small swings: two normal modes

For small angles, sin θ ≈ θ, cos δ ≈ 1 and the ω² terms are second order. With equal masses and lengths l the equations become M θ̈ = −K θ with M ∝ [[2, 1], [1, 1]] and K ∝ (g/l)[[2, 0], [0, 1]]. Setting det(K − ω² M) = 0 gives 2(g/l − ω²)² = ω⁴, so

ω±² = (2 ± √2) g / l  ⇒  ω₊ = 5.79 rad/s, ω₋ = 2.40 rad/s (l = 1 m)

The mode shapes follow from (K − ω²M)u = 0. Its first row gives u₂/u₁ = 2(g/l − ω²)/ω², which is +√2 for the slow mode (both arms swing together, the lower one √2 times further) and −√2 for the fast mode (the arms swing against each other). Any small motion is a mix of the two; since their frequencies are incommensurate, the pattern never repeats exactly, but it stays regular.

TryWith equal masses and lengths, set θ₁ = 5°, θ₂ = 7° (≈ √2 × 5°) and both angular velocities to zero: the pendulum swings almost as one piece in the slow mode, period 2π/2.40 ≈ 2.6 s. Set θ₂ = −7° instead for the fast mode, period 2π/5.79 ≈ 1.1 s.

TrySet both angles to 10° and switch on the chaos twin: the separation stays near 10⁻³ m. Now set 120° and 150°: within a few seconds the twin is metres away.

Energy and chaos

L does not depend explicitly on time, so the energy E = T + V is conserved. At large energy the motion is chaotic: two starts separated by ε drift apart roughly as ε eλt, where λ is the largest Lyapunov exponent. The twin starts with θ₁ larger by ε = 0.001 rad; its readout shows the distance between the two lower masses.

04 · Numerical method

Fourth-order Runge–Kutta

Write the state as s = (θ₁, θ₂, ω₁, ω₂) and the equations as ds/dt = F(s) = (ω₁, ω₂, α₁, α₂). One RK4 step of size h samples the slope four times:

k₁ = F(s),   k₂ = F(s + h k₁/2),   k₃ = F(s + h k₂/2),   k₄ = F(s + h k₃)s(t + h) = s + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄)

The error per step is O(h⁵) and the accumulated error O(h⁴). With h = 1/600 s, the Drift readout typically stays near 10⁻⁹. The drift is measured against the depth of the potential well, Escale = (m₁ + m₂) g l₁ + m₂ g l₂, rather than against E₀ itself, so it stays meaningful when the total energy happens to be close to zero.

  • Each frame advances by the real time elapsed × the Time scale, split into steps of h; at most 600 steps per frame.
  • RK4 does not conserve energy exactly (it is not symplectic), which is why the drift readout is there as a check.
  • Changing a mass, length, angle or angular-velocity slider restarts from those initial conditions; the Time scale slider does not.

05 · On screen

Metres into pixels

The pivot is at the centre of the figure. The scale is chosen so the fully stretched pendulum just fits: pixels per metre = (min(width, height)/2 − 34) / (l₁ + l₂). Because screen y points down, the masses are drawn at (x₀ + l sin θ, y₀ + l cos θ).

The protractor marks θ from the downward vertical every 30°. The burgundy trail of the lower mass is drawn on a separate transparent layer, segment by segment at every RK4 step, and faded each frame by a factor (1 − 0.008)60Δt: its brightness halves every ln 2/(60 × 0.00803) ≈ 1.4 s. Each mass is drawn as a disc with radius ∝ m1/3, as for spheres of equal density.

06 · Limits

What the model leaves out

  • Real rods have mass and moments of inertia; a physical double pendulum with bars obeys similar but not identical equations.
  • No friction: a real pendulum slowly loses energy, and chaos eventually decays into small regular swings.
  • Point masses may pass through each other and through the pivot; the rods cannot collide.
  • Past a few Lyapunov times, any numerical trajectory is only a representative of the true one, not the same path; the statistics remain right.
