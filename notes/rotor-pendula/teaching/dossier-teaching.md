## How to read the reading marks

Every source carries one of three marks. I did not invent any quote; quotes are copied from text I saw.

- **[FULL]** I read the whole text, or all of the parts that matter here, in this session.
- **[PART]** I read the abstract and substantial excerpts (named in the entry), not the whole paper.
- **[ABS]** I saw only the abstract, a publisher record or a search summary. Treat claims as the authors' own summary.

"Application" lines are my reading of how to use the finding in written notes. They are inferences, not results of the cited study.

## A. Teaching methods with evidence

### A1. Worked-example effect

- **Rule.** A novice learns a new kind of problem faster and with fewer errors from studying fully worked solutions than from solving the same problems unaided.
- **Evidence.** Sweller and Cooper, five experiments in algebra: worked examples "require considerably less time to process than conventional problems", later similar problems "were solved more rapidly", and errors fell; both gains were "specific to problems identical in structure to the initial ones", and abstracting general rules "may occur only with considerable practice and exposure to a wider range of schemas".
- **Application.** Give a complete worked example before asking the reader to do the same kind of step. Do not expect it to transfer to a different structure; vary the structure in the next example (a bead on a hoop, then the rod).
- **Source.** John Sweller and Graham A. Cooper (1985), "The Use of Worked Examples as a Substitute for Problem Solving in Learning Algebra", *Cognition and Instruction* 2(1), 59-89. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3> **[ABS]** (abstract seen; paper behind a paywall).

### A2. Fading worked steps (example, then half-example, then problem)

- **Rule.** Move the learner from a complete example to a problem by removing solution steps one at a time, last step first.
- **Evidence.** Renkl, Atkinson, Maier and Staley ran a field experiment and two lab experiments against ordinary example-problem pairs. (a) Fading helped, "at least when near transfer performance is considered"; (b) the number of errors made while learning mediated the effect; (c) "it is more favorable to fade out worked-out solution steps in a backward manner (omitting the last solution steps first) as compared with a forward manner". In their third experiment the two fading orders reached the same scores, but backward fading needed less learning time ("over 6 min" less for forward). Far transfer was open: "forward fading does not substantially foster far transfer, whereas backward fading has the potential".
- **Application.** In a derivation: example 1 shows every step; example 2 leaves the last line to the reader ("now find the equilibrium angle"); example 3 leaves the last two. Put the missing step in a fold-out or footnote answer.
- **Source.** Alexander Renkl, Robert K. Atkinson, Uwe H. Maier, Richard Staley (2002), "From Example Study to Problem Solving: Smooth Transitions Help Learning", *Journal of Experimental Education* 70(4), 293-315. <https://doi.org/10.1080/00220970209599510>; a full-text copy was visible at <http://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2002-Renkl_et_al.pdf> **[PART]** (abstract, the discussion of experiment 3 and the general discussion).
- **Also.** Renkl (2014), "Toward an Instructionally Oriented Theory of Example-Based Learning", *Cognitive Science* 38(1), 1-37, <https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12086> **[ABS]** (bibliographic record and abstract only: it integrates worked examples, observational learning and analogical reasoning).

### A3. Expertise reversal: tune the scaffolding to what the reader already knows

- **Rule.** Help that is good for novices can become useless or harmful for people who already know the material, so scaffold each topic according to the reader's prior knowledge of that topic.
- **Evidence.** Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller: "Instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners."
- **Application.** This reader is an expert in Lagrangians and ODEs and a novice in turbines and generators. Do not explain what a Euler-Lagrange equation is; do work every turbine and generator idea from the ground up, with examples. Mark clearly (a one-line "you know this" tag) the steps the reader may skip, so the expert is not forced through novice scaffolding.
- **Source.** Slava Kalyuga, Paul Ayres, Paul Chandler, John Sweller (2003), "The Expertise Reversal Effect", *Educational Psychologist* 38(1), 23-31. <https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4> **[ABS]**.

### A4. Concreteness fading: start with the real thing, then strip it down in visible steps

- **Rule.** Present an idea first in a concrete, familiar form, then in a stripped-down picture, then as symbols, and make the link between the stages explicit.
- **Evidence.** Fyfe, McNeil, Son and Goldstone review the technique and its roots in Bruner (1966): "(1) an enactive form, which is a physical, concrete model of the concept; (2) an iconic form, which is a graphic or pictorial model; and finally (3) a symbolic form". Four proposed benefits: it helps learners "interpret ambiguous or opaque abstract symbols in terms of well-understood concrete objects"; it gives "embodied perceptual and physical experiences that can ground abstract thinking"; it builds "a store of memorable images that can be used when abstract symbols lose meaning"; and it guides learners "to strip away extraneous concrete properties". Direct support for the full three-step version: McNeil and Fyfe (2012) taught undergraduates modular arithmetic; the concreteness-fading group had the best transfer "at all three time points" (immediately, 1 week, 3 weeks). Goldstone and Son (2005): in two experiments with computer simulations, transfer was better when the look of the elements switched, and "the best transfer was observed when originally concrete elements became idealized".
- **Caveat the authors give.** Kaminski, Sloutsky and Heckler (2008) found students who learned a concept from an abstract example alone did better than those given a concrete example then an abstract one; Fyfe et al. say those examples were shown one after another in isolation, not as an explicit fading progression. The evidence for fading is mostly mathematics and simulations with students, not self-study text.
- **Application.** For the rod: (1) a hand-held bucket or a child on a roundabout, (2) a sketch of the rod on the blade with arrows, (3) the equation. Keep a visible link between the symbol and the picture it came from (label each term in the equation with the arrow it stands for). Remove the decoration at each stage so that the reader sees what stays the same.
- **Sources.** Emily R. Fyfe, Nicole M. McNeil, Ji Y. Son, Robert L. Goldstone (2014), "Concreteness Fading in Mathematics and Science Instruction: A Systematic Review", *Educational Psychology Review* 26, 9-25. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3> **[PART]** (abstract plus excerpts of the main text from the author's copy on ResearchGate: <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262943993>). ERIC record: <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1036777>.
  Robert L. Goldstone and Ji Y. Son (2005), "The Transfer of Scientific Principles Using Concrete and Idealized Simulations", *Journal of the Learning Sciences* 14(1), 69-110. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1401_4> **[ABS]**.
  Fyfe and Nathan (2018), "Making 'concreteness fading' more concrete as a theory of instruction for promoting transfer", *Educational Review*. <https://doi.org/10.1080/00131911.2018.1424116> **[ABS]**.

### A5. Misconceptions and conceptual change in physics

- **Rule.** Learners arrive with working everyday theories of motion and force; telling them the right answer does not remove the old theory, so state the common wrong picture, show where it fails, and replace it with something plausible and useful.
- **Evidence (Hestenes, Wells, Swackhamer).** "Every student begins physics with a well-established system of commonsense beliefs about how the physical world works derived from years of personal experience." They report that commonsense beliefs are "incompatible with Newtonian concepts in most respects" and that "conventional physics instruction produces little change in these beliefs." Their inventory taxonomy lists a category "Centrifugal force" under "Other Influences on Motion" (flagged by choices 4C,D,E and 10C,D,E; item 4 is a heavy ball swung on a string whose string breaks, item 10 a ball leaving a semicircular channel; the authors add that the inventory has no item built specifically to probe it, so it is "only suggested by the form that the listed items take"), and they write: "We have encountered high-school physics teachers who think that centrifugal force is a distinct kind of force. Such is the power of a name!" They also ask that these beliefs "be accorded the same respect we give to scientific concepts" and be treated as "reasonable hypotheses grounded in everyday experience".
- **Evidence (Redish 1994).** Four principles: people build mental models; it is easy to learn what extends an existing model (Corollary 2.1: "It's hard to learn something we don't almost already know"); "It is very difficult to change an established mental model substantially"; and learners differ (Principle 4). For a replacement idea to take hold (Corollary 3.1) "It must be understandable. It must be plausible. There must be a strong conflict with predictions based on the existing model. The new model must be seen as useful." Corollary 1.2: knowing the right statement is not enough; the learner must access it at the right time and be able to cross-check it.
- **Evidence (Hake).** 62 introductory courses, N = 6542: "traditional" courses gained 0.23 ± 0.04 of the possible score on the force-concept tests, "interactive-engagement" courses 0.48 ± 0.14.
- **McDermott.** The Millikan lecture "What we teach and what is learned: closing the gap" (AJP 1991) is described by PER-Central as exploring the "incompatibility" between curriculum materials and students' understandings of introductory physics and "the key role of educational research to guide practice"; I saw only that description (the paper is paywalled), so I draw no claim from it.
- **Application.** Name the wrong picture the learner is likely to hold ("the rod is thrown outward by a force from the centre"), run it against a prediction it gets wrong, then give the replacement (the frame is accelerating; the pull is a bookkeeping term that makes F = ma work in that frame). Do not label the wrong picture "stupid": the FCI authors treat it as a reasonable hypothesis.
- **Sources.**
  David Hestenes, Malcolm Wells, Gregg Swackhamer (1992), "Force Concept Inventory", *The Physics Teacher* 30(3), 141-158. Open copy: <https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/FCI.PDF> **[PART]** (I read the introduction, Tables I and II and the discussion of "Other Influences on Motion"; I did not read the data sections).
  Edward F. Redish (1994), "Implications of cognitive studies for teaching physics", *Am. J. Phys.* 62(9), 796-803. Open copy: <https://physics.umd.edu/perg/papers/redish/cogsci.html> **[PART]** (read the four principles and their corollaries; skimmed the rest).
  Edward F. Redish (2003), *Teaching Physics with the Physics Suite*, Wiley. Preface only: <https://www.PhysPort.org/recommendations/files/RedishBookPreface.pdf> **[FULL]** (the 2-page preface; it says the book is "a handbook with a variety of tools" and rests on classroom observation, cognitive-science experiments and neuroscience plausibility; I did not get the chapters).
  Richard R. Hake (1998), "Interactive-engagement versus traditional methods: a six-thousand-student survey of mechanics test data for introductory physics courses", *Am. J. Phys.* 66, 64-74. <https://doi.org/10.1119/1.18809> **[ABS]**.

### A6. Predict, observe, explain

- **Rule.** Before showing the result, ask the learner to commit to a prediction and a reason; then show the result; then make them reconcile any conflict.
- **Evidence.** White and Gunstone's chapter abstract: POE "probes understanding by requiring students to carry out three tasks. First they must predict the outcome of some event, and must justify their prediction; then they describe what they see happen; and finally they must reconcile any conflict between prediction and observation." The NSTA teacher guide to POE sequences lists the steps (orientation, experiment, written prediction with reasons, discussion, observation, explanation, then "This is what scientists currently think") and warns "students' ideas often are resistant to change and there is no guarantee that a POE will do the trick".
- **Application (self-learner version).** A boxed "Predict" prompt with a fold-out answer: "A mass on a rod is carried round in a circle. When you turn with it, which way does a pendulum hang?" The reader writes a guess, then reads on. The strongest candidates here are the ones where the common guess is wrong (the answer to "will a freely hinged rod hang down or stick out?").
- **Sources.** Richard White and Richard Gunstone (1992), *Probing Understanding*, Falmer/Routledge, ch. 3 "Prediction-Observation-Explanation". <https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9780203761342-3/prediction-observation-explanation-richard-white-richard-gunstone> **[ABS]**.
  NSTA, *Predict, Observe, Explain: Activities Enhancing Scientific Understanding* (introduction, "Using POE Sequences"): <https://us.sagepub.com/sites/default/files/upm-binaries/50697_Using_POE_Sequences.pdf> **[PART]** (introduction and the step list).

### A7. Multiple representations

- **Rule.** Give the same situation in several forms (words, a sketch, a free-body diagram, a graph, an equation), do the qualitative forms first, and make the learner translate between them.
- **Evidence.** Van Heuvelen's review abstract: conventional instruction leaves students "unable to reason qualitatively about physical processes", with "primitive formula-centered problem-solving techniques", and minds holding "a small number of facts and equations that are accessible only by random searches". His companion paper describes a course in which "students actively construct a knowledge hierarchy on a foundation of qualitative understanding" with "repeated exposure to concepts in a variety of contexts". Rosengrant, Van Heuvelen and Etkina (2009): in a two-year study, students who drew free-body diagrams correctly did noticeably better on the problems, and high achievers used the diagrams both to solve and to check.
- **Application.** For each new situation (rod on the blade, wind through the disc), offer: the everyday sentence, a labelled sketch, a diagram of forces in the right frame, a graph of the potential or the power, and then the equation. Add a one-line check at the end ("the units are N", "the sign is toward the hub").
- **Sources.** Alan Van Heuvelen (1991), "Learning to think like a physicist: a review of research-based instructional strategies", *Am. J. Phys.* 59, 891-897. <https://doi.org/10.1119/1.16667> **[ABS]**. Alan Van Heuvelen (1991), "Overview, Case Study Physics", *Am. J. Phys.* 59, 898-907. <https://doi.org/10.1119/1.16668> **[ABS]**. David Rosengrant, Alan Van Heuvelen, Eugenia Etkina (2009), "Do students use and understand free-body diagrams?", *Phys. Rev. ST Phys. Educ. Res.* 5, 010108. Record: <https://works.bepress.com/david-rosengrant/34/> **[ABS]** (summary from record; the APS page refused my download).

### A8. Self-explanation and the limits of "study methods"

- **Rule.** Readers learn more from worked examples when they explain each step to themselves (why this step, which principle), so write examples that invite that, and test yourself rather than re-read.
- **Evidence.** Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann and Glaser had students talk aloud over worked mechanics examples: "Good" students "generate many explanations which refine and expand the conditions for the action parts of the example solutions, and relate these actions to principles in the text"; "Poor" students "do not generate sufficient self-explanations, monitor their learning inaccurately, and subsequently rely heavily on examples." In Dunlosky et al.'s review of ten study techniques, practice testing and distributed practice got "high utility", self-explanation and elaborative interrogation "moderate", and rereading and highlighting "low".
- **Application.** At each step of a worked example, a margin prompt in plain words: "Why did the sine appear here?" Collapsed answer below. End each section with 2 to 3 short "can you" checks that need the reader to produce something (a number, a sign, a sketch), not to recognise it.
- **Sources.** Michelene T. H. Chi, Miriam Bassok, Matthew W. Lewis, Peter Reimann, Robert Glaser (1989), "Self-explanations: how students study and use examples in learning to solve problems", *Cognitive Science* 13, 145-182. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1> **[PART]** (abstract and discussion). John Dunlosky, Katherine A. Rawson, Elizabeth J. Marsh, Mitchell J. Nathan, Daniel T. Willingham (2013), "Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4-58. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266> **[ABS]**.

### A9. Redish's "touchstone problems"

- **Rule.** Pick a few simple systems that the reader will meet again and again, and teach them thoroughly; later topics then reduce to them.
- **Evidence.** Redish, Corollary 2.3: "'Touchstone' problems and examples are very important. By a touchstone problem, I mean one that the student will come back to over and over again in later training." Example in the paper: the mass on a spring, "not really of much interest in itself, but it serves as a touchstone problem for all kinds of harmonic oscillation from electrical circuits up to quantum field theory."
- **Application.** The mass on a spring is also the right touchstone here: the rod held out by the centrifugal pull behaves like a spring, the generator is a damper, and the power question is the driven damped oscillator. Teach it once, cleanly, in the first part and refer back by name.
- **Source.** Redish (1994), as in A5. **[PART]**

### A10. Feynman's way of explaining

Four habits that can be read directly from Feynman's own words.

1. **Say what is deduced and what is new.** From his 1963 preface: "I also felt that for such students it is important to indicate what it is that they should ... be able to understand by deduction from what has been said before, and what is being put in as something new. When new ideas came in, I would try either to deduce them if they were deducible, or to explain that it was a new idea which hadn't any basis in terms of things they had already learned and which was not supposed to be provable but was just added in." Also: "I tried very hard to make all the statements as accurate as possible, to point out in every case where the equations and ideas fitted into the body of physics, and how ... things would be modified."
2. **A backbone for the reader who cannot take it all.** "I wanted there to be at least a central core or backbone of material which he could get. Even if he didn't understand everything in a lecture, I hoped he wouldn't get nervous."
3. **Look at the thing before you name it.** In "What is Science?" he tells of a ball in a wagon: "When I pull the wagon, the ball runs to the back, and when I am running with the wagon and stop, the ball runs to the front. Why?" His father said no one knows why, then described what really happens ("If you look close you will see the ball does not run to the back of the wagon ... It moves forward a bit too, but not as fast as the wagon") and then gave the name, inertia: "He put the difference between what we know and what we call it very distinctly." This is the apparent-force story in its simplest form: in the wagon's frame the ball is "pushed" backward; in the ground frame nothing pushes it.
4. **Test whether you taught an idea or a word.** "Without using the new word which you have just learned, try to rephrase what you have just learned in your own language." If the reader cannot, "you learned nothing about science."
   He also says it is "a good idea to know when we are teaching the tools of science, such as words, and when we are teaching science itself".
   In Feynman Lectures Vol. I, section 12-5, he introduces "pseudo forces" with a moving observer (Joe and Moe), the water in an accelerated jar, and the rotating box: "An observer in a rotating coordinate system, e.g., in a rotating box, will find mysterious forces, not accounted for by any known origin of force, throwing things outward toward the walls. These forces are due merely to the fact that the observer does not have Newton's coordinate system."
- **Application.** Open each new term (centrifugal, impedance, tip-speed ratio) with the observation, then the name; close the section with a "say it without the word" check. Tag derived results "derived" and assumed ones "assumed (put in, not proved)".
- **Sources.** Richard P. Feynman (1963), "Feynman's Preface", *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*. <https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_91.html> **[PART]** (the first four-fifths or so of the preface, as reproduced by search highlights and an identical copy at <https://antilogicalism.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/feynman-lectures.pdf>; the closing remarks, which start with the lecture on inertial guidance, were cut off). Richard P. Feynman (1969), "What Is Science?", *The Physics Teacher* 7(6), 313-320 (talk of 1966). <http://feynman.com/science/what-is-science/> **[PART]** (read the wagon story, the energy-in-the-first-grade-book passage and the "rephrase without the word" test). *Feynman Lectures* Vol. I ch. 12: <https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_12.html> **[PART]** (section 12-5 read through search excerpts; sections 12-1 to 12-4 read in the fetched text).

### A11. Mayer: signalling, segmenting, coherence (and pretraining)

- **Rule.** Cut material that does not serve the point; mark the structure of what remains; split into short pieces; teach the names and parts of a system before the story of how it works.
- **Evidence (Mayer and Moreno's review table, narrated animations, college students).** Coherence ("weeding"): better transfer when extraneous material is left out, median effect size 0.90 (5 experiments). Signalling: better transfer with cues for how to process the material, 0.74 (1 experiment; cues were stressed key words, arrows, an outline with headings, and a map of the lesson). Segmenting: better transfer with learner-controlled pieces, 1.36 (1 experiment; 16 segments of one to two sentences). Pretraining: "better transfer when students know names and behaviors of system components", 1.00 (3 experiments); it "involves a specific sequencing strategy in which components are presented before a causal system is" explained. Spatial contiguity: better transfer when printed words sit near the matching parts of a graphic, 0.48 (1 experiment).
- **Caveat.** These numbers come from short narrated animations (lightning, pumps), few experiments each; effects in long printed notes are an extension, not a measured result. The signalling study was about airplane lift.
- **Application.** Before the chain wind -> blade -> shaft -> gearbox -> generator -> grid is explained, show a labelled picture of each component and what it does in one line (pretraining). Put labels inside figures, not in a legend (spatial contiguity). Remove side stories from the main line and put them in clearly marked boxes (coherence). Use headings, a "where we are" line at the start of each section, and bold key terms once (signalling). Keep sections short enough to be read in one sitting (segmenting).
- **Source.** Richard E. Mayer and Roxana Moreno (2003), "Nine ways to reduce cognitive load in multimedia learning", *Educational Psychologist* 38(1), 43-52. Open copy: <https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/WDFR/MayerMoreno9WaysToReduceCognitiveLoad.pdf> **[PART]** (read Table 3 and the segmenting, pretraining, weeding and signalling sections).

## B. How wind turbines are taught from scratch

Summary of what the sources do (details below). Nearly all start with the power in a moving column of air, ½ρAv³, then the actuator disc and the Betz limit, then lift, the velocity triangle and the tip-speed ratio, then blade-element momentum (BEM). The main split is whether the aerofoil comes before the disc (Hansen) or after it (Burton, Manwell, Delft, MIT). Generators, gearbox and electrical parts come later, as a separate block (Manwell ch. 5, DTU 46300, Delft).

### B1. Hansen, *Aerodynamics of Wind Turbines* (DTU)

- **Order (3rd ed., 2015).** 1 General introduction to wind turbines (5 pages); 2 Two-dimensional aerodynamics; 3 Three-dimensional aerodynamics; 4 One-dimensional momentum theory for an ideal wind turbine; 5 Shrouded rotors; 6 The classical blade element momentum method; 7 Control/regulation and safety systems; 8 Optimization; 9 Unsteady BEM; 10 Introduction to loads and structures; 11 Beam theory for blades; 12 Dynamic structural model; 13 Sources for loads; 14 Wind simulation; 15 Fatigue; 16 Vertical-axis turbines; 17 Final remarks. Hansen says it is written from "notes from two courses given at the Technical University of Denmark" and that "Chapters 1-8, 10 and 11 could form the basis of an undergraduate course in basic wind turbine technology."
- **First picture / first equation.** Chapter 1 opens with the wind resource: the maximum available power is found "if theoretically the wind speed could be reduced to zero", P_max = ½ ṁ V₀² = ½ ρ A V₀³ (his eq. 1.1), "very important since it tells us that the power increases with the cube of the wind speed and only linearly with density and area". The power coefficient C_p and the Betz limit (16/27 = 0.593) are stated at once, and "modern optimized wind turbines operate close to this limit with Cp up to 0.5".
- **Analogy.** "Sailors discovered very early that it is more efficient to use the lift force rather than simple drag as the main source of propulsion."
- **Sticking-point choice.** Aerofoil lift and drag (ch. 2) come before the momentum theory (ch. 4); the Betz derivation is left to ch. 4 but quoted in ch. 1.
- **Source.** Martin O. L. Hansen (2015), *Aerodynamics of Wind Turbines*, 3rd ed., Routledge. Contents and ch. 1 opening seen in the publisher preview: <https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781317671039_A24764877/preview-9781317671039_A24764877.pdf>; contents also <https://www.routledge.com/Aerodynamics-of-Wind-Turbines/Hansen/p/book/9781138775077> **[PART]** (contents and the first pages only).

### B2. DTU course 46300 and DTU rotor-aerodynamics slides

- **Course order.** DTU "Wind Turbine Technology and Aerodynamics" (13 weeks): placement (wind atlas, terrain, annual wind distribution); two-dimensional aerodynamics and 3D effects; blade element momentum theory "to determine the energy production for a rotor with a given twist and chord distribution"; control (stall, pitch, variable speed); structure (static deflections and natural vibrations of blades); then an electrical block (25 % of the course): Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws, coils and capacitors, AC phasors, active and reactive power, three-phase systems, and "high level component introduction to models and operation: transformers, generators cables, converters". Prerequisites: basic fluid mechanics and programming. One learning objective is "Draw velocity triangle incl. induced velocities".
- **Slides.** The DTU rotor-aerodynamics slide set opens with a five-line chain: "The wind hits the rotor plane; the combination of wind speed and blade rotation results in a pressure distribution on the rotor blades; the pressure distribution causes a turning moment (torque); the turning moment rotates the shaft; the shaft is coupled to a generator that produces electrical power." It then asks "What is the optimum number of blades? What is the optimum operating condition (TSR)? What is the maximum efficiency?" and answers "Why 3 blades?" with four reasons: aerodynamics ("close to optimum"), structural dynamics ("no gyroscopic forces in yaw"), looks (harmonic rotation) and history (the "Danish Concept" tradition). It also tabulates the best C_p against tip-speed ratio for a rotor whose wake rotates: 0.288 at TSR 0.5, 0.416 at 1.0, 0.480 at 1.5, 0.512 at 2.0, 0.532 at 2.5, 0.570 at 5, 0.582 at 7.5, and 0.593 at 10.
- **DTU's open Coursera course "Wind Energy" (16 modules).** It starts with an overview and then economics (module 2), wind atlases (3), wind farm design (4), forecasting (5), how and why we measure the wind (6, with a cup-anemometer calibration exercise), remote sensing (7), load measurements (8), and only then, in module 9, "One-Dimensional Momentum Theory and the Betz Limit for Maximum Power Production" by Prof. Jens N. Sørensen (a 19-minute video, a reading and a 30-minute assignment); I did not see modules 10 to 16. It lists "wind measurements, resource assessment, forecasting, aerodynamics, wind turbine technology, structural mechanics, materials, financial and electrical systems". <https://www.coursera.org/learn/wind-energy> **[PART]**.
- **Sources.** DTU Learn for Life, course 46300: <https://lifelonglearning.dtu.dk/en/wind/single-course/wind-turbine-technology-and-aerodynamics/> **[PART]** (course page). DTU course material "The Aerodynamics of Wind Turbines" (2019): <http://vortex.compute.dtu.dk/coursematerial/Rotor-Aerodynamics-2019.pdf> **[PART]** (excerpts only; slide text extraction was partial).

### B3. TU Delft: lecture notes and open course material

- **"Introduction to wind turbine design" (van Kuik and Bierbooms, DUWIND, 2002).** Order: 1 Introduction; 2 Wind modelling (atmospheric boundary layer, mean wind speed, wind statistics, turbulence, extreme winds); 3 Principles of aerodynamic theory (3.1 BEM theory, 3.2 aerofoil characteristics); 4 P-V curve and power control; 5 Energy production. The aerodynamics section opens with the control volume of length UΔt and cross-section A: mass flow ṁ = ρUA, momentum flow ρU²A and energy flow ½ρU³A. The rotor is first an actuator disc, with a pressure jump Δp = D/A. The velocity at the disc is the mean of the up- and down-stream velocities (their eq. 10), then the induction factor a, then C_p = 4a(1−a)², maximum 16/27 at a = 1/3. The physical reason is given in words: "Physically, the occurrence of a maximum is easy to explain: a very large rotor force decelerates the flow too much, by which the mass flow through the rotor disc is small. A small rotor force affects the mass flow only little, but does not extract energy from the flow." It credits Lanchester as first. Next, Glauert's annuli, then "a cross-section through a blade at radius r, seen from a co-rotating observer at the blade tip": a velocity triangle of Ωr and U(1−a) giving the resultant speed, angle of attack and the lift and drag directions. They note that Ω, pitch θ and U are independent and a is the dependent link, and that the calculation "is iterative". Then the tip-speed ratio λ and C_p(λ).

### B8. John C. Bean, "Wind Power" (web lecture notes, revised August 2026): a teacher who tackles beginner sticking points head-on

### B11. Common sticking points the sources warn about (collected)

1. **Swept area against blade area** (Bean): slender blades look as though they intercept little wind.
2. **Why the wind cannot be stopped** (Delft, MIT, MacKay, Wikipedia): the "all the energy" intuition fails because stopped air blocks the next air; the maximum lies between "too little" and "too much" slowdown.
3. **The speed at the disc is the mean of the up- and down-stream speeds** (Delft eq. 10; Wikipedia's Betz article calls it "arguably the most counter-intuitive stage of the derivation of Betz's law", <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betz%27s_law>). It is also challenged in the literature (Zhang, *Energies* 15, 5902 (2022); Khan 2020), which I saw as abstracts only; treat as contested by a few authors, not as textbook consensus.
4. **Betz is not the efficiency of a machine.** The limit applies to an ideal disc; real machines add blade drag, wake rotation, mechanical and electrical losses. A Michigan Tech course note writes the chain as P_e = C_p η_m η_g P_w (power coefficient, transmission efficiency, generator efficiency) and lists the Betz assumptions: "infinite number of blades", "no frictional drag", "non-rotating wake" (<https://pages.mtu.edu/~jstallen/courses/MEEM4200/lectures/wind/wind_energy.pdf> **[PART]**). Wikipedia states that "Practical utility-scale wind turbines achieve at peak 75-80% of the Betz limit" (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betz%27s_law> **[PART]**, a tertiary source).
5. **Tip faster than the wind** (Bean, Fthenakis): a lift device can outrun the wind; a drag device cannot (TSR ≤ 1).
6. **Lift explanations** (Anderson and Eberhardt): avoid the equal-transit-time story.
7. **Twist along the blade** (Fthenakis): the local wind angle changes with radius, so a blade is twisted, unlike a wing.
8. **Aerodynamics before or after the disc** (Hansen against Burton/Manwell): both orders are in use; Burton puts aerofoil basics in an appendix and asks for lift and drag as a prerequisite.

### C3. Effective potential, the Lagrangian in a rotating frame, and the Hamiltonian that is not the energy

- **Where textbooks put the effective potential.** Morin 7.2 "The effective potential" (central forces); Taylor 8.4 "The equivalent one-dimensional problems" (after 4.7 "Curvilinear one-dimensional systems" and 4.8 "Central forces" in the energy chapter); K&K "The energy equation and energy diagrams" (9.5 in the 1st ed., 10.4 in the 2nd). I read the contents lines only, not these sections, so I cannot say how each text derives the centrifugal term. Physics SE gives both readings side by side: the accepted answer to 37968 says the same Lagrangian L = ½μ(ṙ² + r²θ̇²) − U(r) comes from "an inertial frame with no fictitious forces" (the angular kinetic energy) or from "a rotating frame following the reduced particle with fictitious forces and only 1D radial kinematics", where "−½μr²θ̇² is the centrifugal potential (notice the minus sign!)".
- **The rotating hoop and E ≠ H.** Physics SE 37725 (below, D5) asks why for the bead on a rotating hoop the Hamiltonian H = p²/2mR² − ½mR²ω² sin²φ + mgR(1 − cos φ) is conserved while the mechanical energy E (with a plus sign on the ω² term) is not. A reply by the user Diego Mazón: the hoop's motor supplies the difference; "K2 is the kinetic term which does not depend on velocities φ̇ ... for the Hamiltonian it is an effective potential term". Another reply states the sufficient conditions for H = E: velocity-independent potential, and no explicit time in the coordinate transformation. This is exactly the point a Lagrangian-trained learner meets on the rotating-frame effective potential.
- **Application.** State early which quantity is the "energy" in each frame, and say that the rotor, not the rod, pays for the difference (the notes' "who pays the energy" topic).

### C4. Documented misconceptions about centrifugal force

1. **Hestenes, Wells and Swackhamer (1992)**, Force Concept Inventory taxonomy: category 5 "Other influences on motion", item "CF. Centrifugal force", suggested by choices on the string-and-ball and channel questions; "We have encountered high-school physics teachers who think that centrifugal force is a distinct kind of force. Such is the power of a name!" <https://davidhestenes.net/modeling/R&E/FCI.PDF> **[PART]**. The same taxonomy lists "circular impetus" (I5: choices 4A,D; 10A), described as an impetus "that tends to move objects in circles", which a few students justify by a "training metaphor" (objects tend to do what they have been "trained" to do).
2. **Küchemann, Klein, Fouckhardt, Gröber, Kuhn** (arXiv 2019, "Improving students' understanding of rotating frames of reference using videos from different perspectives"; published as "Students' understanding of non-inertial frames of reference", *Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res.* 16, 010112 (2020)). Predict-observe-explain with a sphere rolling over a rotating disc, first-semester physics students: "only one out of five" gave the right observation. Three difficulties: (a) the centrifugal force is believed to be needed only if the object is coupled to the rotating system (42.8 % of participants, which the authors trace to teaching that mixes up the inertial centrifugal force and the reactive centrifugal force of a carousel; they recommend naming the two differently); (b) inertial forces are believed to cause a reaction of the object on which they act; (c) the trajectories in the stationary and the rotating frame are mixed up. Also (d) "about one out of four physics students (23.8 %) do not include the fictitious character of inertial forces in their arguments and rather argue that they have the same effect on objects as real forces". The authors also point out that the Coriolis force alone gives a circle for a free object crossing a rotating disc, and that the centrifugal force is needed to get the spiral that the rotating-frame observer actually sees. Sources: <https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10216> and the HTML full text <https://arxiv.org/html/1902.10216v1> **[PART]** (abstract, sections II.1 and V.1); journal version <https://journals.aps.org/prper/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.16.010112> **[ABS]**.
3. **Volfson, Eshach, Ben-Abu (2020)**, "Identifying physics misconceptions at the circus: the case of circular motion", *Phys. Rev. Phys. Educ. Res.* 16, 010134. Focus groups after a circus act with spinning bowls of water: "Students mistakenly believe that when an object spins in a circular path, there is real force acting on the object in the radial direction pulling it out of the path"; "Only about 2% of the participants provided a correct explanation for why the water stayed in the bowls"; centrifugal force was the most often expressed idea (about 40 %), and 56 % predicted a released bowl flies "in a radial direction, outside the circle". A frequent naive reason: gravity mg "is compensated by the centrifugal force on the top of the circular trajectory". <https://journals.aps.org/prper/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.16.010134> **[PART]** (abstract, results, discussion opening).
4. **Isacsson, Pendrill, Sundberg**, "Embodied experiences of the equivalence principle and the temptation of inertial forces" (*EURASIA J. Math. Sci. Tech. Educ.*, 2026): "while most new students have learned to avoid 'centrifugal forces', many of them are still tempted to draw or choose force diagrams including an outward force in some cases of circular motion, but referring to a 'centripetal force', and many others prefer a diagram with a centripetal force as a real force." They propose using the weak equivalence principle and "embodied experiences of acceleration" and "kinetic diagrams" next to free-body diagrams ("force equilibrium does not apply for accelerating bodies"). <https://doi.org/10.29333/ejmste/18964> **[ABS]**.
5. **Taibu and Mataka (2025)**, "Textbook presentation of circular motion dynamics: centrifugal force controversy and implications for teaching", *Electronic Journal for Research in Science and Mathematics Education*. Of 20 introductory textbooks, "about half ... did not use or mention the term centrifugal force" when discussing riders in rotating systems; 35 % "discussed real vs. pseudo forces clearly"; 10 % "explicitly warned students or suggested not to use the term". They propose a teaching approach that "explicitly acknowledges (1) the controversy rather than shunning away from it, (2) the perceptions within rotating frames, and (3) the interdisciplinary use [of] the concept of centrifugal force." <https://ejrsme.icrsme.com/article/view/23765> **[ABS]**.
6. **What the Physics SE answers add (D, below):** the "I can feel it" argument is answered by "proper acceleration" (an accelerometer in a rotating frame measures the real contact force, not the fictitious one); the "pendulum bob goes backwards" question is answered by Newton's first law seen from outside the car.

### D1. Why a turbine cannot take all the wind's energy (Betz)

1. **"Why is there no upper-bound on power-efficiency of Hydro-turbines?"** score 8 (answer 11, accepted), 3.2 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/112178/why-is-there-no-upper-bound-on-power-efficiency-of-hydro-turbines>. *Confused by:* Betz's law limits wind turbines but not water turbines; is it the incompressibility of water? *Top answer:* "Betz' limit arises from the fact that if the turbine tries to take too much energy out of the flow, the wind will divert and go around the turbine instead of through it. With a hydrothermal [sic; hydroelectric] system the water is typically behind a dam, so the water has nowhere else to go", and the turbines "effectively convert gravitational potential energy into work".
2. **"Optimal speed for the water wheel"** score 8, 4.3 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/120118/optimal-speed-for-the-water-wheel>. *Confused by:* a slower wheel leaves less energy in the exit stream, so efficiency should be highest at zero speed, but then no power is delivered; where is the best speed? *Top answer (score 2):* "There is indeed an optimal arrangement ... The highest efficiency possible is 16/27 ... The ratio of exit speed to entry speed is 1:3 at the Betz limit." (Thin answer; the question itself is a good statement of the trade-off.)
3. **"Why does the pressure drop behind a windmill actuator disk?"** score 2, 312 views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/668097/why-does-the-pressure-drop-behind-a-windmill-actuator-disk>. *Confused by:* the pressure falls below ambient just behind the disc, which seems unmotivated. *Top answer:* it is "the result of the calculation", not an assumption; the model gives v = (v₁ + v₂)/2.
4. **"Is it possible that windmills can consume the wind?"** score 5, <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/12036/is-it-possible-that-windmills-can-consume-the-wind>. *Answer:* wind is driven by pressure differences that sunlight keeps creating; the only effect is that "the upwind ones will spoil it for the down-wind ones, just as a sailing ship can spoil the wind for any directly behind it."
5. **"Do wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of air?"** score 2, 5.4 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/143155/do-wind-turbines-convert-the-kinetic-energy-of-air>. *Confused by:* with lift-based blades designers minimise drag; a "perfect" turbine with infinite lift-to-drag ratio would leave the air with the same kinetic energy, so how can it "convert the kinetic energy of the wind"? *Top answer (score 6):* "The principle of conservation of energy is your friend. The wind turbine generates electricity. That energy has to come from somewhere ... the kinetic energy of the air downwind of the rotor is less than the kinetic energy upwind of the rotor. Your 'perfect' wind turbine would be useless." Follow-up **184648** ("How is the kinetic energy of the wind transferred onto a lift based wind turbine?", score 1): the blades "deflect the flow of air ... By Newton's third law ... the deflected air pushes on the sails", producing torque.

### D2. Why blade tips move faster than the wind

1. **"How to sail downwind faster than the wind?"** score 23, 6.1 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/931/how-to-sail-downwind-faster-than-the-wind>. *Confused by:* how a wind-powered land vehicle can move directly downwind faster than the wind (the asker cannot see how it can push forward both with a tailwind and with a headwind). *Top answer (score 5):* energy is removed from the wind field through the gearing; "In fact wind turbine blades travel at right angles to the wind at multiples of the wind speed. But that is different." (A short answer; the question is the standard stumbling block for "faster than the wind".)
2. **"Why is a beam reach the fastest point of sail on modern sailboats?"** score 17, 38.6 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/186515/why-is-a-beam-reach-the-fastest-point-of-sail-on-modern-sailboats>. *Confused by:* lift should grow with apparent wind speed, so sailing upwind should be faster. *Top answer (score 9):* in general it is not the fastest; "for boats that sail faster than the wind, the limiting factor for speed is how close they can sail to the apparent wind; when they are going faster than the wind speed the apparent wind is always forward of the beam". This is the apparent-wind picture needed for blades (compare B8, B9).
3. **"Maximum rotation rate of a turbine in a fluid with a given speed"** score 1, 233 views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/656018/maximum-rotation-rate-of-a-turbine-in-a-fluid-with-a-given-speed>. The asker states the tip-speed ratio λ = ωR/v, notes it is "often over 1", and asks if there is a maximum rotation rate. (No answers.)
4. **"How could more rotor speed mean less energy in wind turbine?"** score 1, 1.2 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/411954/how-could-more-rotor-speed-mean-less-energy-in-wind-turbine>. *Confused by:* a power-against-rpm chart in which a turbine at 15 rpm produces less than at 12 rpm in 6 m/s wind; a faster shaft should turn the generator faster. *Accepted answer (score 2):* "Current produced by a generator tends to be proportional to the torque on its shaft, Voltage tends to be proportional to its speed, and output power is the product of current and voltage" and the two extremes (open circuit gives zero torque; short circuit gives zero voltage) both give zero power, so "In between those two extremes ... significant power can be converted". This is the best-matched explanation I found for the notes' best-damping topic.

### D3. How lift turns a rotor, and how the generator sees the rotor's speed

1. **"Why airplanes fly: the final truth"** score 11, 4.5 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/31019/why-airplanes-fly-the-final-truth>. *Confused by:* competing explanations (Bernoulli, downward push, Anderson's argument). *Top answer (score 21, accepted):* flight "does not require" any airfoil shape: "You could build an airplane that would fly with completely flat rectangular wings made out of plywood. ### D4. Why three slender blades (and why not more, fewer or smaller)

1. **"Why does a wind turbine have only three blades?"** score 4, 1.1 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/192713/why-does-a-wind-turbine-have-only-three-blades>. *Confused by:* "Intuitively, the more leaves the more power." *Accepted answer (score 11):* the plant is not optimising for power: "it's trying to maximize energy while minimizing cost and part failures", e.g. a one-blade design with a counterweight was studied at TU Delft; more blades need a better, more failure-prone drive at higher rpm.
2. **"Why do blades of windturbine propellers as comp. to propellers of ships cover very different areas?"** score 8, 2.6 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/11241/why-do-blades-of-windturbine-propellers-as-comp-to-propellers-of-ships-cover-v>. *Confused by:* wind-turbine blades cover a small part of the circle they sweep, while ship and Kaplan propellers cover nearly all of it. *Top answer (score 6, not accepted):* "it's not just about water vs air. 3. **"Wind turbine: single large propeller or multiple small propellers?"** score 7. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/14859/wind-turbine-single-large-propeller-or-multiple-small-propellers>. *Answer (score 3):* power goes with the swept area πL², fewer moving parts, and a taller hub sees more wind.
4. **Engineering SE, "Why don't we just build a giant wind turbine?"** score 9. <https://engineering.stackexchange.com/questions/7567/why-dont-we-just-build-a-giant-wind-turbine>. *Confused by:* P ∝ L² V³, so go bigger. *Accepted answer (score 15):* "Not everything scales linearly" (the ant and elephant legs argument): supports, wind loading and manufacture grow faster than power.
5. **Engineering SE, "Why are wind turbines installed slightly 'nose up'...?"** score 20, 11 k views. <https://engineering.stackexchange.com/questions/57061/why-are-wind-turbines-installed-slightly-nose-up-as-opposed-to-the-blade-disk-being-perpendicular-to-the-ground>. ### D5. Centrifugal force, pendulum in a rotating frame

1. **"Is the centrifugal force a real force?"** score 24, 13 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/108925/is-the-centrifugal-force-a-real-force>. *Confused by:* "I can definitely feel it every time I take a sharp turn in a car"; machines such as centrifuges use it. *Accepted answer (score 38):* at a red light the street light obeys F = ma; once you accelerate, in your frame the light accelerates with no force, so "we define pseudo force as a correction term which enables us to apply Newton's second law in accelerating frames. It has no real existence"; what you feel in a turn is your inertia.
2. **"Why is centrifugal force called fictitious?"** score 18, 10.7 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/650245/why-is-centrifugal-force-called-fictitious>. *Confused by:* the rotating object "really feels" it, and time dilation is symmetric between inertial frames, so why privilege the inertial view? *Top answer (score 40):* "A person in a centrifuge actually feels their reaction to the centripetal force"; in a harshly accelerating car "there is no force pushing you back ... it is simply the result of your inertia."
3. **"Why do fictitious forces in a rotating frame produce real accelerations even though they don't obey Newton's 3rd law?"** score 14. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/855588/why-do-fictitious-forces-in-a-rotating-frame-produce-real-accelerations-even-tho>. *Confused by:* we feel and measure them like real forces. *Accepted answer (score 11, Dale):* "We don't. The acceleration that we 'feel and measure' is called proper acceleration ... In a rotating frame an accelerometer does not detect the fictitious centrifugal force, only the real centripetal force ... Coordinate systems are not real, nor are the coordinate accelerations they produce."
4. **"Pendulum with a rotating point of support from Landau-Lifschitz"** score 11, 10.4 k views. <https://physics.stackexchange.com/questions/100807/pendulum-with-a-rotating-point-of-support-from-landau-lifschitz>. But what force?" *Answers:* from the ground the bob simply stays put (Newton's first law) while the car moves forward; the string tension then supplies the real forward acceleration once the bob hangs at an angle; in the car frame the term −ma "is a physical tool introduced to make Newton's equation of motion work". **Wind and the rotor**

1. **Wind power through 1 m² at 10 m/s.** Use the column of air in one second (a hoop of area A, air length v × 1 s). *Check (computed):* ½ρv³ = 612 W/m² (650 W/m² with ρ = 1.3). *Used:* MacKay chapter B opens with the hoop picture and the two "air just before / one second later" drawings; Hansen eq. 1.1; MIT 22.081J lecture 5b. <https://www.withouthotair.com/cB/page_263.shtml>
2. **The cube.** The wind goes from 5 to 15 m/s: by what factor does the power in the wind change (27)? A rotor of twice the diameter in the same wind (4)? *Used:* the 5-to-15 m/s example in Järvinen et al., "Wind Energy" (Springer 2024) <https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-69856-9_5>; Hansen ch. 1 ("power increases with the cube of the wind speed and only linearly with density and area").
3. **MacKay's Wellington windmill.** Diameter 25 m, hub height 32 m, typical wind 6 m/s, "guess" 50 % efficient, ρ = 1.3: estimate the power. *Check (computed):* A = 491 m²; P ≈ ½ × 0.5 × 1.3 × 491 × 6³ ≈ 34 kW. MacKay then reports the real meter showing 60 kW on a very breezy day; what wind speed does that imply (computed: about 7.2 m/s)? *Used:* MacKay B.4 to B.6. <https://www.withouthotair.com/cB/page_264.shtml>
4. **Betz in three lines.** Using only mass flow, the energy loss ½ṁ(U² − U_e²), the thrust D = ṁ(U − U_e), and V = ½(U + U_e), derive C_p = 4a(1 − a)² and its maximum 16/27 at a = 1/3; then say in words why a = 1/2 (wake brought to rest far downstream, C_p = 0.5) is worse than a = 1/3, and why a → 1 (no flow through the disc) gives zero power. *Used:* van Kuik and Bierbooms eqs. 5 to 16 with the verbal reason; MIT 2.60J lecture 22; MacKay's one-paragraph version ("the departing wind speed is one third"). <https://ocw.tudelft.nl/wp-content/uploads/Introduction_to_wind_energy_design.pdf>
5. **Land-use check.** With spacing of 5 diameters and 50 % efficiency, show the farm's power per land area does not depend on the diameter. *Check (computed):* ½ × ½ρv³ × (π/4)/25 ≈ 2.2 W/m² for ρ = 1.3, v = 6 m/s; MacKay's figure is 2 W/m². *Used:* MacKay B.7 to B.10. <https://www.withouthotair.com/cB/page_265.shtml>
6. **Swept area against blade area.** A 63 m blade: what is the area the wind "sees"? How much power is in the wind through it at 10 m/s, and what is the Betz ceiling? *Check (computed):* A = πR² = 12,469 m²; ½ρAv³ ≈ 7.6 MW; 16/27 of that ≈ 4.5 MW (real machines reach about 0.45 of 7.6 = 3.4 MW). *Used:* Bean's swept-area discussion (the "slender blades" confusion) and Hansen's introductory C_p of "up to 0.5". <https://www.wecanfigurethisout.org/ENERGY/Web_notes/Wind/Wind%20Power.pdf>

**Lift, tip speed and the velocity triangle**

7. **Tip-speed ratio for the notes' rotor.** Tip radius 63 m at 1 rad/s: tip speed, rpm, and the tip-speed ratio in a 9 m/s wind. *Check (computed):* 63 m/s; 9.5 rpm; λ = 7 (λ is 9 at 7 m/s, 5.3 at 12 m/s). The sources give typical λ of 5 to 10 and 10 to 30 rpm for large rotors. *Used:* Fthenakis et al.; MIT 2.60J; Delft. <https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119854500.ch3>
8. **Velocity triangle at the tip and at a third of the radius.** Take the rotor-plane speed Ωr and an axial speed U(1 − a) = 6 m/s (U = 9, a = 1/3). Find the resultant speed and the angle from the rotor plane. *Check (computed):* tip 63.3 m/s at 5.4°; one third of the radius 21.8 m/s at 15.9°. So the blade must twist by about 10° from root to tip. (Fthenakis et al. show 72°, 81°, 84° from the rotor axis for their λ = 9 example.) *Used:* Delft's blade-element figure; DTU learning objective "Draw velocity triangle incl. induced velocities".
9. **Why a cup anemometer cannot beat the wind and a blade can.** Explain with one sentence each: a drag device (tip speed ratio at most 1) and a lift device (λ ≈ 7); name the sailing analogue. *Used:* Bean's cup anemometer, Savonius and sailboat slides; Hansen's "Sailors discovered very early ..." (B1, B8).
10. **Check the equal-transit-time story with numbers.** If the air over the top of a wing is 1.5 % faster than the air below, what fraction of the weight of a 2,300 lb aeroplane (wing area 174 ft², 65 mph) does Bernoulli's Δp = ½ρ(v_t² − v_b²) support? *Check (computed):* Δp ≈ 15 Pa over 16 m² ≈ 0.25 kN against 10.2 kN, about 2 to 3 %; the authors give "about 2%". *Used:* Anderson and Eberhardt's Cessna 172 calculation. <https://lss.fnal.gov/archive/2001/pub/Pub-01-036-E.pdf>

**Rotating and accelerating frames**

11. **Pendulum hung in an accelerating car, and the tilted water in a jar.** A = 3 m/s²: equilibrium angle and the change in small-oscillation frequency. *Check (computed):* φ = tan⁻¹(A/g) = 17.0°; g_eff = 10.26 m/s²; frequency up by 2.3 %. The jar's surface tilts by the same 17°. *Used:* Taylor ex. 9.1 (per course slides), K&K ex. 8.3, Feynman I-12-5 (jar of water). Predict-first version: ask "which way does the bob swing, and does the period get longer or shorter?" before the formula (Physics SE 645938, 588619).
12. **Bead on a rotating hoop.** Hoop R = 0.10 m rotating about a vertical diameter: at what ω does the bead leave the bottom, and where does it sit at 15 rad/s? *Check (computed):* ω_c = √(g/R) = 9.9 rad/s; above it cos θ = g/(Rω²) = 0.436 at 15 rad/s, so θ = 64°. *Used:* Physics SE 739636, 166162; Strogatz §3.5 (the overdamped version gives the pitchfork at γ = Rω²/g = 1).
13. **Bucket swung in a vertical circle.** R = 1 m: slowest speed at the top that keeps the water in; state the inertial-frame equation (N + mg = mv²/R with N ≥ 0). *Check (computed):* v_min = √(gR) = 3.1 m/s. Make the learner say why the naive answer fails: Volfson et al. report it as "the gravity force mg is compensated by the centrifugal force on the top of the circular trajectory". *Used:* Volfson et al. 2020 (spinning bowls in a circus act). <https://journals.aps.org/prper/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.16.010134>
14. **Surface of a spinning bucket.** In the rotating frame the surface is perpendicular to g plus centrifugal acceleration; show z = ω²r²/(2g). *Check (computed):* a bucket of radius 0.10 m at 2 rev/s (ω = 12.6 rad/s) has water 8 cm higher at the rim. *Used:* K&K ex. 8.6 "Surface of a rotating liquid"; Newton's bucket; Feynman's jar.
15. **Pendulum on a support that moves on a vertical circle.** Derive L = ½ m l² φ̇² + m a l γ² sin(φ − γt) + m g l cos φ, check that γ = 0 returns the pendulum, and put in a = 63 m, γ = 1 rad/s: the "pull" aγ² against g. *Check (computed):* aγ² = 63 m/s², which is 6.4 g, so gravity is a small once-per-turn wobble on top of a strong outward pull. *Used:* Landau and Lifshitz §5 Problem 3(a); Physics SE 100807.

**Oscillators and matching**

16. **Driven damped oscillator, power at and away from resonance.** m = 1 kg, k = 100 N/m, b = 2 kg/s, force amplitude 1 N. Average power absorbed by the damper, P = ½ b ω² A²: at ω₀ = 10 rad/s, and at 5 rad/s. *Check (computed):* 0.25 W (equal to F₀²/2b) and 4.4 mW, 57 times smaller. *Used:* Taylor 5.5 and 5.6; K&K 11.4; Feynman I-23-2; Morin 4.4.
17. **Best damping off resonance (impedance matching).** For the same oscillator driven at ω, show P(b) = ½F₀² b/(b² + X²) with X = (k − mω²)/ω, so the best damping is b = |k − mω²|/ω and then P = F₀²ω/(4|k − mω²|). *Check (computed):* at ω = 5 rad/s, b_opt = 15 kg/s and P_max = 16.7 mW. This is the "maximum power transfer theorem" with a purely reactive source (my own short derivation; standard). Caveat from the literature: as quoted by Stephen (2006), Williams and Yates (1996) and Mitcheson et al. claimed power grows without limit as damping goes to zero at resonance; Stephen shows that is wrong for steady state and that, with a coil, "maximum power is delivered to the electrical load when its resistance is equal to the sum of the coil internal resistance and the electrical analogue of the mechanical damping coefficient". <https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/37842/1/Step_06a.pdf> **[PART]**
18. **Resistor matched to a source.** 10 V source, internal resistance 5 Ω: load power and efficiency for R_L = 1, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 Ω. *Check (computed):* 2.78, 4.44, 5.00, 4.44, 3.20 W; efficiency 17, 33, 50, 67, 80 %. State the "contradiction" the learner will raise: 1 Ω source into 9 Ω gives 9 W at 90 %. *Used:* Electronics SE 716486, 461679, 449645; the topic sits near Feynman I-25-5 ("Series and parallel impedances", title only; I did not read whether it treats matching).
19. **Open circuit and short circuit of a generator.** Take voltage proportional to speed and torque proportional to current. Why is the shaft power zero at both extremes and positive between? Where is the speed for most power? *Used:* the accepted answer to Physics SE 411954.
20. **Gearbox ratio.** The notes' rotor turns at 9.5 rpm; a four-pole generator on a 50 Hz grid runs at 1,500 rpm. What ratio? *Check (computed):* about 157:1. Compare with the quoted typical drivetrains: rotor 12 to 20 rpm, generator 1,500 rpm, "typically around 80:1" (Gear Solutions); "three-stage gearbox, ratio of 80 or 100:1" (NREL). <https://gearsolutions.com/features/troubleshooting-wind-gearbox-problems/> **[PART]**; <https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy15osti/62887.pdf> **[PART]**

*Not included, because I could not source a typical form:* a chaos warm-up (Taylor's chapter 12 treats the driven damped pendulum, 12.2 to 12.8, which is the place to look), a gust or turbulence warm-up (Burton ch. 2.6 to 2.7 is the place to look), and a sizing warm-up for a sensor power budget.

## F. Ten rules for rewriting the notes

Each rule is distilled from A to E and names the sources it rests on (section numbers point back into this file, where the links and the reading marks are). The weight of evidence differs: rules 2, 4, 6 and 10 rest on experiments (mostly with students, not with self-study text); rules 1, 3, 5, 7, 8 and 9 rest on case studies, teachers' practice and the questions learners post. I say which.

**1. Open every topic with something the reader has seen, say what is seen, and only then give the name.**
*Rests on:* Feynman's wagon and ball and his test "rephrase it without the new word" (A10, <http://feynman.com/science/what-is-science/>); Redish's corollary "It's hard to learn something we don't almost already know" (A5, <https://physics.umd.edu/perg/papers/redish/cogsci.html>); MacKay's hoop and cyclist for wind power and Bean's pinwheels (B7, B8); Fyfe et al.'s first, concrete stage (A4).
*In the notes:* wind power with a hoop held in the wind and the cyclist's 6 m/s (MacKay); lift with a tilted hand in a moving air stream; the rotating frame with the ball in the pulled wagon, the water jar and the pendulum in the accelerating car; the generator with two dc motors wired back to back (Feynman II-16-1, B10). Evidence type: teaching experience, plus the concrete-first experiments.

**2. Fade from the picture to the symbol in visible steps and keep the link.**
*Rests on:* Fyfe, McNeil, Son and Goldstone (2014) and Goldstone and Son (2005) (A4); Van Heuvelen's qualitative-first multiple representations (A7); Rosengrant et al. on free-body diagrams (A7).
*In the notes:* for each model: everyday sentence, a labelled sketch of the rod on the blade with arrows, a diagram in the right frame, a graph (potential or power), then the equation, with each term in the equation tied to the arrow it stands for. Caveat: Kaminski et al. (2008) found abstract-only examples can beat concrete-then-abstract when the fading is not explicit, so do the fading in the open and name each stage.

**3. Name the parts of the machine, and the one job of each, before explaining the chain; put labels inside the figures.**
*Rests on:* the pretraining and spatial-contiguity effects in Mayer and Moreno's table (A11, effect sizes 1.00 and 0.48, narrated animations); DTU's five-line "how does a wind turbine work" chain (B2); Delft's and Manwell's component lists (B3, B4); Feynman's "backbone" for the reader who cannot take it all (A10).
*In the notes:* one labelled page: wind, blade, hub, low-speed shaft, gearbox, generator, converter, grid, and the extra hinged rod, each with a single sentence and its typical speed or force (rotor 10 to 30 rpm, generator about 1,500 rpm, ratio about 80:1 to 100:1; B6, B10). Then the chain is a story about parts the reader already knows. Evidence type: experiments, but on short multimedia lessons.

**4. Ask for a prediction before every surprising result, and make the prediction one the common wrong idea would get wrong.**
*Rests on:* predict-observe-explain (A6; White and Gunstone; NSTA); Redish's corollary 3.1 (a replacement idea needs "a strong conflict with predictions based on the existing model", A5); Küchemann et al. used exactly this with a rotating disc: after predicting, only one in five students correctly reported what they saw, though their answers improved from the prediction to the observation (C4); Volfson et al. (2 % correct for water in a spun bowl, C4).
*In the notes:* boxed "Predict" prompts with fold-out answers at: the pendulum bob in an accelerating car (which way, longer or shorter period?); a free object crossing a rotating disc; the rod hung at the blade tip (does it hang down, stick out, or flop?); a faster rotor with a fixed load (more power or less, Physics SE 411954?); the best load on a generator (open circuit, short circuit or in between?). Evidence type: strong for conceptual change in classes; untested for fold-out answers in print.

**5. State the wrong picture fairly, replace it with a better one, and name the two different "centrifugal forces".**
*Rests on:* FCI taxonomy, "Such is the power of a name!" (A5, C4); Küchemann et al.: 42.8 % think the centrifugal force is only needed when the object is coupled to the rotating system, 23.8 % treat inertial forces like real ones (C4); Volfson et al. (about 40 % centrifugal-force explanations, 56 % predicting radial release); Physics SE: "I can feel it" is answered by proper acceleration and the reaction to the centripetal force (D5: 108925, 650245, 855588); Taibu and Mataka's proposal to acknowledge the controversy (C4).
*In the notes:* say once, early: in the rotating frame the outward term is a real, needed term in that frame's equation (it makes the rod stiff like a spring) but nothing pushes in the ground frame; what you feel is the contact force; the "reactive centrifugal force" of the carousel is a different object. Give the ground-frame view and the rotating-frame view of the same case, and the one place a learner is likely to slip (the Coriolis term alone gives a circle; the centrifugal term makes the spiral, C4).

**6. Treat the reader as an expert in some parts and a novice in others: skip what they know, work out fully what they do not, and fade the help as they go.**
*Rests on:* expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2003, A3); the worked-example effect (Sweller and Cooper 1985, A1); backward fading (Renkl et al. 2002, A2); Chi et al. on self-explanation (A8).
*In the notes:* one-line "you know this" tags on Lagrangian and ODE steps so the expert can skip; complete worked examples for Betz, the velocity triangle, the tip-speed ratio, the generator and the gearbox; then the next example in each family leaves out the last step, then the last two (backward fading), with the answer folded away. Margin prompts "why this step?" for self-explanation.

**7. Say what is derived, what is assumed, and what is only an approximation, in the text, not in a footnote.**
*Rests on:* Feynman's preface (deduced against put in, A10); the Betz assumption list (infinite blades, no drag, no wake rotation, MTU note, B11) and the real-machine gap (C_p of about 0.45 to 0.5 against 0.593: Hansen, B1; Järvinen et al., E2); the disputed steps of the actuator disc (Zhang 2022, Khan 2020, B11); the contradictory claims on optimal damping (Williams and Yates 1996, Mitcheson et al. against Stephen 2006, E17); the lift story (Anderson and Eberhardt, B9).
*In the notes:* a "What we assumed" box at the end of the Betz section, the lift section (use deflection and pressure, not equal transit time; test with the Cessna numbers, E10) and the damper section (fixed force amplitude, steady state, small damping, which regime, because the answer for the best damping changes with these).

**8. Teach three touchstone systems and reuse them: the mass on a spring, the source and its load, and the sail's apparent wind.**
*Rests on:* Redish's touchstone problems ("the mass on a spring ... a touchstone problem for all kinds of harmonic oscillation", A9); the repeated learner questions on matching (D7: 716486, 449645, 461679, 733470, 7744; and D2: 411954); sailing as the bridge to lift and tip speed (Hansen, Bean, Physics SE 186515 and 931, B1, B8, D2).
*In the notes:* show that "the best load" is one calculation in three costumes: a resistor on a source (E18: 5 W at 50 % at R_L = R_s), a damper on a driven oscillator (E17: b_opt = |k − mω²|/ω), and a generator on a rotor (E19: voltage ∝ speed, torque ∝ current). Keep the contrast "most power" against "highest efficiency" in view (D7: the 50 % worry).

**9. Do the bookkeeping out loud: which energy, which voltage, whose work, and a number to check against.**
*Rests on:* Physics SE 37725 (E and H differ on a rotating hoop because the motor pays; D5); 444005 (which voltage drop is in P = IV; D7); 449645 and 716486 (efficiency against power; D7); Redish's corollary 1.2 (learners must cross-check what they call up, A5); Chi et al. (good self-explainers monitor their understanding, A8).
*In the notes:* in the rotating-frame section, state that H is conserved and E is not, and that the rotor supplies the difference; at the generator, name the load voltage separately from the source voltage drop; in every section give one check number from the notes' own case, as in E: tip speed 63 m/s, λ ≈ 7 at 9 m/s, centrifugal acceleration aγ² = 63 m/s² = 6.4 g, gear ratio about 157:1, wind power through the swept area ≈ 7.6 MW at 10 m/s with a ceiling of about 4.5 MW.

**10. Write short sections with signposts, and end each with things to produce, not things to recognise.**
*Rests on:* signalling and segmenting (Mayer and Moreno: headings, outline, a map of the lesson; learner-controlled short segments; A11); coherence (cut the interesting but extraneous; A11); practice testing rated high utility, rereading and highlighting low (Dunlosky et al.; A8); the learning gains of interactive-engagement courses (Hake, 0.48 against 0.23; A5); Feynman's "say it without the word" test (A10); the warm-ups in E, placed before each derivation.
*In the notes:* each section starts with "where we are, what we need next", has one idea, and ends with two or three short tasks that need a number, a sign or a sketch (the E list is the stock), answers folded. Move side stories (Lanchester before Betz, the ice-yacht idea, the Williams-Yates controversy) into marked boxes outside the main line. Evidence type: experiments on narrated animations and on classes; applying them to a long printed text is an extension.

### A suggested order that the sources support (inference, not a finding)

1. The chain and the numbers: a labelled machine page (B2, B3), the hoop and the cyclist (B7), power ½ρAv³, E1 to E3.
2. The disc and Betz: Delft's control volume and the "stopped air blocks the next air" reason (B3, B6, B7), E4.
3. Lift, apparent wind and the tip-speed ratio through the sailing analogue and the velocity triangle (B8, B9), E7 to E10.
4. The rotating frame: wagon, jar, car pendulum (C1, C2), then the bead on the hoop, the effective potential, E ≠ H (C3, D5), E11 to E15.
5. The rod: Landau §5 Problem 3(a) as the model equation (C1), the stiff spring in the rotating frame.
6. The driven damped oscillator and matching, with the resistor case first (D7, E16 to E19).
7. Generator and gearbox (B10, E19, E20) before the power-balance and sizing topics.
Only Hansen puts the aerofoil before the disc; Burton, Manwell, Delft and MIT put the disc first. This order follows the disc-first majority; if it does, say that lift and drag are needed "for blade theory" (Burton's own wording is that a knowledge of the lift and drag of aerofoils "is essential" there) and place the lift picture right before the velocity triangle (B5).
