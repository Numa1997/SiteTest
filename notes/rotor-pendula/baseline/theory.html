Chapter 01

The question before the equations

Can a pendulum riding on a turbine blade power a small sensor there, what does it cost the rotor, and what can this model say?

Picture a wind-turbine blade turning steadily. Fix a hinge to the blade and hang a small weight from it on a short rod. As the blade goes round, two things pull on the weight. The turning blade flings it outward, the way a merry-go-round throws you toward the rail. Gravity pulls it down. Because the blade keeps turning, “down” sweeps once around the blade every revolution: sometimes it points toward the hub, sometimes toward the tip, sometimes sideways. So the weight rocks to and fro on its hinge, once per turn. Put a small generator in the hinge, and the rocking makes electricity.

Where would that be useful? On the blade itself. A sensor that watches a blade’s vibration needs power where it sits. Wires through the turning hub are awkward, and so is climbing up to change a battery. A pendulum that takes a little of the blade’s motion could feed the sensor on the spot.

The study asks three plain questions. Is the power enough for a small sensor node? What does it cost the rotor? And what can this particular model say, and what can it not?

One wrong idea is worth naming at the start. The pendulum does not find free energy in gravity, or in chaos. Every joule its generator delivers is paid for by the rotor, and so by the wind. Chapter 4 shows this from the equations. The honest question is whether a tiny local power supply is worth its extra moving parts. It is not whether the pendulum raises the turbine’s output; it cannot.

The little background we actually need

Tip-speed ratio. A rotor of radius $R_{\rm rot}$ (m) turning at angular speed $\Omega$ (rad/s) moves its blade tips at $\Omega R_{\rm rot}$. Divide by the wind speed $v$ (m/s) and you get the tip-speed ratio $\lambda_T=\Omega R_{\rm rot}/v$, a pure number. Holding it fixed ties a rotor’s size to its speed: a big rotor turns slowly, a small one fast. Assumed The study takes $\lambda_T=7$ and $v=8$ m/s.

Power coefficient. Wind of density $\rho$ (kg/m³) moving at speed $v$ through a disc of area $A$ (m²) carries kinetic energy at the rate $\tfrac12\rho Av^3$, in watts. The power coefficient $C_p$ is the fraction of that a rotor actually captures. Assumed The study takes $C_p=0.45$ and $\rho=1.225$ kg/m³. No blade model stands behind these numbers. They only set the size of the turbine that the harvest is compared with.

Sensor node. A small electronics package that measures and reports. Here it is an accelerometer that samples all the time (the ADXL362) and a chip with a processor and a radio (the CC2652R) that wakes once a second to send a short message. Its average power is set mostly by how rarely the radio is on. Chapter 10 builds that budget from the two datasheets.

A generator as a brake. A small generator driving a resistive load pushes back with a torque roughly proportional to its speed, like a paddle stirred in thick oil. Such a brake is called viscous. Assumed Here each generator torque is exactly $c$ times the joint’s relative angular speed, with $c$ in N m s/rad. The energy the brake removes is the harvest, before any electrical loss.

The boundary of this calculation

The pendulum is point masses on rigid, massless rods. The rotor is planar and turns at a prescribed, constant $\Omega$: the pendulum can tug on it but cannot slow it down. The generator torques are viscous. There is no aerodynamic force on the pendulum, no friction, no electrical threshold or loss inside the generator, and no flexibility in blade or rods. The single pendulum used for comparison carries the same total mass at the same total reach as the double.

These choices make a clean mechanical comparison. They do not describe a qualified blade installation. The powers are ideal mechanical figures, conditional on these assumptions; they are not predictions for hardware.

The chain of the argument is a pendulum on a moving pivot → its Lagrangian → equations of motion → the energy the generator removes → linear resonance and its optimum → real turbine sizes → double against single, chaos → the sensor’s budget and limits. Chapter 02

Geometry and the Lagrangian

Where are the masses, how fast do they move, and what do the generators take?

Tie a stone to a string and fix the other end of the string to the arm of a merry-go-round. Now tip the merry-go-round up on its edge, the way a turbine stands, so the arm turns in a vertical plane. The end of the string, the pivot, goes round a circle at a steady rate. The stone follows, but not rigidly: it can swing about the pivot. Seen from the arm, the stone is flung outward, so it likes to stretch straight out along the arm. Gravity pulls it down. As the arm goes round, “down” points first one way along the arm, then across it, then the other way. That side-pull comes back once per revolution, and it keeps the stone swinging.

The study’s pendulum is that stone, with one addition: it may have an elbow. The upper rod hangs from the pivot, and the lower rod hangs from the end of the upper rod. To write its motion we need only two things: where the masses are, and how fast they move.

Put the hub at the origin, with $x$ horizontal and $y$ pointing up. The pivot sits on the arm at radius $r_p$ (m), and the arm’s angle is $s=\Omega t$ (rad), with time $t$ in s. Write $\mathbf e(a)=(\cos a,\sin a)$ for the unit vector at angle $a$. The upper rod has length $l_1$ (m) and carries mass $m_1$ (kg) at its end, the elbow. The lower rod has length $l_2$ and carries $m_2$. Each rod angle $\theta_i$ is measured anticlockwise from $+x$ in the fixed frame. The pivot, the elbow mass and the end mass are at

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf x_p&=r_p\,\mathbf e(s),\\ \mathbf x_1&=\mathbf x_p+l_1\,\mathbf e(\theta_1),\\ \mathbf x_2&=\mathbf x_1+l_2\,\mathbf e(\theta_2).\end{aligned}\tag{1}$$

Each position is the previous one plus a rod. Nothing else about the geometry is needed.

Write $M=m_1+m_2$ for the total mass and $L=l_1+l_2$ for the total reach. The single pendulum used for comparison puts all of $M$ at the end of one rod of length $L$. It is the case $l_1=L$, $l_2=0$, with one angle $\theta$.

Kinetic energy

Differentiate the positions and add up $\tfrac12m|\dot{\mathbf x}|^2$ for each mass. With the shorthand $A_d=Ml_1^2$, $B_d=m_2l_2^2$, $D_d=m_2l_1l_2$ (all in kg m²) and $\delta=\theta_1-\theta_2$, the kinetic energy $T$ (J) is

$$\begin{aligned}T={}&\tfrac12M\Omega^2r_p^2+\tfrac12A_d\dot\theta_1^2+\tfrac12B_d\dot\theta_2^2\\ &+D_d\cos\delta\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\\ &+\Omega r_pMl_1\dot\theta_1\cos(\theta_1-s)\\ &+\Omega r_pm_2l_2\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_2-s).\end{aligned}\tag{2}$$

Each mass moves with the pivot and also swings relative to it. Squaring the sum of these velocities produces the cross terms in the last two lines.

Read it term by term.

  • $\tfrac12M\Omega^2r_p^2$: both masses are carried round the pivot circle at speed $\Omega r_p$. This term is constant, so it does not affect the swing.
  • $\tfrac12A_d\dot\theta_1^2+\tfrac12B_d\dot\theta_2^2$: the swinging, as if the pivot stood still. The upper rod carries both masses at distance $l_1$, so its moment of inertia is $Ml_1^2$.
  • $D_d\cos\delta\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$: the end mass moves with both rods. When the rods swing the same way, their velocities add; when they swing opposite ways, they partly cancel.
  • The two $\Omega r_p$ terms: the pivot’s velocity adds to the swing velocity. Derived These cross terms are how the rotor drives the pendulum. Drop them and the rotor could not push the pendulum at all.
Derivation detail: the kinetic energy, step by step

The derivative of $\mathbf e(a)$ with respect to its angle is $\mathbf e'(a)=(-\sin a,\cos a)$, a unit vector at right angles to $\mathbf e(a)$. Two such vectors have the dot product $\mathbf e'(a)\cdot\mathbf e'(b)=\cos(a-b)$. The chain rule on (1) gives the velocities:

$$\begin{aligned}\dot{\mathbf x}_p&=\Omega r_p\,\mathbf e'(s),\\ \dot{\mathbf x}_1&=\dot{\mathbf x}_p+l_1\dot\theta_1\,\mathbf e'(\theta_1),\\ \dot{\mathbf x}_2&=\dot{\mathbf x}_1+l_2\dot\theta_2\,\mathbf e'(\theta_2).\end{aligned}\tag{3}$$

Square them, using the dot-product rule for every cross term:

$$\begin{aligned}|\dot{\mathbf x}_1|^2={}&\Omega^2r_p^2+l_1^2\dot\theta_1^2\\ &+2\Omega r_pl_1\dot\theta_1\cos(\theta_1-s),\\[4pt] |\dot{\mathbf x}_2|^2={}&|\dot{\mathbf x}_1|^2+l_2^2\dot\theta_2^2\\ &+2\Omega r_pl_2\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_2-s)\\ &+2l_1l_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2).\end{aligned}\tag{4}$$

Now form $T=\tfrac12m_1|\dot{\mathbf x}_1|^2+\tfrac12m_2|\dot{\mathbf x}_2|^2$. The part $|\dot{\mathbf x}_1|^2$ appears in both speeds, so it is multiplied by $m_1+m_2=M$. That gives the terms $\tfrac12M\Omega^2r_p^2$, $\tfrac12Ml_1^2\dot\theta_1^2$ and $\Omega r_pMl_1\dot\theta_1\cos(\theta_1-s)$. The rest of $|\dot{\mathbf x}_2|^2$ is multiplied by $m_2$ alone and gives the remaining three terms of (2). A computer-algebra check of (2) against (1) written for this page leaves no residual.

Potential energy

With $g=9.80665$ m/s², each mass contributes its weight times its height $y$:

$$V=g\big[Mr_p\sin s+Ml_1\sin\theta_1+m_2l_2\sin\theta_2\big].\tag{5}$$

The first term rises and falls with the pivot itself. It does not depend on the rod angles, so it puts no torque on the rods.

The generators: Rayleigh’s dissipation function

A brake turns motion into heat or electricity, so it cannot be written as a potential energy. Lord Rayleigh’s way around this is a second function, half the brake constant times the relative speed squared. Its derivative gives the brake torque.

The pivot generator has its housing on the blade, which turns at $\Omega$, and its shaft on the upper rod. It feels the relative speed $\dot\theta_1-\Omega$. The elbow generator sits between the two rods and feels $\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1$. With brake constants $c_0,c_1\ge0$ (N m s/rad),

$$\mathcal R=\tfrac12c_0(\dot\theta_1-\Omega)^2+\tfrac12c_1(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1)^2.\tag{6}$$

The brake torque on rod $i$ is $-\partial\mathcal R/\partial\dot\theta_i$. Twice $\mathcal R$ is the power the generators take out, the harvest of chapter 4.

Derived An ideal generator turns exactly this dissipated mechanical power into electrical power. A real one loses some of it; chapter 10 accounts for that with an assumed efficiency. Assumed The main runs use both generators with $c_0=c_1=c$. Setting $c_1=0$ gives a pivot-only double; setting $c_0=0$ gives an elbow-only double. The single pendulum has one generator, $\mathcal R=\tfrac12c(\dot\theta-\Omega)^2$.

Chapter 03

Equations of motion and dimensionless numbers

From the energies to one equation in which three pure numbers decide the motion.

Lagrange’s recipe turns energies into equations of motion. It is the same physics as Newton’s law, but it lets us skip the forces inside the rods, which we do not need. With the brake included through $\mathcal R$, there is one equation for each rod angle:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot\theta_i}-\frac{\partial T}{\partial\theta_i}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta_i}+\frac{\partial\mathcal R}{\partial\dot\theta_i}=0.\tag{7}$$

This is Newton’s law written for the independent angles. The first two terms together account for inertia; the last two supply the potential and dissipative contributions. The quantity $\partial T/\partial\dot\theta_i$ is the momentum conjugate to an angle, not automatically angular momentum about a fixed point. For the single pendulum it is the mass’s angular momentum about the moving pivot, not about the fixed hub.

The single pendulum

For the single pendulum, (2), (5) and (6) shrink to $T=\tfrac12M[\Omega^2r_p^2+L^2\dot\theta^2+2\Omega r_pL\dot\theta\cos(\theta-s)]$, $V=Mg(r_p\sin s+L\sin\theta)$ and $\mathcal R=\tfrac12c(\dot\theta-\Omega)^2$. Put them into (7):

$$\begin{aligned}ML^2\ddot\theta={}&-Mr_p\Omega^2L\sin(\theta-s)\\ &-MgL\cos\theta-c(\dot\theta-\Omega).\end{aligned}\tag{8}$$

A torque balance about the pivot: moment of inertia times angular acceleration equals three torques.

The first torque comes from the pivot’s own acceleration toward the hub. Seen from the arm, it is the centrifugal pull, and it turns the rod toward $\theta=s$, straight out along the arm. The second is gravity. The third is the generator brake, which feels only the rod’s speed relative to the blade.

Check the signs in a simple position. Take the blade horizontal to the right ($s=0$) and tilt the rod slightly above it, by a small angle $\theta\gt0$. The first term of (8), $-Mr_p\Omega^2L\sin\theta$, is negative: it turns the rod back toward the outward radial line. Gravity’s term, $-MgL\cos\theta$, is also negative there, since gravity pulls the mass down. The brake term, $-c(\dot\theta-\Omega)$, opposes the rod’s motion relative to the blade, whichever way it goes.

Now ride along with the arm. Measure the rod from the outward direction of the arm: $\varphi=\theta-\Omega t$. Then $\varphi=0$ means the rod points straight out along the blade. Because $\Omega$ is constant, $\dot\theta-\Omega=\dot\varphi$ and $\ddot\theta=\ddot\varphi$: the brake sees exactly the swing $\dot\varphi$. Measure time in radians of rotor turn, $\tau=\Omega t$, so one revolution is $\tau=2\pi$, and write a prime for $d/d\tau$. Divide (8) by $ML^2\Omega^2$:

$$\varphi''+\kappa\varphi'+R\sin\varphi+G\cos(\varphi+\tau)=0,\tag{9}$$
$$R=\frac{r_p}{L},\qquad G=\frac{g}{\Omega^2L},\qquad \kappa=\frac{c}{ML^2\Omega}.\tag{10}$$

Three pure numbers decide the motion. Two machines with the same $R$, $G$ and $\kappa$ swing identically, turn for turn, whatever their size.

Each number has a plain meaning.

  • $R$, the centrifugal stiffness. The term $R\sin\varphi$ is a restoring torque: tilt the rod off the arm and the outward pull swings it back, like a spring. Small swings about $\varphi=0$ have angular frequency $\Omega\sqrt R$ (chapter 5). Computed Across the 18 turbine mountings of chapter 6, $R$ runs from 2.005 to 300.8.
  • $G$, the gravity drive. $\Omega^2L$ is the centripetal acceleration of a point a distance $L$ from a centre turning at $\Omega$; $G$ compares $g$ with it. The term $G\cos(\varphi+\tau)$ is gravity seen from the arm. It comes round once per turn, a push that repeats at the rotor’s own rate. Across the mountings, $G$ runs from 0.02795 on the fastest rotor (41.888 rad/s) to 69.86 on the slowest (0.83776 rad/s).
  • $\kappa$, the brake. The generator constant measured against the swing’s inertia $ML^2$ and the rotor rate. The units agree: N m s/rad is kg m²/s, and so is $ML^2\Omega$. At the single pendulum’s best damping in the mappings, $\kappa$ runs from 1.005 to 299.8.

Three more ratios complete the set: $\Gamma$, a combination of $R$ and $G$ that the sensor chapter uses; $\mu$ and $\lambda_\ell$, how the double pendulum splits its mass and its reach; and one damping number for each joint:

$$\begin{aligned}\Gamma&=\frac{\Omega^2r_p}{g}=\frac RG,&\kappa_i&=\frac{c_i}{ML^2\Omega},\\ \mu&=\frac{m_2}{m_1},&\lambda_\ell&=\frac{l_2}{l_1}.\end{aligned}\tag{11}$$

$\Gamma$ is the centrifugal acceleration at the pivot in units of $g$; $\mu$ and $\lambda_\ell$ say how the double splits its mass and its reach; $\kappa_0,\kappa_1$ are the two brakes.

Computed $\Gamma$ spans 1.435 to 215.3 across the mountings; it matters for the sensor in chapter 10. All turbine mappings use equal masses and rods, $\mu=\lambda_\ell=1$; chapter 8 varies them. The rotor radius is written $R_{\rm rot}$ and the turbine’s tip-speed ratio $\lambda_T$, to keep them apart from $R$ and $\lambda_\ell$.

The double pendulum

Derived Apply (7) to each rod, using (2), (5) and (6). After collecting terms, with $\delta=\theta_1-\theta_2$:

$$\begin{aligned}&A_d\ddot\theta_1+D_d\cos\delta\,\ddot\theta_2\\ &\quad=-D_d\sin\delta\,\dot\theta_2^2-Ml_1r_p\Omega^2\sin(\theta_1-s)\\ &\qquad-Mgl_1\cos\theta_1-c_0(\dot\theta_1-\Omega)\\ &\qquad+c_1(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1),\end{aligned}\tag{12}$$
$$\begin{aligned}&D_d\cos\delta\,\ddot\theta_1+B_d\ddot\theta_2\\ &\quad=D_d\sin\delta\,\dot\theta_1^2-m_2l_2r_p\Omega^2\sin(\theta_2-s)\\ &\qquad-m_2gl_2\cos\theta_2-c_1(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1).\end{aligned}\tag{13}$$

Two torque balances like (8), one for each rod, now coupled through the elbow.

The new pieces have plain meanings. The $D_d\cos\delta$ terms couple the two accelerations: jerk the lower rod and the upper rod feels it. The $D_d\sin\delta\,\dot\theta^2$ terms are the pull of one mass swinging round its joint, felt by the other rod. The upper rod feels the centrifugal and gravity pull of the whole mass $M$ at distance $l_1$, because it carries both masses. The elbow brake $c_1$ appears in both lines with opposite signs: what it does to one rod, it undoes to the other.

These equations also follow from (2)–(7) in a computer-algebra check written for this page. More importantly, the audit derived its own equations from the positions, in different variables, and reproduced every turbine-mapping power to $2.8\times10^{-12}$ relative (chapter 11).

Derivation detail: the double pendulum in blade coordinates

Sol integrates the double in the same turning frame as (9). Let $q_i=\theta_i-s$ be each rod’s angle from the arm and $u_i=dq_i/ds$ its rate per radian of rotor turn. Write $a=l_1/L$, $b=l_2/L$, $\beta=m_2/M$, $A=a^2$, $B=\beta b^2$, $D=\beta ab$ and $d=q_1-q_2$. Dividing (12) and (13) by $ML^2\Omega^2$ gives

$$\begin{aligned}&Aq_1''+D\cos d\,q_2''\\ &\quad=-D\sin d\,(1+u_2)^2\\ &\qquad-a\big[R\sin q_1+G\cos(s+q_1)\big]\\ &\qquad-\kappa_0u_1+\kappa_1(u_2-u_1),\\[4pt] &Bq_2''+D\cos d\,q_1''\\ &\quad=D\sin d\,(1+u_1)^2\\ &\qquad-\beta b\big[R\sin q_2+G\cos(s+q_2)\big]\\ &\qquad-\kappa_1(u_2-u_1).\end{aligned}\tag{14}$$

The factors $(1+u_i)$ appear because each rod’s absolute rate is the rotor’s rate plus the swing. The determinant of the two acceleration coefficients is $AB-D^2\cos^2d=\beta a^2b^2(1-\beta\cos^2d)$. It is positive whenever both masses and both rods are positive, so the two accelerations can always be solved for. With $a=1$, $b=0$ the first line becomes (9). Only $R$, $G$, $\mu$, $\lambda_\ell$, $\kappa_0$ and $\kappa_1$ appear, which chapter 8 uses.

Chapter 04

Where the power comes from

Every joule the generator takes, the rotor pays for.

Back to the merry-go-round, now with a motor that holds its speed exactly. While the stone swings, it tugs on the arm, sometimes forward, sometimes back. When it drags behind, the motor must push a little harder to keep the speed. When it runs ahead, the motor pushes a little less. Any energy the stone carries away had to come through that motor.

The turbine is the same. Its speed is held at $\Omega$, so it supplies whatever torque the pendulum demands. The wind pays the rotor, the rotor pays the pendulum, and the generators take their share. Let us make that bookkeeping exact.

What the generators take

$$\begin{aligned}P_h&=c(\dot\theta-\Omega)^2&&\text{(single)},\\ P_h&=c_0(\dot\theta_1-\Omega)^2+c_1(\dot\theta_2-\dot\theta_1)^2=2\mathcal R&&\text{(double)}.\end{aligned}\tag{15}$$

The harvested power $P_h$ (W) is never negative. A brake only ever takes energy.

Derivation detail: the two ends of a generator

Take the pivot generator of the single pendulum. Its shaft turns with the rod at $\dot\theta$; its housing turns with the blade at $\Omega$. It pushes the rod with torque $-c(\dot\theta-\Omega)$ and, by action and reaction, pushes the housing with $+c(\dot\theta-\Omega)$. The power it delivers to the rod is that torque times $\dot\theta$; to the housing, the opposite torque times $\Omega$. Add them: $-c(\dot\theta-\Omega)\dot\theta+c(\dot\theta-\Omega)\Omega=-c(\dot\theta-\Omega)^2$. The negative sum means the generator removes mechanical energy from the rod–blade pair. It absorbs $c(\dot\theta-\Omega)^2$, and that is the harvest. It also tells us the blade feels the torque $+c(\dot\theta-\Omega)$ through the housing, which is the second term of (16) below.

What the rotor gives

The blade touches the pendulum in two places: the pivot pin and the pivot generator’s housing. The pin pushes with a force $\mathbf F$ while it moves at speed $\Omega r_p$ along $\mathbf e'(s)$. Because the rods are massless, Newton’s law for the two masses fixes this force: $\mathbf F=\sum_im_i(\ddot{\mathbf x}_i+g\,\hat{\mathbf y})$, where $\hat{\mathbf y}$ points up. Its component along the pivot’s motion, $F_\perp$ (N), and the power the rotor delivers, $P_{\rm rotor}$ (W), are

$$\begin{aligned}F_\perp={}&Ml_1\big(\ddot\theta_1\cos q_1-\dot\theta_1^2\sin q_1\big)\\ &+m_2l_2\big(\ddot\theta_2\cos q_2-\dot\theta_2^2\sin q_2\big)+Mg\cos s,\\ P_{\rm rotor}={}&\Omega r_pF_\perp-c_0\Omega(\dot\theta_1-\Omega),\qquad q_i=\theta_i-s.\end{aligned}\tag{16}$$

Force times velocity at the pin, plus torque times angular speed at the housing.

Let $E=T+V$ be the pendulum’s mechanical energy. Derived Substituting the equations of motion (12)–(13) into $dE/dt$ gives

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{dE}{dt}&=P_{\rm rotor}-P_h,\\ \overline P_{\rm rotor}&=\overline P_h+\frac{E(t_b)-E(t_a)}{t_b-t_a}.\end{aligned}\tag{17}$$

Bars are time averages over a window from $t_a$ to $t_b$. The rotor pays for the harvest plus any change in the energy the pendulum stores.

Sol checked the first identity at random states, with a largest normalised error of $3.18\times10^{-9}$. The audit derived the rotor’s drive torque separately, from a Lagrange equation for the rotor angle itself. A symbolic check written for this page gives zero residual for the single and $4\times10^{-15}$ at random states for the double, which is rounding.

Read (17) in three situations.

  • A steady orbit repeats once per turn. The pendulum’s energy returns to the same value each turn, so the storage term vanishes. The rotor’s mean input then equals the mean harvest exactly.
  • A window of chaotic motion can end with more or less stored energy than it began with. The two means then differ by that storage term, and the ledger still balances.
  • Gravity is already inside $V$. Its work is minus the change in gravitational potential energy. It gives no net work when the masses return to their starting heights. A chaotic bob need not do so after one rotor turn; its height change belongs in the storage term. For bounded heights, gravity’s mean contribution tends to zero over a long window.

Derived Once the mean storage term vanishes, each harvested watt requires one watt of mechanical input from the rotor in this ideal model. If the rotor’s aerodynamic power is held fixed, that watt is unavailable to its main generator. The pendulum transfers rotor energy to a local load; it supplies no extra energy source. Predicting whether an attachment changes the rotor’s aerodynamic capture would require a blade and flow model, which this study does not contain.

Computed The stored runs keep this ledger. For the best mounting of chapter 6, the mean harvest is 0.05708639 W and the rotor input matches it with an energy residual of $2.4\times10^{-13}$. Across all 111 nonlinear runs the largest residual is $1.98\times10^{-9}$ (chapter 11).

Chapter 05

Linear response and the optimum

Seen from the blade, the pendulum is a driven, damped oscillator. Where is its resonance, and what brake takes the most?

Ride on the blade. From there, the pivot stands still and two things act on the rod. The centrifugal pull holds it pointing outward, like a spring: tilt the rod and it swings back. Gravity, seen from the turning blade, goes round once per turn: a push that repeats at the rotor’s own rate. A mass on a spring, pushed periodically, with a brake attached. That is the driven, damped oscillator of every mechanics course. A child on a swing is the same system. Push in step with the swing’s own rhythm and it grows; push out of step and little happens.

The small-swing equation

When the swing is small, $\sin\varphi\approx\varphi$ and $\cos(\varphi+\tau)\approx\cos\tau-\varphi\sin\tau$. When $G$ is small, the swing itself is of order $G$, so the term $G\varphi\sin\tau$ is of order $G^2$. Keep only the first order in the gravity push, and (9) becomes

$$\varphi''+\kappa\varphi'+R\varphi=-G\cos\tau.\tag{18}$$

Stiffness $R$, brake $\kappa$, and a push of strength $G$ at frequency 1, that is, once per rotor turn.

Approximation detail: small angle and weak forcing are different assumptions

Expanding only in the angle leaves, to first order in $\varphi$,

$$\varphi''+\kappa\varphi'+[R-G\sin\tau]\,\varphi=-G\cos\tau.\tag{18a}$$

Thus gravity also changes the instantaneous stiffness. Equation (18) makes a further approximation: away from a large response, take the swing to be about as large as the push, $\varphi=O(G)$, and discard $G\varphi\sin\tau=O(G^2)$ as a small number times a small number. A small angle alone does not justify this step for arbitrary $G$. Near resonance the response can be large even for small $G$; for the turbine cases, the nonlinear power and peak-angle checks below decide whether this simpler formula is useful.

Why $R=1$ is resonance

Without brake or push, $\varphi''+R\varphi=0$: the rod swings at frequency $\sqrt R$ in $\tau$, which is $\Omega\sqrt R$ in real time. The push comes at frequency 1 in $\tau$, which is $\Omega$. The two match when $R=1$, that is, when $r_p=L$.

In words: the centrifugal spring holds the rod with a torque proportional to $\Omega^2r_p$, against an inertia proportional to $L$. So its own frequency is $\Omega\sqrt{r_p/L}$. Gravity’s push comes once per revolution, at the rotor’s own rate $\Omega$. When the pivot radius equals the pendulum’s reach, the two rates agree. Notice what follows. Both the spring and the push are tied to the rotor, so speeding the rotor up or down cannot tune the pendulum into or out of resonance. In this leading weak-forcing model, the frequency ratio is set by $r_p/L$. The rotor speed changes $G$, the strength of the push, and for a fixed physical generator it also changes $\kappa=c/(ML^2\Omega)$. At stronger forcing, nonlinear motion and the discarded time-varying stiffness can alter this simple resonance picture.

The response and its power

Derivation detail: solving the driven oscillator

Try a steady swing $\varphi=\operatorname{Re}(Ze^{i\tau})$ with a complex amplitude $Z$. Then $\varphi'=\operatorname{Re}(iZe^{i\tau})$ and $\varphi''=\operatorname{Re}(-Ze^{i\tau})$, and $-G\cos\tau=\operatorname{Re}(-Ge^{i\tau})$. Put these into (18):

$$\begin{aligned}(R-1+i\kappa)Z&=-G,\\ |Z|^2&=\frac{G^2}{(R-1)^2+\kappa^2}.\end{aligned}\tag{19}$$

The harvest is $P_h=c\dot\varphi^2=c\Omega^2\varphi'^2$. Measure power in units of $ML^2\Omega^3$ and call the result $\widehat P$; then $\widehat P=\kappa\varphi'^2$. The swing speed is $\varphi'=-|Z|\sin(\tau+\text{phase})$, and over a turn the mean of a squared sine is one half:

$$\langle\widehat P\rangle=\kappa\langle\varphi'^2\rangle=\tfrac12\kappa|Z|^2.\tag{20}$$

Together, (19) and (20) give (21). For the best brake, differentiate $\kappa/[(R-1)^2+\kappa^2]$ with respect to $\kappa$. The numerator of the derivative is $(R-1)^2+\kappa^2-2\kappa^2$, which vanishes when $\kappa^2=(R-1)^2$. Putting $\kappa=|R-1|$ back into (21) gives $G^2|R-1|/[2\cdot2(R-1)^2]=G^2/(4|R-1|)$.

$$\langle\widehat P\rangle=\frac{\kappa G^2}{2\big[(R-1)^2+\kappa^2\big]},\qquad \widehat P=\frac{P_h}{ML^2\Omega^3}.\tag{21}$$

Too little brake: the swing is large but the brake barely pulls. Too much: the brake nearly locks the joint and the swing dies. The best lies between.

$$\kappa_*=|R-1|,\qquad \widehat P_*=\frac{G^2}{4|R-1|}.\tag{22}$$

For $R\ne1$, the best brake makes the damping contribution $\kappa$ as large as the magnitude of the stiffness-minus-inertia contribution $R-1$ at the driving frequency. These contributions act in quadrature, a quarter cycle apart, so they do not cancel each other.

There are two useful limits away from resonance. If $\kappa\ll|R-1|$, the motion changes little when the brake is strengthened, so power initially grows with $\kappa$. If $\kappa\gg|R-1|$, the speed amplitude falls as $1/\kappa$, and power falls as $\kappa/\kappa^2=1/\kappa$. Differentiating joins these limits at (22). At exactly $R=1$, the linear formula instead grows without bound as $\kappa\to0$; it has no finite positive damping maximum there.

Multiply by $ML^2\Omega^3$ and put back $R=r_p/L$, $G=g/(\Omega^2L)$:

$$P_*=\frac{MLg^2}{4\Omega\,|r_p-L|}.\tag{23}$$

Units: kg · m · (m/s²)² divided by (1/s · m) is kg m²/s³, which is a watt. More mass and more reach give more power. At a fixed pivot, a faster rotor gives less: it stiffens the spring as $\Omega^2$, while gravity’s push stays $g$.

Derived Formula (23) blows up at $r_p=L$. That does not mean unlimited power. It means the small-swing model has failed: near resonance the swing grows until $\sin\varphi\approx\varphi$ is no longer true. The audit’s own model reproduces (21) numerically to $3\times10^{-13}$, and gives the optimum $\kappa=|R-1|$ and (23).

Assumed Sol accepts the linear optimum only when a nonlinear run at that damping has peak angle $|q|\lt0.3$ rad and its power agrees within 2 %. Otherwise a local nonlinear search finds the damping, between half and twice the linear value. Chapter 07

Generator placement and the double pendulum

Does an elbow with a second generator harvest more? Why does the single pendulum win?

A double pendulum is an arm with an elbow, and the classic toy for chaos. Its motion can look far livelier than a single pendulum’s, but each generator takes power only from the relative speed across its own joint, and two rods swinging together can move a great deal while the elbow between them hardly bends. The comparisons below hold total mass and total reach fixed. In the sampled cases the double harvests less than the single; this chapter shows how much less, and why.

The pivot generator sits at the shoulder: housing on the blade, shaft on the upper rod. It brakes the upper rod relative to the blade. The elbow generator sits between the rods and brakes the lower rod relative to the upper one.

The linear double

Derived Linearise (12)–(13) about $q_i=0$, as in chapter 5, and look for a steady swing $\mathbf q=\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf ze^{i\Omega t})$ with a complex amplitude vector $\mathbf z$:

$$(\mathbf K-\Omega^2\mathbf M+i\Omega\mathbf C)\,\mathbf z=\mathbf f,\qquad \overline P_{h,\rm lin}=\tfrac12\Omega^2\,\mathbf z^*\mathbf C\mathbf z,\tag{28}$$
$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf M&=\begin{pmatrix}A_d&D_d\\D_d&B_d\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf C=\begin{pmatrix}c_0+c_1&-c_1\\-c_1&c_1\end{pmatrix},\\ \mathbf K&=\Omega^2\begin{pmatrix}Ml_1r_p+D_d&-D_d\\-D_d&m_2l_2r_p+D_d\end{pmatrix},\\ \mathbf f&=-g\begin{pmatrix}Ml_1\\m_2l_2\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}\tag{29}$$

The same oscillator as (18), with two coordinates: an inertia matrix, a centrifugal stiffness coupled through the elbow, a brake matrix and a gravity push on each rod.

Computed Across all 18 turbine mountings the double with both generators delivers 0.6351–0.7354 of the single. The reason is where the mass sits. $$P_{*,\rm rigid}=\frac{g^2S^2}{4\Omega\,|r_pS-I|}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{g^2S}{4\Omega r_p}\qquad(r_p\gg L).\tag{30}$$

Derived Why proportional to $S$? The tilt that gravity produces against the spring does not depend on $S$, because the push and the spring both scale with it. The best brake does scale with $S$, and so does the power it takes at that tilt. For the equal-mass, equal-rod double welded straight, $S=m_1l_1+m_2L=0.015$ kg m, against $ML=0.020$ kg m for the single. So even welded, the double would give at most 0.75 of the single on a large rotor. The real elbow is not welded: it bends by about a third of the upper rod’s angle, and the double reaches 0.7354 at the largest $R$. Near resonance the single’s advantage grows. At $R=2.005$ the welded estimate gives 0.643, and the computed ratio is 0.6351.

This result applies to the stated comparator, damping law, geometries and sampled branches. It does not rule out every possible double-pendulum design. But the extra joint does not make up for putting half the mass at half the reach.

Chapter 09

Chaos and the matched comparison

How is chaos detected here, and does it pay?

Drop two leaves side by side into a calm stream and they travel together. Drop them into rapids and they soon end up far apart, though they started a finger’s width away. Regular motion is the calm stream; chaotic motion is the rapids. A complicated-looking trace is not enough to tell them apart: the test is what happens to a tiny change of the starting state under the same equations and the same forcing. While their separation is still small, two nearby chaotic trajectories drift apart roughly as $\|\delta\mathbf x(t)\|\propto e^{\Lambda t}$, like money at compound interest, and the largest Lyapunov exponent $\Lambda$, in 1/s, is that interest rate; chaos means it is positive. Regular motion can show a brief growth, or a phase difference that stays, but its long-run rate is not positive.

Measuring the separation rate

Derived Benettin’s method follows the trajectory and a companion started a tiny distance $\epsilon$ away in the state $(q_1,q_2,u_1,u_2)$. Every quarter of a rotor period, it measures how far the companion has drifted, records the stretch, and pulls the companion back to distance $\epsilon$ along the same direction. The pulled-back companion keeps finding the fastest-growing direction. Averaging the logarithms of the stretches gives

$$\widehat\Lambda=\frac1{\Delta s}\sum_j\log\frac{\|\delta\mathbf x_j\|}{\epsilon},\qquad \Lambda=\Omega\widehat\Lambda.\tag{33}$$

$\widehat\Lambda$ is the growth rate per radian of rotor turn, over a total turn $\Delta s$; $\Lambda$ is the same rate in 1/s. Positive means nearby starts separate.

Assumed The chaos criterion. After 8 alignment periods, a candidate that is not period-one is called chaotic only if $\widehat\Lambda\gt0.002$ and all four 50-period blocks of the estimate are positive. Period-one trajectories get a shorter 24-period stability check. “Regular” means only that the criterion was not met; it does not prove the motion is periodic. In fact 16 of the 52 “regular” cases are not period-one, and 36 of the 54 cases got only the short check.

The scan has 48 combinations of $\Omega\in\{2,4,6\}$ rad/s, $r_p\in\{0.05,0.20\}$ m and eight dampings from $3\times10^{-5}$ to 0.1 N m s/rad, plus six refinements: 54 cases. Correction to the report: honest error bars

The report quotes the 200-period chaotic power as 0.253 ± 0.003 W. That ± 0.003 W is a naive block standard error. The same run gives 0.2530 W with Sol’s native integration kernel and 0.2439 W with plain SciPy, and an independent model gives 0.2470 W. Which number comes out depends on the integrator backend alone. The spread, 0.009 W, is three times the quoted error. The honest statements are: about 0.25 ± 0.01 W over 200 periods, about 0.245 ± 0.005 W over 800 periods, and a chaotic-to-regular ratio of 0.65 ± 0.01. The conclusion that chaos gives less power is untouched.

Why would two correct integrators disagree? Because the motion is chaotic. With $\widehat\Lambda\approx0.03$, a difference grows by $e^{0.03\,\Delta s}$; over 200 periods, $\Delta s=400\pi\approx1257$, that is a factor of about $10^{17}$. A last-digit rounding difference, near $10^{-16}$, grows to full size within the window. Each integrator then follows a different, equally valid chaotic trajectory, and finite-window averages over different trajectories differ. Longer windows shrink that scatter, which is why the 800-period values agree more closely.

The exponent itself is uncertain in the same way. Two 400-period checks from the same recorded state, with $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ and $10^{-7}$, give $\widehat\Lambda=0.027$ and 0.039, with every 100-period block positive. The same $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ check started from the unrounded state, rather than its float32 copy, gives 0.0443. The sign is robust; the size is known to about ±30 %.

Chaos gives no gain here

A picture helps, though it is not a proof. A child pumping a swing gains most when every push comes at the same point of the swing. A regular orbit repeats exactly once per turn, so gravity’s push meets it at the same phase every time, and the brake can be tuned to that rhythm. A chaotic orbit meets the push at a different phase each turn; on some turns it hands energy back. Derived More precisely: damping changes both the trajectory and the work drawn from the rotor, and larger irregular swings do not by themselves mean more power at the best damping.

The evidence is the computed comparison, not the picture. Neither branch has been optimised over all starting states, dampings and generator laws. Within what was tested, there is no reason to prefer a chaotic double pendulum.

Sensor power and acceleration limits

What does a real sensor node need, and at which mountings can its accelerometer read the blade at all?

A sensor node spends most of its life asleep. Its average power is a weighted sum: the power of each activity times the fraction of time it runs. The radio is power-hungry, so the trick is to switch it on rarely and briefly.

A sourced reference budget

The accelerometer’s range on the blade

An accelerometer measures the push its mounting gives it. On a turning blade, that push holds the sensor on its circle, $\Omega^2r_p$ toward the hub, and also holds it up against gravity. Along the radial axis the two add when the blade points straight down. Derived The worst radial reading, with no vibration added, is

$$a_{\rm max}=\Omega^2r_p+g=g(\Gamma+1)\lt8g\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Gamma\lt7.\tag{36}$$

The ADXL362’s largest range setting is ±8 g. At $\Gamma=7$, the gravity-only peak already reaches the range limit. $\Gamma\lt7$ is necessary, not sufficient: it leaves a headroom of $g(7-\Gamma)$, between 2.0 g and 5.6 g at the six of the 18 mountings that pass, and that headroom must cover the vibration. This is a measurement-range condition, not a structural survival rating.

Computed Across the mountings $\Gamma$ spans 1.435 to 215.3. Six slow-rotor mountings pass (Γ from 1.435 to 5.023). All nine fast-rotor mountings fail, with Γ from 17.94 up. The other three slow mountings (Γ = 7.176, 7.535, 10.76) exceed the range before any vibration is added.

So enough power does not make the ADXL362 a usable radial sensor at most mountings. At those, it is only a power reference. A higher-range accelerometer would need its own sourced budget and its own check of bandwidth and noise.

Chapter 11

Numerical checks and reproducibility

How do we know the numbers are what the equations say?

A computer will happily integrate a wrong equation to twelve digits. So the checks come in an order: first the cheap ones that catch gross errors, then the ones that test the arithmetic, and last the independent rebuild.

1. Units and limits

Derived Formula (23) comes out in watts. An exactly absent brake harvests nothing. Away from $R=1$, the linear steady-response power also tends to zero as $\kappa\to0$, and it tends to zero as $\kappa\to\infty$. The first limit is not uniform: the closer $R$ is to 1, the smaller $\kappa$ must be before the power falls, and at $R=1$, $\kappa=0$ the steady response (19) has no bounded solution, so (21) diverges. At $R=1$ the linear formula diverges, and the text treats that as a breakdown, not a result. In the large-rotor limit, (27) predicts nearly flat slow-rotor powers, and the table of chapter 6 shows them. The single at $R=5$, $G=0.1$ gives $\widehat P/G^2=0.062499$ nonlinear against $1/16$ from (22).

2. Conservation

Power and rotor work are integrated as extra state variables, not estimated from sparse saved samples. That allows the energy balance (17) to be tested over each whole run:

$$\epsilon_E=\frac{\big|W_{\rm rotor}-H-\Delta E\big|}{|H|},\tag{37}$$

$W_{\rm rotor}$ is the work the rotor did, $H$ the energy harvested and $\Delta E$ the change in the pendulum’s stored energy, all in J over the averaging window.

Computed The largest $\epsilon_E$ among the 111 nonlinear runs is $1.98\times10^{-9}$. A separate check of the identity (17) at random states has a largest normalised error of $3.18\times10^{-9}$. These checks test the ideal equations. They say nothing about the losses the model leaves out.

3. Convergence

Assumed Sol’s runs use DOP853, an adaptive eighth-order Runge–Kutta method, with relative tolerance $10^{-10}$, absolute tolerance $10^{-12}$ and largest step 0.20 in $s$. Most runs discard 100 rotor periods and average over the next 200; the chaos scan uses 200 and 200, the robustness runs 400 and 400 or 800.

The first delivery used a fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4). Its retained outputs at 2048 and 4096 steps per period can be compared:

$$\delta_{\rm RK4}=\frac{\big|\overline P_{2048}-\overline P_{4096}\big|}{\big|\overline P_{4096}\big|}.\tag{38}$$

Computed For the two retained cases, $\delta_{\rm RK4}=4.61\times10^{-12}$ and $3.21\times10^{-12}$, against an explicitly chosen threshold of $1\times10^{-8}$. The audit ran its own DOP853 on the same two cases; it agrees with RK4 at 4096 steps per period to $1.7\times10^{-13}$ and $4.0\times10^{-13}$. The threshold was chosen after the fact; that is acceptable because it is stated. The tolerance dependence of the similarity test in chapter 8 is a convergence check of the same kind.

Computed Before changing anything, Sol reproduced the three reference anchors of the first delivery with unmodified code: a single at 0.151801057623 W, a double at 0.101284543458 W, and the matched regular orbit at $\widehat P=1.480157448$. The double anchor rounds to the printed 0.101285 W; its relative difference of $4.51\times10^{-6}$ from that six-digit number is a rounding question, not an equation change.

4. Independent models

Computed The audit rebuilt the physics from scratch. It derived its own equations by computer algebra from the inertial positions, in SI units, with relative angles and physical time, and its own rotor drive torque. With its own integrator, every one of the 36 turbine-mapping powers agrees with Sol to $2.8\times10^{-12}$ relative or better. Its own Benettin test, with an exact tangent equation, gives the same chaos label on all 54 cases. Its numerical linearisation reproduces the relative-motion table of chapter 7 to every printed digit, and its reruns of Sol’s own code with the same integrator backend reproduce Sol’s stored values.

Verification in a useful order

Check units and limiting cases first; then conservation; then convergence with tolerance and step size; then independent models. Each stage catches errors the earlier ones cannot. A tiny energy residual is compatible with a wrong model that conserves energy perfectly; only a rebuild from different variables can catch that. None of these stages can test the omitted physics: air drag, friction, generator curves, structure.

What the stored files hold

Calculations run in double precision (float64). The saved trajectories are single precision (float32), with 16 samples per rotor period, to keep the files small. Two consequences are worth knowing. Peak angles read from 16 samples per period can be up to 1.5 % low: the largest is 0.3643 rad from the samples and 0.3695 rad from a continuous run. No validity flag changes; the nearest case moves from 0.2663 to 0.2702 rad, still below the 0.3 rad limit.

Correction to the report

The caption of Sol’s Fig. 6 says the regular orbit’s fixed-phase section “collapses to a point”. The plotted section shows a horizontal smear 0.97 mrad wide. It is not motion. The saved angle keeps counting whole turns, so it reaches 7 600 to 10 100 rad, and at that size float32 can only resolve steps of about 0.98 mrad. The spread in the rate $u_1$ is exactly zero. The smear is a storage effect that the report does not mention.

Everything needed to rerun the study is in Sol’s delivery: code/README.md gives the exact commands and package versions, and summary.csv holds the 111 nonlinear runs and 49 linear evaluations with their dimensionless coordinates. Exponents that were not measured are stored as NaN with the regime “not measured”; none is labelled regular by assumption.

A defensible statement of a result

State the model, the scenario, the observable and the check, then the narrow conclusion. This is supported: “For point masses on rigid rods, a rotor held at constant speed, viscous generators and the stated turbine scenario, a 0.10 kg, 0.20 m single pendulum supplies 9.55–57.1 mW of ideal mechanical power at the sampled admissible mountings. These are not supported: “a pendulum will power a blade sensor”, which needs hardware tests; “chaos increases the harvest”, which the matched comparison contradicts; and “the device improves turbine output”, which chapter 4 rules out.

Verdict: enough power, conditional usefulness

Enough power. Computed The ideal mechanical result is favourable for a very small load. At 50 % conversion the single gives 4.78–28.5 mW, against a 37.58 µW reference load: a margin of at least 127. Even a 1 mW planning allowance is covered. The model supports power sufficiency for the stated duty cycle. It does not support a reliability claim for hardware.

Not free. Derived Every harvested joule is supplied by the rotor, after accounting for the energy the pendulum stores. At the best mounting the harvest is $7.2\times10^{-5}$ of the assumed turbine power. Real drag, friction and regulation would raise the cost. This device is not worth building to raise turbine efficiency or net turbine electricity.

Simple beats complicated. Computed The equal-mass, equal-reach double loses at all 18 mountings, because it puts mass closer to the pivot. At matched forcing, the regular orbit gives about 0.379 W and the chaotic one about 0.24–0.25 W. Added hinges, wiring and moving parts would have to earn their place through a benefit that has not been shown.

Usefulness is conditional. Assumed A prototype is worth considering only as a local sensor supply, where wiring, power transfer through the hub or battery service is costly enough. The first candidate should be the single pendulum. It should go on a slow rotor at a mounting that keeps the accelerometer within range, with margin for vibration, or the sensor must be replaced by a higher-range one with its own budget. The pendulum must stay inside the blade; one of the 18 studied mountings does not.

Before any blade installation, the generator must produce the assumed near-viscous braking torque at the real joint speeds, which are small: about 0.8 rad/s peak in the worked example of chapter 5. It must overcome its own electrical threshold and deliver useful power after leakage and storage losses. Cold start and still air need a storage design. The mount must survive centrifugal loads of up to 143.5 g at the pivot among the admissible mountings, vibration, fatigue and rotor imbalance, and a detached mass must not become a hazard. The point-mass, massless-rod model also leaves out the hardware mass, which may dominate in so small a generator.

Assumed The deciding experiment is a rotating rig equivalent to a blade. Measure joint torque and speed, the work drawn from the rotor, net regulated electrical energy, sensor saturation, and start-up and storage behaviour under the intended duty cycle. If wiring or a maintained battery is cheaper and more reliable, reject the mechanism. If local autonomy outweighs the mechanical burden and the measured margin survives those tests, a single-pendulum supply is plausible. The study justifies that narrow trial. It does not justify a turbine retrofit or a claim of power from chaos.
