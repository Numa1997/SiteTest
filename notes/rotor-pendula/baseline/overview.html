Can a swinging weight on a turbine blade power a sensor?

57.1 mWbest harvest, single pendulum, 100 g on 20 cm37.6 µWa blade sensor node needs (datasheet typical)0.64–0.74double ÷ single, same mass and reach

Hang a 100 g weight on a 20 cm rod from a point on a turning blade. Each time the blade goes round, gravity pulls the weight first one way and then the other, so it swings once per turn, like a child on a swing pushed once per pass. A generator at the pivot brakes that swing and turns it into electrical energy. This site asks three things: how much power that gives, what it costs the rotor, and whether it is enough for a small sensor on the blade.

The short answer: on the 18 mountings studied, the generator takes 9.6 mW to 57.1 mW from the swing; a sensor node needs about 0.04 mW. The power is not free: the rotor pays for every milliwatt, so the turbine's output drops by exactly what the pendulum harvests. 01What the study finds

four results · each tagged with how it is known

  1. Computed There is enough power. Every one of the 18 mountings gives at least 9.6 mW, more than a hundred times the 37.6 µW a low-duty sensor node draws, even after an assumed 50 % loss in the electronics.
  2. Derived The rotor pays for it. At fixed rotor speed, the energy the generator removes is replaced by the rotor's drive. The pendulum cannot raise the turbine's net output; it takes at most 0.0072 % of it.
  3. Computed One rod beats two. With the same mass and the same total reach, a double pendulum gives 0.64–0.74 times the single one's power, at every mounting.
  4. Computed Chaos does not help. Of 54 double-pendulum cases scanned, 2 are chaotic; in the matched comparison the chaotic motion harvests less than the regular one.

The catch is not power but force: on a fast rotor the pendulum sits in 18–215 g of centrifugal acceleration, far beyond a ±8 g accelerometer. See Theory chapter 10.

03Corrections made to the study

found by an independent audit · the numbers stand

An independent model, written from scratch, re-ran all 36 mapping cases and all 54 chaos cases; every headline number reproduces. Five things in the report needed correcting:

  • Eq. 17 prints the turbine size as 56 mm/s ÷ Ω; it is 56 m/s ÷ Ω (the numbers used the right value).
  • The text says the centrifugal factor Γ falls with rotor speed at a fixed blade fraction; it rises (Γ ∝ Ω).
  • The chaotic power's error bar, ±0.003 W, is too small: the same run gives 0.244 or 0.253 W depending only on the integrator.
  • At 400 rpm with the pivot at 90 % of the blade, the bob reaches 66 mm past the blade tip: that mounting cannot be built as drawn.
  • One panel of the Poincaré figure is blurred by single-precision storage, though its caption says it shrinks to a point.

Each is shown with its evidence on the checks page.
