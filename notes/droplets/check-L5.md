## Data-level checks of (6.1) and of the quoted numbers (model output used as input, not under test)

- Exact runs against (6.1): 100 cm3: empirical median 286 s, (6.1) 280 s; 1000 cm3: 174 s (text 175, upper median of 30) against 175 s; fractions of runs with a 100 um drop at 250, 300, 400 s: 0.34, 0.54, 0.97 against 0.356, 0.606, 0.903 (differences within 2.3 binomial standard errors). 1 m3: first 100 um drop at 60 s, half the water above 100 um at 584.4 s (text 585), 1 mm drop at 1075 s = 17.9 min (text "about 18 min"), all from the 16-bins-per-doubling tail that the text uses (8 bins: 55 s, 581 s).
- 10 cm3 (the text now says the argument fails there): the mean field gives V max n100 = 0.391, so at most 1 - e^-0.391 = 32.4 % of volumes (text 32 %), while the exact runs reach 0.48 at 600 s and 0.91 at 900 s (3 and 12 standard errors above). So (6.1) does fail for 10 cm3, as the text states, because the drops are not independent of the volume's own water (Poisson premise violated).
- Arithmetic: untruncated modes hold 1.1163 g/m3 of water; a 100 um drop holds the water of 3.75 cm3, a 1 mm drop of 3.75 litres; 10 cm3 hold one drop of 139 um; 1 cm3 (1.109e-9 kg after the 3-sigma cut) could at best make one drop of 64 um, so no drop reaches 100 um (text); (100^3 - 69^3)/8^3 = 1312 captures (text 1312); 40 x 1.2^3 = 69.12 um (text 69); the untruncated lognormal tail puts 0.251 drops of >100 um per m3 (text 0.25).

## Notes (none changes a verdict)

1. The Poisson premise is an assumption, and the text now says so ("rare and independent"; failure at 10 cm3 explained). The mean-field n100(t) is the mean of the count of drops above 100 um at time t; the count is Poisson only if drops arose independently of one another, which is why the dashed curves use the largest n100 so far (a volume that once held one has had its first drop). That is a modelling statement, consistent with the numbers above, not a derivation.
2. Telford (1955) and Kostinski and Shaw (2005) factor of ten: literature statements, not checked here (no primary source read).
3. The Gillespie and thinning statements are standard; they were checked by derivation and simulation, and the bookkeeping that makes thinning exact for same-class pairs (ordered proposals, i = j rejected) is the one in the code comment of `gillespieRun`.
