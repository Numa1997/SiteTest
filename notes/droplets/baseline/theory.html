Chapter 01

The question before the equations

What grows, what multiplies, and what can this particular model explain?

Look at a window after a light drizzle. Small beads of water sit still on the glass. Then one bead grows heavy enough to slide. As it runs down, it touches the beads in its path and swallows them. It gets bigger, so it runs faster, so it meets more beads. A cloud does the same thing in three dimensions, with air instead of glass.

A cloud is mostly air. Its visible part is a crowd of water droplets, a few hundred per cubic centimetre, each about ten micrometres (µm) in radius. Each falls, slowly. Computed With the app’s fall-speed law, a droplet of radius 10 µm settles at 1.24 cm/s at 10 °C and 850 hPa; a drop of radius 1 mm falls at 6.9 m/s and holds the water of a million droplets. Something has to gather them.

Gravity gathers them. A fast drop overtakes the slow droplets below it, touches some, and merges with some of those: gravitational collection. The thesis asks the same for ice. When ice particles collide, small pieces can break off, each a new particle. Collisions can then multiply particles as well as merge them, the “collisional ice multiplication” of the thesis title.

So three things must be kept apart: how big the particles are, how many there are, and how much condensed water they hold. A merger lowers the number and keeps the mass. A break-up raises the number and also keeps the mass. More ice particles does not mean more ice. Each quantity needs its own balance.

The thesis (Numa Koudsie, Collisional Ice Multiplication Processes in Clouds, Leipzig 2022) follows one large particle, the collector, through a pool of smaller ones and derives its mass gain from the volume it sweeps out. This app keeps that construction and asks how the whole size distribution changes when every particle may collect or be collected. The first question gives a gravitational collection equation (GCE); the second, the stochastic collection equation (SCE) in its mean-field form. The app also changes the thesis’s fall speed, collision efficiency and ice-fragment rule; each change is marked where it enters.

The little cloud background we actually need

Condensed water and vapour. The visible particles are condensed water, liquid or ice, suspended in air. Water vapour is a separate gas mixed into the air. The app evolves the condensed particles only. Nothing creates or feeds droplets, so a pool of small droplets can be used up.

Supercooled water. A droplet can stay liquid below 0 °C; it is then supercooled. Freezing needs a trigger, such as contact with ice. Assumed The app freezes a droplet completely and at once when it merges with ice, and ignores the heat released.

Surface tension. New water surface costs energy, as stretching a rubber sheet does. Surface tension $\sigma$ is that energy per added area, $dE_s=\sigma\,dA$, in J/m², the same as N/m. At fixed volume the sphere has the least area, so surface tension pulls a small drop round. It decides, in chapter 6, whether two drops that touch stay together.

Terminal speed. A falling particle speeds up until air drag balances its weight less its buoyancy, then falls steadily (chapter 4).

The boundary of this calculation

The app treats a homogeneous box of cloud at fixed temperature and pressure. Particles are spheres, and they meet only because they fall at different speeds. The model leaves out condensation and evaporation, turbulence, electric charge, the break-up of large raindrops, transport through space, any updraft, and the heat of freezing. Its curves are conditional collection calculations, not predictions of when a real cloud rains.

The chain of the argument is an encounter volume → a fall speed → a collision and a merger → an event rate → a population balance → conserved moments → a numerical grid → ice reaction channels → a defined diagnostic. Each chapter is one link. Where mechanics does not fix an input, the text says it is an adopted closure.

Chapter 02

Distributions and notation

What does a size distribution count, and why does every change of variable need its Jacobian?

Sort marbles into jars by size. A jar for 1.1 to 1.3 cm holds more than one for 1.0 to 1.1 cm simply because it is twice as wide; to compare jars fairly, divide each count by the jar’s width. The result is a density, marbles per unit of size. A size distribution is exactly that, per cubic metre of air.

Let $r$ be a particle’s radius (m), $d=2r$ its diameter, $m$ its mass (kg) and $\rho_p$ the density of its material (kg/m³): $\rho_w=1000$ kg/m³ for water and $\rho_i=900$ kg/m³ for ice in the app. In an encounter, $R$ is the larger radius. “Collector” names a role in one encounter, not a separate kind of particle. For a sphere,

$$m(r)=\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_pr^3,\qquad \frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi\rho_pr^2.\tag{1}$$

Mass grows as the cube of the radius. Equal masses of ice and water have different radii.

Computed A water droplet of radius 10 µm has mass $4.19\times10^{-12}$ kg; a drop of radius 1 mm, a million times more. The app’s grid runs from a 1 µm to a 2 mm water sphere, a span of $2000^3=8\times10^9$ in mass. Only a logarithmic axis can show that.

Write $n_r(r,t)\,dr$ for the number of particles per cubic metre of air with radius between $r$ and $r+dr$ at time $t$ (s). Its unit is m⁻⁴: three lengths for the volume of air, one for the width of the radius interval. In mass coordinates, $n_m(m,t)\,dm$ counts the same particles, in kg⁻¹ m⁻³. The densities differ because a radius interval $dr$ maps onto a mass interval of a different width:

$$n_m(m,t)\,dm=n_r(r,t)\,dr,\qquad n_m=\frac{n_r}{dm/dr}=\frac{n_r}{4\pi\rho_pr^2}.\tag{2}$$

Same particles, same count; the density changes by the stretch factor $dm/dr$, the Jacobian of the change of variable.

Two integrals matter most: the number concentration $\mathcal N$ (m⁻³) counts particles, and the condensed-water mass concentration $L$ (kg/m³) weighs them:

$$\mathcal N=\int_0^\infty n_m\,dm,\qquad L=\int_0^\infty m\,n_m\,dm.\tag{3}$$

The zeroth and first moments in mass. A merger lowers $\mathcal N$ and keeps $L$.

Sizes span decades, so the app plots against $\ln r$, read as $\ln(r/r_*)$ for a fixed reference $r_*$; the step $d\ln r=dr/r$ does not depend on $r_*$. Since $dm/d\ln r=3m$, one more Jacobian gives the densities per unit $\ln r$:

$$\frac{d\mathcal N}{d\ln r}=r\,n_r=3m\,n_m,\qquad G_r\equiv\frac{dL}{d\ln r}=m\frac{d\mathcal N}{d\ln r}=3m^2n_m.\tag{4}$$

On a logarithmic radius axis the area under $G_r$ is the water mass and the area under $d\mathcal N/d\ln r$ the number. Only on these curves does the eye read areas correctly.

$G_r$ has the unit kg/m³, because $\ln r$ is a pure number. The solver calls it g and the charts label it “g = dM/d ln r” in g/m³. It is a mass spectrum, not gravity; on this page gravity is $g_0$, and Assumed the code uses $g_0=9.81$ m/s².

Worked example: number and mass are different moments

The app’s reference cloud has two modes (chapter 9): 300 droplets per cm³ around radius 8 µm and 1 drop per cm³ around 40 µm. Computed On the grid the first carries 0.805 g/m³ of water, the second 0.311 g/m³. The collector mode is 1 particle in 301, yet it holds 28 % of the water. A chart of number hides the collectors; a chart of mass shows them as a second hump. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.

Every other symbol is defined where it first appears, with its unit. Inputs in µm, hPa and cm⁻³ are converted to SI before any calculation.

Chapter 03

The encounter kernel

How does a swept volume become a rate, and why are contact and merger kept apart?

Walk through falling snow holding an umbrella flat. The umbrella sweeps a column of air and catches the flakes in it. Walk faster and you sweep more air per second; carry a bigger umbrella and the column is wider. A falling drop is that umbrella. Its walk is its speed relative to the smaller droplets, and what it sweeps is a tube of air.

Start with straight paths, seen from the collector. Two spheres touch when their centres come within $R+r$, so the collector catches every partner whose centre enters a tube of radius $R+r$, not $R$. In a time $dt$ the partners advance $\Delta v\,dt$, with $\Delta v=|v_t(R)-v_t(r)|$ the difference of the fall speeds. Multiply the tube volume by the partners per volume in $dr$:

$$dN_{\rm geom}=\pi(R+r)^2\,\Delta v\;n_r(r,t)\,dr\,dt.\tag{5}$$

Tube area, times the length it advances, times the partners per volume: the partners one collector touches in $dt$.

Two things spoil this count. Air flows around the collector and carries light partners around it; the fraction that still touch is the collision efficiency $E_{\rm coll}$ (chapter 5). And two drops that touch can bounce apart on a cushion of air; the fraction of contacts that end in a lasting merger is the coalescence efficiency $E_{\rm coal}$ (chapter 6). It is a probability given contact, so the two multiply:

$$K(R,r)=\pi(R+r)^2\,\Delta v\,E_{\rm coll}(R,r)\,E_{\rm coal}(R,r),\qquad dN_{\rm merge}=K\,n_r\,dr\,dt.\tag{6}$$

The kernel is the volume of air one collector effectively clears of partners per second.

$K$ is in m³/s. Assumed The thesis’s equations (9)–(10) use the geometric area and a coalescence factor, with no collision efficiency; the airflow efficiency is the app’s extension. The app’s “thesis kernel” keeps Stokes fall speeds, $E_{\rm coll}=1$ and the thesis’s $E_{\rm coal}$; its “new kernel” uses the drag-balance speeds of chapter 4 and the stored efficiencies of chapter 5.

For a fixed pair of sizes, doubling the partner concentration doubles one collector’s merger rate. Doubling both populations quadruples the events per volume, equation (8) below. The kernel itself stays the same: it describes how the pair moves and interacts; concentrations say how often such pairs are available.

Each merger adds the partner’s mass $m(r)$ to the collector. Summing over partners gives the collector’s mass gain, and (1) turns it into radius growth:

$$\dot M=\int m(r)\,K(R,r)\,n_r(r,t)\,dr,\qquad \dot R=\frac{\dot M}{4\pi\rho_pR^2}.\tag{7}$$

Partner mass, times the rate of meeting such partners, summed over partners: the thesis’s equation (10).

If the collectors themselves have a density $n_R(R,t)$, the mergers per cubic metre per second between two size classes are the per-collector rate times the number of collectors:

$$d\mathcal C=K(R,r)\,n_R(R,t)\,n_r(r,t)\,dR\,dr\,dt.\tag{8}$$

Events per volume grow with the product of the two concentrations, as in a chemical reaction between two species.

Worked example: one kernel value, from the app’s own code

Air at 10 °C and 850 hPa, a collector of radius $R=40$ µm and a partner of $r=8$ µm. Computed The drag-balance speeds of chapter 4 are 0.1771 and 0.00796 m/s, so $\Delta v=0.1691$ m/s. The geometric rate is $\pi(48\times10^{-6})^2\times0.1691=1.224\times10^{-9}$ m³/s. The stored collision efficiency is $E_{\rm coll}=0.343$ and $E_{\rm coal}=0.99998$, so $K=4.20\times10^{-10}$ m³/s.

If all 300 cloud droplets per cm³ ($3\times10^8$ m⁻³) had radius 8 µm, the collector would merge with one every $1/(KN)=7.9$ s, gaining $2.7\times10^{-13}$ kg/s; by (7) its radius would grow at 0.013 µm/s. With the real spread of sizes in the reference cloud mode, the code gives 0.131 events per second and 0.022 µm/s. The thesis kernel for the same pair is 3.3 times larger: Stokes makes the collector fall 11 % faster, and $E_{\rm coll}=1$ counts every droplet in the tube.

Keep three numbers apart: $K$ (m³/s) is a volume rate; $Kn_r\,dr$ (s⁻¹) is one collector’s event rate; $Kn_Rn_r\,dR\,dr$ (m⁻³ s⁻¹) is the event rate per volume of air.

A larger drop usually sweeps a wider tube and overtakes small droplets faster. That can reinforce growth, as on the window pane, but it does not prove that $\dot R$ always rises with $R$. Radius growth divides the mass gain by $4\pi\rho_pR^2$, and the efficiencies and available partners also change. For partners much smaller than the collector, the $R^2$ of the swept tube cancels this $R^2$: $\dot R\approx E\,v_t(R)\,L/(4\rho_p)$, with $E$ the collection efficiency and $L$ the water content of the partners, so the radius grows faster only because the drop falls faster and collects more efficiently. In particular, fast encounters can merge less readily. And two identical liquid spheres fall at the same speed: $\Delta v=0$, and gravity never brings them together. Derived In the code such a pair gets $K=0$; turbulence or electric forces, which could join them, are outside this model.

Chapter 04

The fall speed

Start with forces, derive the creeping-flow law, check where it holds, and see what replaces it.

Every encounter depends on a difference of fall speeds. Air resists a moving sphere in two ways. It sticks to the surface, so layers of air must slide past each other, like honey round a slowly drawn spoon. And the sphere must push air out of its way, like a hand held out of a car window. Which of the two wins decides the law of fall.

Newton’s law and terminal settling

Take the downward speed $v$ (m/s) of a sphere of radius $R$. Its weight less buoyancy is $(4\pi/3)R^3(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0$; the buoyancy uses the density of the surrounding air, not the cloud’s water content. With Stokes’ creeping-flow drag $6\pi\mu_aRv$, derived below, Newton’s second law reads

$$m\dot v=\frac{4\pi}{3}R^3(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0-6\pi\mu_aRv,\qquad \tau_p=\frac{m}{6\pi\mu_aR}=\frac{2\rho_pR^2}{9\mu_a}.\tag{9}$$

Mass times acceleration equals a fixed push down minus a drag that grows with speed. $\tau_p$ (s) is the time the drag needs to change the speed appreciably.

Divided by $m$, this is $\dot v=(v_t-v)/\tau_p$: the speed relaxes toward a constant $v_t$ with time constant $\tau_p$. Setting $\dot v=0$ gives the terminal speed, the thesis’s equation (19):

$$v_t^S=\frac{2(\rho_p-\rho_a)\,g_0R^2}{9\mu_a}.\tag{10}$$

A weight growing as $R^3$ balances a drag growing as $Rv$, so $v\propto R^2$: double the radius, four times the speed.

Worked example: how quickly a droplet forgets its starting speed

The solution of (9) is

$$v(t)=v_t+\big[v(0)-v_t\big]\,e^{-t/\tau_p}.\tag{11}$$

Whatever speed the sphere starts with, it forgets it exponentially and settles at $v_t$.

Computed At 10 °C the app’s viscosity is $\mu_a=1.771\times10^{-5}$ Pa s. A water droplet of radius 10 µm has $\tau_p=2\times1000\times(10^{-5})^2/(9\times1.771\times10^{-5})=1.26$ ms and falls at 1.24 cm/s, so in one relaxation time it travels 16 µm, less than its own diameter. A droplet of radius 50 µm has $\tau_p=31$ ms and travels 8 mm. Collection changes sizes over seconds to minutes, so taking $v=v_t(R)$ at every moment is safe here.

Two kinds of resistance, and their ratio

Estimate both. Viscous stress is of order $\mu_av/R$, a speed difference $v$ across a distance $R$; over an area $R^2$ it gives a force of order $\mu_aRv$. Pushing air aside is a momentum flow: each second the sphere meets air of mass $\rho_a\pi R^2v$ and gives it a speed of order $v$, a force of order $\rho_aR^2v^2$. Their ratio is the Reynolds number:

$$\frac{F_{\rm inert}}{F_{\rm visc}}\sim\frac{\rho_aRv}{\mu_a},\qquad \mathrm{Re}\equiv\frac{2R\,\rho_a\,v}{\mu_a}.\tag{12}$$

Small Re: viscosity wins and drag is linear in speed. Large Re: pushing air aside wins. By custom Re uses the diameter.

Derivation detail: the factor 6π in Stokes’ drag

The steady inertia term in the flow equations is of order $\rho_aU^2/R$, the viscous term $\mu_aU/R^2$. For $\mathrm{Re}\ll1$ the first is dropped, and incompressible air obeys $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, $\nabla p=\mu_a\nabla^2\mathbf u$. Hold the sphere at the origin and let air stream past at $U$ along $+\mathbf e_z$. In spherical coordinates, $\varrho$ is the distance from the centre, $q=\varrho/R$, and $\theta$ the angle from $+\mathbf e_z$, the direction the air flows. Stokes’ solution, with no slip at the surface, is

$$\begin{aligned}\psi_S&=\tfrac12UR^2q^2\sin^2\theta\Big(1-\frac{3}{2q}+\frac{1}{2q^3}\Big),\\ u_\varrho&=U\cos\theta\Big(1-\frac{3}{2q}+\frac{1}{2q^3}\Big),\\ u_\theta&=-U\sin\theta\Big(1-\frac{3}{4q}-\frac{1}{4q^3}\Big).\end{aligned}\tag{13}$$

Far away, uniform flow. The $1/q$ term is the slow, long-range disturbance of the force the sphere exerts; the $1/q^3$ term makes the velocity vanish on the surface.

$2\pi\psi_S$ is the volume of air per second through the circle traced by rotating a point about the axis, with $u_\varrho=\partial_\theta\psi/(\varrho^2\sin\theta)$ and $u_\theta=-\partial_\varrho\psi/(\varrho\sin\theta)$. At $q=1$ both brackets vanish. The pressure is $p-p_\infty=-\tfrac32\mu_aU\cos\theta/(Rq^2)$. On the surface $\partial_\varrho u_\varrho=0$, so the normal viscous stress vanishes and only the shear $\mu_a\partial_\varrho u_\theta$ survives:

$$p-p_\infty=-\frac{3\mu_aU}{2R}\cos\theta,\qquad \tau_{\varrho\theta}=-\frac{3\mu_aU}{2R}\sin\theta.\tag{14}$$

Higher pressure on the upstream face, lower downstream, and a shear dragging the surface along with the flow.

Along $\mathbf e_z$ the pressure contributes $-(p-p_\infty)\cos\theta$ per unit area and the shear $-\tau_{\varrho\theta}\sin\theta$: $3\mu_aU/(2R)$ times $\cos^2\theta$ and $\sin^2\theta$. Their sum is uniform over the sphere. Integrate over $4\pi R^2$; the constant $p_\infty$ integrates to zero:

$$F=\underbrace{\frac{3\mu_aU}{2R}\cdot\frac{4\pi R^2}{3}}_{\text{pressure}}+\underbrace{\frac{3\mu_aU}{2R}\cdot\frac{8\pi R^2}{3}}_{\text{shear}}=2\pi\mu_aRU+4\pi\mu_aRU=6\pi\mu_aRU.\tag{15}$$

One third of Stokes’ drag is pressure, two thirds skin friction. Dimensions give $\mu_aRU$; only solving the flow gives the $6\pi$.

Self-consistency comes before extrapolation

Stokes’ law assumes $\mathrm{Re}\ll1$. Check it with the speed the law itself predicts, (10) in (12):

$$\mathrm{Re}(v_t^S)=\frac{2R\rho_a}{\mu_a}\cdot\frac{2(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0R^2}{9\mu_a}=\frac{4\rho_a(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0}{9\mu_a^2}\,R^3.\tag{16}$$

The Reynolds number of Stokes’ own speed grows as $R^3$: the law undermines itself as drops grow.

A general drag balance

The drag can depend on $R$, $v$, $\rho_a$ and $\mu_a$: with the force, five quantities in three base units, so by Buckingham’s theorem exactly two pure numbers. One is Re. The other is the drag in units of the dynamic pressure $\tfrac12\rho_av^2$ times the cross-section $\pi R^2$: the drag coefficient $C_D$. So the general law is $F_d=\tfrac12\rho_av^2\pi R^2C_D(\mathrm{Re})$. Balance it against weight less buoyancy:

$$\frac43\pi R^3(\rho_p-\rho_a)\,g_0=\frac12\rho_a\,\pi R^2\,v_t^2\,C_D(\mathrm{Re})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_D=\frac83\,\frac{R(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0}{\rho_av_t^2}.\tag{17}$$

The unknown speed sits on both sides: once in plain view, once hidden inside $C_D(\mathrm{Re})$.

Multiply by $\mathrm{Re}^2=4R^2v_t^2\rho_a^2/\mu_a^2$, and $v_t^2$ cancels:

$$X\equiv C_D\,\mathrm{Re}^2=\frac{32\,\rho_a(\rho_p-\rho_a)\,g_0}{3\mu_a^2}\,R^3=\frac{4\rho_a(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0}{3\mu_a^2}\,d^3,\qquad v_t=\frac{\mu_a\,\mathrm{Re}}{2R\,\rho_a}.\tag{18}$$

The Davies number $X$ (also called the Best number) holds only the sphere and the air. Find the Re that matches it, then read off the speed.

Check the recipe with Stokes’ drag: $6\pi\mu_aRv=\tfrac12\rho_av^2\pi R^2C_D$ gives

$$C_D=\frac{24}{\mathrm{Re}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X=24\,\mathrm{Re}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ln\mathrm{Re}=\ln X-3.178.\tag{19}$$

With Stokes’ drag the chain returns (10) exactly. Any other drag law is a different function $\mathrm{Re}(X)$.

Derived Dimensional analysis does not supply $C_D(\mathrm{Re})$. Between Re of about 1 and 1000 the flow equations around a sphere have no closed-form solution, so this step rests on measurement.

The empirical closure the app uses

Assumed The app adopts piecewise fits attributed to Beard (1976), with coefficients as in the open-source disdrodb implementation: below a diameter of 19 µm, Stokes’ law with a slip correction; from 19 µm to 1.07 mm, a fitted curve $\mathrm{Re}(X)$; above 1.07 mm, for liquid only, a fit that lets surface tension flatten the drop. Ice continues the middle fit to the top of the grid, an extrapolation. The slip correction enters the first two regimes. A droplet a few hundred molecular free paths across no longer sees smooth air; molecules slip past it and it falls a little faster:

$$v_t=\frac{(\rho_p-\rho_a)\,g_0\,d^2}{18\,\mu_a}\,C_{\rm sc}\ \ (d\lt19\ \mu\text{m}),\qquad C_{\rm sc}=1+\frac{2.51\,\ell_a}{d},\qquad \ell_a=\frac{\mu_a}{p}\sqrt{\frac{\pi R_dT}{2}}.\tag{20}$$

Stokes’ speed in terms of the diameter, times a slip factor built on the mean free path $\ell_a$ of air molecules. It matters only for the smallest droplets.

Here $R_d=287.05$ J/(kg K) is the gas constant of dry air and $T$ is in kelvin. Computed At 10 °C and 850 hPa, $\ell_a=0.074$ µm; the slip factor is 1.0098 at $d=19$ µm and 1.093 at 2 µm.

Technical detail: the fitted regimes, their coefficients, and the air

$$\mathrm{Re}=C_{\rm sc}\exp\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{6}b_n(\ln X)^n\Big),\qquad \begin{aligned}&b_0=-3.18657,\ b_1=0.992696,\ b_2=-1.53193\times10^{-3},\\ &b_3=-9.87059\times10^{-4},\ b_4=-5.78878\times10^{-4},\\ &b_5=8.55176\times10^{-5},\ b_6=-3.27815\times10^{-6}.\end{aligned}\tag{21}$$

The first two coefficients are close to Stokes’ $-3.178$ and 1 in (19); the higher powers carry the measured inertial correction.

$$\mathrm{Bo}=\frac{4(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0d^2}{3\sigma},\quad N_P=\frac{\sigma^3\rho_a^2}{\mu_a^4(\rho_p-\rho_a)g_0},\quad \mathrm{Re}=N_P^{1/6}\exp\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{5}c_nY^n\Big),\quad Y=\ln\big(\mathrm{Bo}\,N_P^{1/6}\big).\tag{22}$$

Chapter 05

Collision efficiency

Does a partner carried by the air actually touch the collector?

Drive through heavy rain and every drop in front of the car hits the windscreen. Drive through fine mist and much of it never touches the glass: the air pushed aside carries it around the car. A falling drop does the same to the droplets below it, like a stone in a stream turning leaves aside. Only a droplet heavy enough to keep going while the air turns reaches the surface. That fraction is the collision efficiency $E_{\rm coll}$.

The partner’s equation of motion

Fix the collector at the origin with $\mathbf e_z$ up. Far away the air rises past it at $U=v_t(R)$ and a partner sinks through that air at $v_t(r)$. Near the collector the air has a velocity field $\mathbf u(\mathbf x)$. The partner, at $\mathbf x$ with velocity $\mathbf v$, feels Stokes drag on its velocity relative to the local air, and its weight less buoyancy:

$$\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf v,\qquad \tau_p\,\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf u(\mathbf x)-\mathbf v-\tau_pg^*\mathbf e_z,\qquad g^*=g_0\Big(1-\frac{\rho_a}{\rho_p}\Big).\tag{24}$$

The partner relaxes toward the local air velocity, shifted down by its own settling speed $\tau_pg^*$, with the relaxation time (9) for radius $r$.

Assumed This Stokes drag concerns the partner’s slip, so its validity rests on the partner’s Reynolds number. Computed With the balance speed as slip, it reaches 1 at $r=42.7$ µm (10 °C, 850 hPa). The tables run to $r/R=0.6$ for collectors up to 2 mm, partners of 1.2 mm, where it is about 1100: there the trajectory model is outside its range.

Scale it, and one number appears

Measure lengths in $R$, speeds in $U$, and time in $R/U$, the time the air takes to pass the collector: $\mathbf y=\mathbf x/R$, $\mathbf w=\mathbf v/U$, $s=tU/R$, $\mathbf a=\mathbf u/U$. Then

$$\frac{d\mathbf y}{ds}=\mathbf w,\qquad \mathrm{St}\,\frac{d\mathbf w}{ds}=\mathbf a(\mathbf y)-\mathbf w-S\,\mathbf e_z,\qquad \mathrm{St}=\frac{\tau_pU}{R},\quad S=\frac{v_t(r)}{U}.\tag{25}$$

The Stokes number St compares the partner’s memory time with the time the air needs to turn around the collector; $S$ is the partner’s own settling, in collector speeds.

One approximation in the trajectory equation

Equation (24) uses linear Stokes drag. Strictly, its settling speed is $\tau_pg^*=v_t^S(r)$, the Stokes speed of (10), so that is the value of $S\,U$ that (24) gives when (25) is derived. Using the app’s fitted speed $v_t(r)$ in $S$ while keeping the same relaxation time is a hybrid approximation, not an exact rescaling of (24). The browser reads stored efficiency tables; their generating code is unavailable, so this page cannot establish which choice it used. The trajectory argument explains the mechanism without proving the tables valid at every size.

When $\mathrm{St}\to0$ the partner follows the air, shifted by its settling, and touches only by interception: its radius reaches the surface while its centre rides past. When $\mathrm{St}\to\infty$ it goes straight, and every partner in the tube hits. Contact is at $|\mathbf y|=1+r/R$. Computed At 10 °C and 850 hPa an 8 µm partner has $\mathrm{St}=1.9$ with a 20 µm collector and 3.6 with a 40 µm one; a 10 µm partner of a 100 µm collector has 9.1.

Two model flows around the collector

The air field depends on the collector’s Reynolds number, with exact solutions only in the limits. Creeping flow ($\mathrm{Re}\to0$) is Stokes’ field (13): the air sticks to the surface and its disturbance decays slowly, as $1/q$, deflecting droplets many radii upstream. Potential flow ($\mathrm{Re}\to\infty$, no boundary layer) lets the air slip along the surface; its disturbance decays as $1/q^3$, so droplets are deflected only close by and more of them hit. Neither has a wake, so the two are model limits, not proven bounds.

Technical detail: the potential-flow field
$$\psi_P=\tfrac12UR^2q^2\sin^2\theta\Big(1-\frac{1}{q^3}\Big),\qquad a_\varrho=\cos\theta\,(1-q^{-3}),\qquad a_\theta=-\sin\theta\,\Big(1+\frac{1}{2q^3}\Big).\tag{26}$$

Uniform flow plus a dipole. Only the normal velocity vanishes on the surface. The disturbance decays as $1/q^3$, so droplets are deflected only close by, and more of them hit.

The critical Stokes number: a point droplet on the axis

Take a point partner ($r/R\to0$) that does not settle ($S\to0$), on the axis straight below the collector. In potential flow the air on this axis approaches the surface and stops there. Let $\eta=q-1$ be the distance from the surface in collector radii. On the upstream axis $\cos\theta=-1$, so the air moves toward the surface at $1-(1+\eta)^{-3}\approx3\eta$. Near the surface the partner obeys, with primes for derivatives in the scaled time $s$,

$$\mathrm{St}\,\eta''+\eta'+3\eta=0,\qquad \eta\propto e^{\kappa s},\quad \kappa=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-12\,\mathrm{St}}}{2\,\mathrm{St}}.\tag{27}$$

A damped oscillator. If $12\,\mathrm{St}\gt1$ it overshoots zero and the partner reaches the surface; if not, it creeps toward the surface for ever.

Derived So in potential flow $\mathrm{St}_c=1/12$ exactly, radius-based (1/24 with the collector’s diameter). In creeping flow the air sticks, its approach speed vanishes as $\eta^2$, and the linear argument fails; the threshold needs the whole axis. The literature value (Phillips and Kaye 1999, as cited in arXiv:1708.03198) is $\mathrm{St}_c\approx1.21$ (0.605 diameter-based): a heavier partner is needed to reach a surface the air itself never quite reaches.

From the grazing trajectory to the efficiency

Start the partner far below at a sideways offset $b$, moving with the far-field velocity, integrate (25), and see whether it touches. Search for the grazing offset $b_c$: inside it partners hit, outside they miss. If hits fill one disc and far-upstream partners are spread evenly and move at one speed $\Delta v$, the hit rate is $\pi b_c^2\Delta v$ times the concentration. Divide by the straight-path rate of chapter 3:

$$E_{\rm coll}=\frac{\pi b_c^2\,\Delta v}{\pi(R+r)^2\,\Delta v}=\frac{b_c^2}{(R+r)^2}.\tag{28}$$

The real capture area over the geometric one. Below 1 means the air steered partners out of the tube.

Technical detail: starting at a finite distance

The cancellation needs equal speeds and concentrations in both counts, true only infinitely far upstream. A real calculation starts at a finite distance $Z$ (in units of $R$). The earlier write-up counted partners through the starting disc with the air’s stream function (in units of $UR^2$), less the partners’ own settling through it:

$$\Phi(b)=2\pi\Big[\psi(b,-Z)-\tfrac12S\,b^2\Big],\qquad E_{\rm coll}=\frac{2\big[\psi(b_c,-Z)-\tfrac12S\,b_c^2\big]}{(1-S)(1+r/R)^2}.\tag{29}$$

Partner flux through the start disc over the geometric flux. Far upstream $\psi\to\tfrac12b^2$ and this becomes (28).

The stream function is conserved along air streamlines, but an inertial partner does not follow them, so this alone does not make the result independent of $Z$. The earlier write-up reports a convergence test ($Z=30\,\mathrm{St}$; doubling $Z$ changed no efficiency by more than $10^{-3}$) and a time-step test (largest change $10^{-4}$). The program that made the tables is not in the app, so neither test can be rerun here.

Chapter 06

Coalescence

Two drops touch. Do they stay together? Derive the energy ratio, then name the fitted law.

Watch rain fall on a puddle. Some drops vanish into it at once; others bounce, or skate across on a thin film of air before they sink. Two drops in a cloud face the same choice. A gentle touch lets surface tension pull them into one sphere. A hard hit can deform them so much that they bounce apart or break up again. So compare the energy the collision brings with the energy held in the drops’ surfaces.

The energy that can deform the drops

Two drops of masses $m_1$, $m_2$ move with velocities $\mathbf v_1$, $\mathbf v_2$. The kinetic energy of their common centre of mass survives the collision whatever happens, because momentum is conserved. Only the rest can squash the drops: the energy of relative motion, carried by the reduced mass $\mu_{\rm red}=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$.

Derivation detail: splitting off the centre of mass
$$\tfrac12m_1v_1^2+\tfrac12m_2v_2^2=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)V^2+\tfrac12\mu_{\rm red}\,|\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2|^2,\qquad \mu_{\rm red}=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.\tag{32}$$

Total energy is the energy of the centre of mass, moving at $V$, plus the energy of relative motion, carried by the reduced mass $\mu_{\rm red}$.

Write $\mathbf v_1=\mathbf V+\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}\Delta\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf v_2=\mathbf V-\frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}\Delta\mathbf v$, with $\Delta\mathbf v=\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf V=(m_1\mathbf v_1+m_2\mathbf v_2)/(m_1+m_2)$. Square and add with weights $\tfrac12m_1$, $\tfrac12m_2$. The cross terms in $\mathbf V\cdot\Delta\mathbf v$ cancel, because $m_1\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}=m_2\frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}$. What is left is $\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)V^2$ plus $\tfrac12\Delta v^2\,m_1m_2(m_1+m_2)/(m_1+m_2)^2=\tfrac12\mu_{\rm red}\Delta v^2$.

For falling drops $\Delta v$ is the difference of terminal speeds. In terms of the diameters $D\ge d$, with $m=\pi\rho_wD^3/6$:

$$\Delta E_k=\frac12\,\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,(\Delta v)^2=\frac{\pi\rho_w}{12}\,\frac{D^3d^3}{D^3+d^3}\,(\Delta v)^2.\tag{33}$$

The energy of relative motion present before contact. The collision does not create it; it is what the collision can spend.

The energy held in surfaces

Volume is conserved, so the merged drop has diameter $d_f=(D^3+d^3)^{1/3}$. The thesis compares the collision energy with that sphere’s surface energy:

$$E_s=\pi\sigma d_f^2=\pi\sigma\,(D^3+d^3)^{2/3},\qquad E_{\rm freed}=\pi\sigma\big(D^2+d^2-d_f^2\big)\gt0.\tag{34}$$

One sphere has less surface than two of the same total volume, so a merger frees surface energy. $E_s$ is the scale against which the thesis measures the hit.

The ratio and the adopted law

The thesis defines an energy-based Weber number as the ratio of the two, and lets the coalescence efficiency fall exponentially with it:

$$W_E=\frac{\Delta E_k}{\pi\sigma\,(D^3+d^3)^{2/3}},\qquad E_{\rm coal}=\exp(-1.15\,W_E).\tag{35}$$

Gentle collisions ($W_E\ll1$) almost always merge; hard ones ($W_E\gg1$) almost never.

Derived The ratio follows from energy bookkeeping. Assumed The exponential and its 1.15 are a fitted law, the thesis’s equations (21)–(24) in §4.3.1, not a consequence of energy conservation. Nor is this the common $\rho v^2d/\sigma$ Weber number, so thresholds cannot be moved between the two.

Technical detail: the surface-tension fit
$$\sigma=0.241322\,\tau_T^{1.26}\big(1-0.058\,\tau_T^{1/2}-0.569\,\tau_T\big)\ \text{N/m},\qquad \tau_T=1-\frac{T}{647.096\ \text{K}}.\tag{36}$$

Surface tension falls as water warms toward its critical temperature. Cold water pulls harder: 0.0772 N/m at −10 °C, 0.0744 at 10 °C.

Chapter 07

Population balances

From one collector to the whole spectrum: the GCE, the SCE, and how one contains the other.

Keep a ledger with one line per size. A merger of a 10 µm and a 20 µm droplet crosses out an entry on each of their lines and writes one on the line of their combined mass, about 20.8 µm. A population balance is that ledger per unit time and size; chapter 3 gave the rates, and now we add them up without counting anything twice.

The thesis’s collector-and-pool construction

The thesis splits the particles into two fixed groups: collectors $n_R(R,t)$ and a pool of collected droplets $n_r(r,t)$. A pool droplet disappears when any collector absorbs it. Summing (8) over collectors:

$$\partial_t n_r(r,t)=-n_r(r,t)\int K(R,r)\,n_R(R,t)\,dR.\tag{37}$$

Each pool droplet is removed at a rate set by all collectors together. Pool droplets keep their size; they only become fewer.

The minus sign is required by depletion and agrees with the thesis’s equations (28)–(30); the printed equation (12) does not display it. The collectors keep their number and grow, each at the rate $\dot R$ of (7). A crowd that keeps its number but moves along the radius axis obeys a continuity equation, like cars on a road without exits:

$$\partial_t n_R(R,t)+\partial_R\big(\dot R\,n_R\big)=0,\qquad n_R(R,t)\,dR=n_{R}(R_0,0)\,dR_0.\tag{38}$$

Collectors between two radii change only by growing in or out. Each carries its share of the count along its growth path from $R_0$.

Derivation detail: why the density changes when the number does not

Follow two collectors that start at $R_a(0)$ and $R_b(0)$ and grow at $\dot R(R_a)$ and $\dot R(R_b)$. On a single growth law they cannot overtake each other, so the number between them is fixed: $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{R_a(t)}^{R_b(t)}n_R\,dR=0$. Leibniz’s rule gives $\int_{R_a}^{R_b}\partial_tn_R\,dR+\dot R_bn_R(R_b)-\dot R_an_R(R_a)=0$, and the last two terms are $\int_{R_a}^{R_b}\partial_R(\dot Rn_R)\,dR$. The interval is arbitrary, so the integrand vanishes: (38). Because bigger collectors grow faster, the crowd spreads along the axis; its number is fixed but its density per unit radius falls, as cars thin out when the ones in front speed up.

Three kinds of event are absent here: collectors never collect each other, pool droplets never collect each other, and a pool droplet never becomes a collector. The thesis says so in §5.1: “collectors do not disappear or even cross roads”. That is good for a short time and for collectors far larger than the pool; over tens of minutes the pool coarsens and collectors catch one another.

Binary events for a single spectrum

Now drop the two groups: every particle may collect or be collected. A particle of mass $m$ is created when partners of masses $m'$ and $m-m'$ merge, and disappears when it merges with anything. In a well-mixed box pair rates follow (8). Add every way to make $m$ and subtract every way to lose it:

$$\partial_tn_m(m,t)=\underbrace{\frac12\int_0^mK(m',m-m')\,n_m(m',t)\,n_m(m-m',t)\,dm'}_{\text{gain}}-\underbrace{n_m(m,t)\int_0^\infty K(m,m')\,n_m(m',t)\,dm'}_{\text{loss}}.\tag{39}$$

Births from all pairs that add up to $m$, minus deaths of particles of mass $m$ in any merger, with the kernel (6) written in masses.

The one half is there because, as $m'$ runs from 0 to $m$, each unordered pair is met twice; the loss term follows one particular particle, so nothing is doubled there. This is Smoluchowski’s coagulation equation, in cloud physics the stochastic collection equation. “Stochastic” describes the random events; the equation evolves their expected density deterministically, treating pairs as uncorrelated. The app does not draw random merger histories.

For a concrete entry in the ledger, suppose the only allowed event joins a particle of mass $m_0$ to one of mass $2m_0$. If it happens $C$ times per cubic metre per second, the three populations change at rates $-C$, $-C$ and $+C$. Number falls at rate $C$; the mass change is $-m_0C-2m_0C+3m_0C=0$. The two integrals in (39) apply this same rule to every possible pair. In the gain integral at $m=3m_0$ this pair appears twice, at $m'=m_0$ and at $m'=2m_0$; the ½ counts it once.

The GCE inside the SCE: a jump expansion

Two initially equal collectors need not catch the same number of droplets. One catches sooner and moves ahead; another waits longer. The smooth growth law follows their mean motion along the mass axis. A second term is needed to describe the spread around that mean. The thesis’s smooth growth comes back out of (39) in a limit. Keep only collector–pool events, write $f_C(M,t)$ for the collectors, and suppose each jump is small, $m\ll M$. Expanding the gain term in the jump size gives

$$\partial_tf_C=-\partial_M\big(A\,f_C\big)+\tfrac12\,\partial_M^2\big(B\,f_C\big)-\cdots,\qquad A(M)=\int m\,K(M,m)\,n_m\,dm,\quad B(M)=\int m^2K(M,m)\,n_m\,dm.\tag{40}$$

A drift along the mass axis at speed $A$, plus a spreading at rate $B$, because the jumps are discrete and random in timing.

Derivation detail: the expansion

A collector of mass $M$ is born when one of mass $M-m$ swallows a pool droplet of mass $m$, and lost when a collector of mass $M$ swallows anything:

$$\partial_tf_C(M)=\int K(M-m,m)\,f_C(M-m)\,n_m(m)\,dm-f_C(M)\int K(M,m)\,n_m(m)\,dm.\tag{41}$$

Collectors jump up the mass axis by the mass of each droplet they swallow: gain from below, loss from here.

Write $h(M)=K(M,m)\,f_C(M)$ for fixed $m$ and expand $h(M-m)=h-m\,\partial_Mh+\tfrac12m^2\partial_M^2h-\cdots$. Integrate with weight $n_m(m)$. The first term is exactly the loss. The second gives $-\partial_M(Af_C)$, the third $\tfrac12\partial_M^2(Bf_C)$. Units: $A$ is kg/s, $B$ kg²/s, and one derivative of $Af_C$ or two of $Bf_C$ have the units of $\partial_tf_C$. Multiply the drift term by $M$ and integrate by parts: the mean collector mass grows at $A$, the GCE rate (7). Dropping the higher terms needs small jumps; it is not an identity.

Derived So the thesis’s equations are the SCE with two classes of event removed and the collectors’ gains and losses collapsed into a drift. Comparing the two changes two things at once: which events are allowed, and how growth is represented.

Chapter 08

Conservation

Mass and number are different moments. Derive what each event does to them before looking at any chart.

Push two piles of coins together: one pile fewer, not one coin fewer. Merging drops is the same: number goes down, mass stays. Splintering ice is the reverse: number goes up, mass still stays. To make this exact for the whole spectrum, take moments of the SCE.

Moments of the collection equation

Multiply (39) by a weight $\phi(m)$ and integrate. In the gain term change variables to the two incoming masses; in the loss term use the symmetry $K(m,m')=K(m',m)$ to share the term between partners. For one phase,

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int\phi(m)\,n_m(m)\,dm=\frac12\iint K(m,m')\,n_m(m)\,n_m(m')\,\big[\phi(m+m')-\phi(m)-\phi(m')\big]\,dm\,dm'.\tag{42}$$

Every merger replaces $\phi(m)+\phi(m')$ by $\phi(m+m')$. The bracket is that change, event by event; the rest counts events.

Derivation detail: from (39) to (42)

Gain: $\frac12\int_0^\infty\phi(m)\int_0^mK(m',m-m')n(m')n(m-m')\,dm'\,dm$. Put $m''=m-m'$; the region $0\lt m'\lt m$ becomes all $m',m''\gt0$ with Jacobian 1, so the gain is $\frac12\iint\phi(m'+m'')K(m',m'')n(m')n(m'')\,dm'\,dm''$. Loss: $\iint\phi(m)K(m,m')n(m)n(m')\,dm\,dm'$; swap $m\leftrightarrow m'$ and average the two forms to get $\frac12\iint[\phi(m)+\phi(m')]Kn\,n'$. Subtract, rename $m''\to m'$, and (42) follows.

Now choose the weight:

$$\begin{aligned}\phi=m:&\quad \frac{dL}{dt}=0,\\ \phi=1:&\quad \frac{d\mathcal N}{dt}=-\frac12\iint K\,n_m\,n_m'\,dm\,dm'\le0,\\ \phi=m^2:&\quad \frac{d}{dt}\int m^2n_m\,dm=\iint K\,m\,m'\,n_m\,n_m'\,dm\,dm'\ge0.\end{aligned}\tag{43}$$

Mass is conserved event by event; number falls by one per merger; the second moment grows, since $(m+m')^2-m^2-m'^2=2mm'$: merging always builds bigger particles.

Derived Collection conserves mass and lowers number. “The scheme conserves number” can only mean that a fixed set of products is placed on the grid without changing their count (chapter 9), never that merging keeps the number. For the sum kernel $K=b\,(m+m')$, with $b$ in m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹, the number integral can be finished: it is $\frac12b\,(L\mathcal N+\mathcal NL)$, so

$$\frac{d\mathcal N}{dt}=-bL\mathcal N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal N(t)=\mathcal N_0\,e^{-bLt}.\tag{44}$$

With the sum kernel the number decays exponentially at a rate set by the conserved mass: an exact test used in chapter 9.

Phases and reservoirs

Freezing moves mass from the liquid to the ice spectrum; splintering moves part of a particle’s mass into fragments. Neither creates mass. On a finite grid two more places can hold mass: products heavier than the last bin go to an overflow store, and passive fragments to a passive store. Neither interacts again. The complete inventory is

$$L_{\rm liq}(t)+L_{\rm ice}(t)+L_{\rm over}(t)+L_{\rm pass}(t)=L_0.\tag{45}$$

In-grid liquid, in-grid ice, overflow and passive fragments add up to the initial mass. Leave out a reservoir and mass seems to vanish.

Computed In the reference reruns at 8 bins per doubling, the largest departure from (45) over 60 minutes is $1.3\times10^{-13}$ (warm, new kernel), $2.8\times10^{-13}$ (warm, thesis kernel) and $4$–$5\times10^{-11}$ (cold, with splintering); the earlier write-up’s bound of $10^{-11}$ holds for the warm runs. The reservoirs are not small: overflow holds 5.2 % of the mass after 60 minutes in the warm run with the new kernel, and with $\lambda=0.001$ and passive fragments the passive store holds 23 % after 10 minutes, 61 % after 20 and 90 % after 60.

Verification in a useful order

Check units and limits first; then that no density goes negative; then the inventory with every reservoir; then convergence in time step and grid; last, independent exact or measured results. A rounding-level mass residual is compatible with a badly resolved spectrum, a wrong kernel or an unphysical fragment law. Conservation is necessary; it is not validation.

Chapter 09

The numerical scheme

What each bin stores, what one time step does, what the scheme preserves, and how it fares against an exact solution.

A computer cannot hold a continuous distribution. It holds a row of boxes, one per size class, and moves mass between them. Two choices decide the quality: how the boxes are spaced, and what happens when a merger makes a particle whose mass falls between two boxes. The app spaces them logarithmically and splits each product between two boxes so that both its number and its mass are kept.

The grid and what it stores

$$x_k=x_0\,2^{k/s},\quad k=0,\ldots,N-1,\qquad \Delta=\ln\frac{r_{k+1}}{r_k}=\frac{\ln2}{3s}.\tag{46}$$

Each bin is a fixed factor heavier than the last: $s$ bins per doubling of mass. Since $m\propto r^3$, the bins are evenly spaced in $\ln r$, with step $\Delta$.

$x_0=4.19\times10^{-15}$ kg is the mass of a 1 µm water sphere, and the grid ends at the first bin that reaches a 2 mm water sphere. The same ice mass has a radius 3.6 % larger.

Each bin stores the mass spectrum $G_r$ of (4) at its grid mass, not a number density:

$$G_k=3x_k^2\,n_m(x_k),\qquad L_k=G_k\,\Delta,\qquad N_k=\frac{G_k\,\Delta}{x_k}.\tag{47}$$

Mass per unit $\ln r$, times the bin width, is the bin’s mass per cubic metre; divided by one particle’s mass, its number per cubic metre.

Storing mass makes the total $L=\Delta\sum_kG_k$ a plain sum, the quantity the scheme must keep exactly. The charts show $G_r$ in g/m³, the code computes in kg/m³: a unit change, not a different normalisation.

One pair and one time step

Take bins $i\le j$ and a time step $\Delta t$ (1 s in the app). Each particle of bin $j$ clears the volume $K_{ij}\Delta t$ of bin-$i$ particles, giving $C_{ij}$ mergers per cubic metre. Each removes a particle of mass $x_i$ from bin $i$ and one of mass $x_j$ from bin $j$. In the stored variable:

$$\begin{aligned}C_{ij}&=K_{ij}\,N_i\,N_j\,\Delta t,\\ Q_{ij}&=K_{ij}\,\Delta t\,\Delta\,G_i\,G_j=\frac{C_{ij}\,x_i\,x_j}{\Delta},\\ \delta G_i&=-\frac{Q_{ij}}{x_j},\qquad \delta G_j=-\frac{Q_{ij}}{x_i}.\end{aligned}\tag{48}$$

$C_{ij}$ counts events per cubic metre in the step; $Q_{ij}$ (kg² m⁻³) is a convenient intermediate. Bin $i$ loses $C_{ij}x_i/\Delta$, bin $j$ loses $C_{ij}x_j/\Delta$.

The code visits every pair once per step, smaller bin first, and updates in place, so a bin drained by one pair offers less to the next; pair order and $\Delta t$ therefore matter a little, and both are tested. A pair within one bin uses half the rate and removes two particles per event; with gravity alone equal sizes have $K_{ii}=0$, so that branch serves test kernels.

Deposit a product and keep two moments

The product mass $x_*=x_i+x_j$ almost never lands on a grid mass; it lies between some $x_k$ and $x_{k+1}$. Of the $\nu=C_{ij}$ products per cubic metre, put $\nu_k$ in bin $k$ and $\nu_{k+1}$ in bin $k+1$, and demand that their number and their mass both come out right:

$$\nu_k+\nu_{k+1}=\nu,\qquad x_k\nu_k+x_{k+1}\nu_{k+1}=x_*\nu\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nu_{k+1}=f\nu,\ \ \nu_k=(1-f)\nu,\ \ f=\frac{x_*-x_k}{x_{k+1}-x_k}.\tag{49}$$

Two conditions, two unknowns: a lever rule. The nearer bin gets the larger share.

Derived This keeps the number of products and their mass. It still replaces particles of one mass by a mixture of two neighbouring masses, so the spectrum is smeared a little. Mass conservation and spectral accuracy are separate tests.

A limiter that keeps every bin non-negative

An explicit step can ask a bin for more than it holds. The smaller partner’s bin loses $Q/x_j$, so $Q\le G_ix_j$. For the larger partner the guard $Q\le G_jx_i$ is too strict when the product stays in its bin, the usual case for a big drop swallowing a small one: $(1-f)$ of the loss comes straight back, and the net loss is $fQ/x_i$. So

$$Q_{ij}\le G_i\,x_j,\qquad Q_{ij}\le\begin{cases}G_j\,x_i/f,&x_*\lt x_{j+1}\ \ (\text{product stays in bin }j),\\ G_j\,x_i,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\tag{50}$$

Never take from a bin more than it can give, counting what the product returns to it.

Rounding leftovers below zero are set to zero and counted (zero in the warm reruns, $10^{-48}$ of the mass in the cold). The limiter prevents negative densities; it cannot prove the step accurate.

Initial data also define the experiment

Each initial mode is a lognormal number distribution, a bell curve in $\ln r$, with geometric median radius $r_g$, geometric standard deviation $\sigma_g$ (a pure number above 1) and number concentration $\mathcal N_0$:

$$\frac{d\mathcal N}{d\ln r}=\frac{\mathcal N_0}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\ln\sigma_g}\exp\Big[-\frac{\ln^2(r/r_g)}{2\ln^2\sigma_g}\Big],\qquad L_0=\frac{4\pi\rho_p}{3}\,\mathcal N_0\,r_g^3\,e^{\frac92\ln^2\sigma_g}.\tag{51}$$

Half the particles are smaller than $r_g$. The mass is the third moment: a wider mode holds more water at the same number and median.

Derivation detail: the third moment of a lognormal

Put $y=\ln(r/r_g)$ and $\beta=\ln\sigma_g$. Then $r^3=r_g^3e^{3y}$, and $\int e^{3y}e^{-y^2/(2\beta^2)}dy/(\sqrt{2\pi}\beta)$ is the moment-generating function of a normal variable at 3, $e^{9\beta^2/2}$. Multiply by $\tfrac43\pi\rho_p\mathcal N_0r_g^3$.

An exact test: the Golovin solution

No exact solution exists for the gravitational kernel, but one does for the artificial sum kernel $K=b\,(m+m')$ with an exponential start, $n_m(m,0)=(\mathcal N_0/\bar m)\,e^{-m/\bar m}$. It tests the whole spectrum, not just its mass. The Laplace transform $F(q,t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-qm}n_m\,dm$ turns the convolution in the gain term into a product, and leads (derivation below) to Golovin’s spectrum:

$$n_m(m,t)=\frac{\mathcal N_0}{\bar m}\,\frac{1-\zeta}{u\sqrt\zeta}\,I_1\big(2u\sqrt\zeta\big)\,e^{-(1+\zeta)u},\qquad u=\frac{m}{\bar m},\quad \zeta=1-e^{-bLt}.\tag{52}$$

As $\zeta\to0$ it returns the exponential start; as $\zeta\to1$ the mass moves to ever larger particles. $I_1$ is a modified Bessel function.

Derivation detail: the transform, the characteristics and the inverse

Transform. With $K=b(m'+m'')$ and $e^{-q(m'+m'')}=e^{-qm'}e^{-qm''}$, the gain becomes $\tfrac12b\cdot2\int m'n'e^{-qm'}dm'\int n''e^{-qm''}dm''=-bF\partial_qF$. The loss is $-b\int e^{-qm}n\,[m\mathcal N+L]\,dm=b\mathcal N\partial_qF-bLF$. Together:

$$\partial_tF=b\,(\mathcal N-F)\,\partial_qF-bLF.\tag{53}$$

A first-order equation for the transform; mass stays $L=\mathcal N_0\bar m$ and the number follows (44).

Rescale. Put $z=q\bar m$, $\zeta=1-e^{-bLt}$ and $H=F/\mathcal N$. The term $-bLF$ cancels against $H\,d\mathcal N/dt$, leaving $\partial_tH=b\mathcal N\bar m\,(1-H)\,\partial_zH$. Since $\mathcal N\bar m=Le^{-bLt}$ and $d\zeta/dt=bLe^{-bLt}$, the time derivative becomes a derivative in $\zeta$:

$$\partial_\zeta H=(1-H)\,\partial_zH,\qquad H(z,0)=\frac{1}{1+z}.\tag{54}$$

$H$ is carried unchanged along the characteristics $z=z_0+(H-1)\zeta$.

Solve. Along a characteristic $H=1/(1+z_0)$, so $z_0=1/H-1$. Insert into $z=z_0+(H-1)\zeta$ and multiply by $H$:

$$\zeta H^2-(1+z+\zeta)\,H+1=0,\qquad H=\frac{(1+z+\zeta)-\sqrt{(1+z+\zeta)^2-4\zeta}}{2\zeta}.\tag{55}$$

Choose the root that stays finite as $\zeta\to0$; it returns $1/(1+z)$ there.

Invert. The identity $\int_0^\infty e^{-Au}I_1(Bu)\,du/u=\big(A-\sqrt{A^2-B^2}\big)/B$, for $A\gt|B|$, with $A=1+z+\zeta$ and $B=2\sqrt\zeta$, shows that this root is the transform of (52). Here $I_1(a)=\sum_{k\ge0}(a/2)^{2k+1}/[k!\,(k+1)!]$.

Assumed The test uses Bott’s (1998) parameters: $b=1.5$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻¹, mean radius 10 µm ($\bar m=4.19\times10^{-12}$ kg), $L=1$ g/m³, so $\mathcal N_0=238.7$ cm⁻³. After 60 minutes $\zeta=0.99548$: 99.5 % of the particles have merged away and the mass peak has moved from about 13 µm to about 0.5 mm. The error $\varepsilon$ sums the absolute mass differences between the numerical and exact spectra, then divides by $L$. A deficit in one region and its matching excess elsewhere both count.

Technical detail: the error measure on the grid
$$\varepsilon=\frac{1}{L}\int\big|G_{\rm num}-G_{\rm exact}\big|\,d\ln r\approx\frac{\Delta}{L}\sum_k\big|G_k-G_{\rm exact}(x_k)\big|.\tag{56}$$

Zero is perfect. If both spectra contain the mass $L$, mass that sits in the wrong place is counted twice, once where it is missing and once where it has turned up; so when the two totals agree, the deficit and the excess are equal, each $\varepsilon L/2$, and $\varepsilon/2$ is the smallest fraction of the mass that must be moved to turn one spectrum into the other. Two spectra with no overlap at all give $\varepsilon=2$.

The fragment rule

Assumed In an ice–ice event with $m_b\ge m_a$, the app removes a fixed fraction $\lambda$ of $m_b$ and turns it into fragments of mass $m_f$, that of a 5 µm ice sphere ($4.71\times10^{-13}$ kg). The rest merges:

$$m_a+m_b\longrightarrow m_{\rm rem}+\nu_f\,m_f,\qquad m_{\rm rem}=m_a+(1-\lambda)\,m_b,\qquad \nu_f=\frac{\lambda\,m_b}{m_f}.\tag{58}$$

Mass in equals mass out. The number changes by $\nu_f-1$: two particles in, one remnant and $\nu_f$ fragments out. $\nu_f$ is an expected number, so it need not be whole.

Technical detail: the same rule on the grid

For bins $i\le j$ with $x_*=x_i+x_j$, the code splits the mass taken in an ice–ice step between remnant and fragments:

$$\phi_f=\frac{\lambda\,x_j}{x_i+x_j},\qquad \text{remnant at }x_*(1-\phi_f)\text{ with share }1-\phi_f,\qquad \text{fragments at }m_f\text{ with share }\phi_f.\tag{59}$$

The remnant is deposited by (49) at its own, smaller mass; the fragments go to the bin of $m_f$ or to the passive store.

Two consequences follow from (58). Derived An event raises the number only when $\nu_f\gt1$, that is when the larger particle exceeds $5\ \mu\text{m}\times\lambda^{-1/3}$: 50 µm for $\lambda=0.001$, 29 µm for 0.005, 23 µm for 0.01. A 100 µm ice sphere at $\lambda=0.001$ sheds 8 fragments per event. And an event shrinks the larger particle when it gains less than it sheds, $m_a\lt\lambda m_b$: for $\lambda=0.001$, whenever the partner’s radius is below a tenth of its own. Most partners are cloud-sized, so here large ice is ground down by its own collisions. A toy shows how fast. If a particle neither grows nor meets a changing population, and loses $\lambda$ in each of $n$ events,

$$\frac{m_n}{m_0}=(1-\lambda)^n\approx e^{-\lambda n}.\tag{60}$$

The fraction kept falls exponentially in $\lambda n$, the fraction per event times the number of events.

Active and passive fragments

Fragments in the race enter the ice spectrum at the bin of $m_f$ and collide like any ice. Passive fragments, as in the thesis, go to a separate store and are not counted in the ice number. Computed A 5 µm ice fragment relaxes in 0.30 ms at −10 °C and settles at 3.0 mm/s, about 11 m per hour, so passivity is not a consequence of slow settling. It is a population assumption.

Correction to the earlier write-up

The earlier write-up said the ice number rises “to about 2000 cm⁻³ in 20 minutes for all three λ and stays there”. The retained arrays give 1272–1642 cm⁻³ at 20 minutes, still rising to 1729–2143 at 60. It also said that with passive fragments “the rain fraction behaves the same way”. Early on it does; after an hour the passive runs keep 9–11 % of the mass at radius ≥ 40 µm, against 0.2–4 % in the race runs, where fragments keep feeding ice–ice events.

The passive choice hides the multiplication in the ice number but not its cause. A fixed fraction per event, whatever the partner’s size, drives these results. A yield scaling with the collision energy or the partner’s mass would be a different closure needing physical evidence; conservation cannot choose between them, and the machinery would take either.

The 40 µm threshold

$$F_{40}(t)=\frac{\Delta}{L_0}\sum_{k}\Big[G^{\rm liq}_k(t)\,\mathbb 1\{r_w(x_k)\ge40\ \mu\text{m}\}+G^{\rm ice}_k(t)\,\mathbb 1\{r_i(x_k)\ge40\ \mu\text{m}\}\Big].\tag{61}$$

Mass in bins whose particles are at least 40 µm in radius, as water or as ice, over the starting mass. $\mathbb 1\{\cdot\}$ is 1 when the condition holds, else 0.

$$t_{50}=\min\{t\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\ \text{min}:F_{40}(t)\ge0.5\},\qquad t_{90}=\min\{t:F_{40}(t)\ge0.9\}.\tag{62}$$

The fraction is recorded once a minute, so threshold times are whole minutes, rounded up to the next record.

A rise of $F_{40}$ means that more of the initial water mass sits in the selected large-particle bins. It need not mean that more large particles exist: a few large drops can hold the same mass as many smaller ones. Nor does a falling curve necessarily mean droplets shrank; mass can also leave through the grid’s top edge, as the section of that name below shows.

The solver calls $F_{40}$ “rain”. Its radius of 40 µm is a diameter of 80 µm, a drizzle-sized drop. The model has no updraft and no cloud depth, so it cannot say whether such drops reach the ground. The interface calls it the 40 µm growth threshold; the stored values keep exactly this definition.

Computed $F_{40}$ does not start at zero: 0.2009 in the warm reference cloud on the 8-bins grid, because the collector mode is mostly above 40 µm, and 0.2087 in the cold one, where ice has a 3.6 % larger radius. The start depends on the grid too: 0.2226, 0.2083 and 0.2009 at 2, 4 and 8 bins per doubling. Simulate defaults to 4 and Results shows 8, so the two do not match exactly.

The grid’s top edge

Products heavier than the last bin go to overflow. $F_{40}$ leaves overflow out while the inventory (45) keeps it, so a late fall of $F_{40}$ can be a grid-edge effect with mass conserved. Computed In the warm run with the new kernel, $F_{40}$ is 0.9995 at 30 minutes and 0.994, 0.985, 0.970, 0.948 at 45, 50, 55, 60 minutes, while overflow grows from $6\times10^{-6}$ to 0.006, 0.015, 0.030 and 0.052. At 60 minutes $0.9476+0.0524=1.0000$: the biggest drops passed 2 mm. Enlarge the grid or watch the overflow before calling such a fall physical.

Comparing models fairly

The kernel changes encounter speeds and efficiencies; the population equation changes which events are allowed. Match the initial data and change one ingredient at a time, as chapter 7 does. The retained arrays are snapshots of the earlier solver; rerun with the app’s code, the SCE runs give $F_{40}$ within $5\times10^{-6}$ of the stored values at every minute. They do not certify conclusions for other clouds.

The earlier write-up also quoted a single-mode benchmark, “W1”, in which the thesis kernel reached the threshold in 11 minutes and the new kernel in 43. Its data are not among the app’s retained arrays and its initial spectrum is not specified fully enough to rerun, so this page does not repeat those numbers.

What the model supports

Derived Stokes’ law is not self-consistent above a radius of about 40 µm. Computed At 200 µm it is 3.2 times too fast, and the error enters coalescence squared; collectors below 20 µm collect with efficiencies under 0.001, a bottleneck absent from the thesis kernel; the SCE reaches the 90 % threshold about a quarter sooner than the GCE with either kernel, while the kernel moves it more. Assumed The ice results follow the adopted fragment rule, which grinds large ice down and puts most of the water into fragments within an hour; a different rule would give different curves on the same machinery.

A defensible statement of a result

State the initial spectrum, the model, the observable and the check, then the narrow conclusion. This is supported: “For a homogeneous box at 10 °C and 850 hPa holding 300 droplets per cm³ at 8 µm and 1 per cm³ at 40 µm, with the drag-balance fall speed, the stored collision efficiencies and the thesis’s coalescence law, the SCE on 8 bins per doubling first puts 50 % of the initial mass in particles of radius ≥ 40 µm at minute 6 and 90 % at minute 14; the thesis kernel gives minutes 3 and 5. The mass inventory closes to $10^{-13}$.”

These are not supported by this model: “a warm cloud rains after 14 minutes”, which needs cloud dynamics, condensation and observations; “ice splintering multiplies ice tenfold in real clouds”, which rests on the adopted fragment rule; and “the thesis’s growth times were three times too short in nature”, which compares two models, not a model with a cloud.
