## 1. Q1. Energy behaviour of advection and of energy-conserving discretisations

### 1.1 Selle et al. 2008, "An unconditionally stable MacCormack method", J. Sci. Comput. 35:350-371
Link: https://yingjie.math.gatech.edu/publications/SFKLR.pdf (preprint). Read: intro, section 2, start of section 3.
- Establishes: MacCormack is the "cheap BFECC". Forward semi-Lagrangian (SL) step, backward SL step, error estimate e = (phi_hat_n - phi_n)/2, corrected result phi_{n+1} = A(phi_n) - e. Costs two SL advections instead of three.
- For the ODE y' = lambda y the scheme is identical to RK2; leading truncation error dt^3 lambda^3 y/6. Order 2 in space and time for the wave equation, with leading truncation coefficient E_m(lambda) = 1/lambda^2 - 1 (BFECC: 1/lambda^2 - 3/lambda + 2), derived for CFL lambda <= 1.
- States that the error correction can create new extrema and instability, so a limiter is needed; the scheme "reverts" to first-order SL when the two SL building blocks pull from non-commensurate regions (fluid vs solid wall). The paper says plain SL is very dissipative and that vorticity confinement was the earlier fix.
- Usable: the algorithm and the revert rule. The limiter (clamp to the min/max of the departure-cell corners) is in the part I did not read.
- Does NOT establish (in what I read): any energy law. It gives truncation order, not a dissipation rate for NS. It is stated for reversible advection; the paper itself says BFECC-type correction is wrong for non-reversible terms (heat equation), so it should not be applied to the viscous term. Limited-MacCormack is not energy-conserving; where the limiter fires it is first-order SL again, hence dissipative.
- **[mine]** Plain linear-interpolation SL in 1D with CFL a = U dt/dx is identical to upwind (u_i^{n+1} = (1-a) u_i + a u_{i-1}); its modified equation has numerical viscosity nu_num = U dx (1-a)/2. Glycerol numbers: cell Re = U dx/nu = 10 gives U = 0.44 m/s, a = 0.35, nu_num = 3.6e-3 m^2/s, about 3 times the physical 1.1e-3. This is why SL alone would be far too dissipative and MacCormack helps; limiter activation puts part of it back. Note nu_num falls as dt grows (up to a = 1), the opposite of normal schemes, so refining dt need not reduce the advection loss. Needs a measurement, see recommendation 5.

### 1.2 Morinishi 1995 CTR brief (predecessor of Morinishi et al. 1998)
Link: https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19960022304/downloads/19960022304.pdf. Read in full (12 pages).
- Establishes: in the continuous incompressible equations kinetic energy is conserved by convection and pressure (given div u = 0) and lost only by viscous dissipation. Discrete conservation of mass and momentum does NOT imply discrete conservation of energy; one can have either without the other. The classical second-order centered staggered scheme (Harlow & Welch) conserves kinetic energy; common higher-order staggered schemes (Kajishima 1994, others) do not. Table 1 of the brief gives which convective forms (divergence, advective, skew-symmetric, rotational) are conservative for momentum, v_i^2/2 and K.
- The paper proposes higher-order staggered schemes that conserve all three; the 1998 JCP paper is the refined version. I could not check the 1998 formulas. A 2019 review (https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11831-018-09307-7.pdf, highlights only) says the Morinishi method conserves momentum and kinetic energy to fourth-order accuracy on uniform staggered grids.
- Does not establish: stability at cell Re > 2 or with explicit time stepping. It is a spatial analysis; time integration is separate. Experience claim in the brief: kinetic-energy conservation of the convective term "is required for stable" unsteady simulations (its words, abstract).

### 1.3 Verstappen & Veldman: symmetry-preserving discretisation
Links: conference paper https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_155c0141-cc64-4fbe-a672-38decf130fff (partial read); JCP 187 (2003) 343-368 (not opened).
- Establishes (from the conference paper): write the semi-discrete system as Omega du/dt + C(u) u + D u - M^T p = 0, M u = 0. If C(u) is skew-symmetric (C + C^T = 0) and D is symmetric positive definite, then d/dt(u^T Omega u) = -2 u^T D u <= 0: convection does not touch the energy, viscosity always removes it, "unconditionally stable" in the energy norm on any grid, including non-uniform grids, and for any cell Reynolds number. Truncation-error-optimal stencils on non-uniform grids lose skew-symmetry; that is the motivation.
- A 2019 review (highlights) adds: with the implicit midpoint rule the fully discrete scheme conserves energy exactly for inviscid flow and is unconditionally stable (Verstappen & Veldman; Sanderse 2013 with symplectic RK). Explicit schemes do NOT keep this: forward Euler applied to a skew-symmetric operator is unstable (eigenvalues on the imaginary axis); RK3/RK4 are stable for CFL below about sqrt(3) / 2.8 for the centered 1D operator **[mine, standard stability-region fact]** but add small dissipation of order dt^3 (RK3) / dt^4 (RK4).
- Perot: not read. From memory **[mine]**: Perot's papers show that the staggered-grid projection method is equivalent to an exact LU block factorisation, which is the cleanest way to see the pressure-velocity splitting error.
- Does not establish: any behaviour of the energy under moving immersed boundaries; nor accuracy at cell Re 10 (energy-stable is not the same as non-oscillatory: central schemes at cell Pe > 2 produce wiggles, though energy stays bounded).

### 1.4 Consequence for us
A skew-symmetric (energy-conserving) convective operator plus an explicit RK3 with CFL below about 1.7 would make the advection step lose only the physical viscous dissipation plus a tiny RK term. For glycerol at CFL 0.35 that is feasible. For water (cell Re about 1e4 or more) it is not safe: with no dissipation at the grid scale, energy and enstrophy pile up at 2*dx (in 2D, enstrophy cannot be dissipated by the scheme without a model; see 3).

## 2. Q2. Coupling and projection with a moving body

### 2.1 Batty, Bertails, Bridson 2007, "A fast variational framework for accurate solid-fluid coupling", SIGGRAPH / ACM TOG 26(3)
Link: https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~c2batty/papers/Batty07.pdf. Read in full.
- Establishes: the pressure solve is the minimiser of the total kinetic energy of fluid + rigid body with respect to pressure (the pressure is the Lagrange multiplier of incompressibility). The fluid part is sum of 1/2 m_cell u^{n+1}^2 with u^{n+1} = u* - dt/rho grad p; the rigid body adds 1/2 (V + dt M_S^{-1} J p)^T M_S (V + dt M_S^{-1} J p) (their Eq. 13), which adds dt J^T M_S^{-1} J to the pressure matrix and -J V to the right-hand side. Result: symmetric positive semi-definite system, the body's new velocity is implicit in the pressure (this is the "implicit added mass" they and we use).
- Energy meaning: since the minimised quantity is the kinetic energy and the update is a projection (an orthogonal one in the mass-weighted norm), the projection step cannot increase the total kinetic energy of the fluid+body system; it removes the non-solenoidal/incompatible part. This follows from the paper's statement that the projection is a minimisation of kinetic energy (**[mine]** the sign argument). It is the structure the implicit coupling inherits: the dissipation is the energy of the removed part, of order dt^2 (change in velocity per step)^2 times the mass. They do not state a dissipation rate. The paper itself says nothing about long-time energy drift.
- Limitation they state: the method is first-order in time in the sense of the splitting; they note it cannot stably handle very light solids with other methods, not with theirs; they use near-zero-dissipation FLIP so the numerical dissipation of advection is avoided in their tests. Their weights use volume fractions: the impulse is shared by volume, which Robinson-Mosher says is nonphysical in mixed cells (below).

### 2.2 Robinson-Mosher et al. 2008, "Two-way coupling of fluids to rigid and deformable solids and shells", ACM TOG 27(3):46
Link: https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~shinar/papers/2008_solid_fluid.pdf. Read in full.
- Establishes: momentum-conserving, fully implicit, symmetric (indefinite) system for pressure and solid velocity solved together (their Eq. 13-16, MINRES), stable for arbitrary dt and density ratios; for explicit rigid bodies it reduces to a simple pressure-integration. Algorithm (section 7): advect with a velocity that matches the solid velocity at the interface (prevents leaking), apply non-pressure fluid forces, solve the coupled system, then update solid. It criticises Batty's kinetic-energy-based impulse split as non-conservative of the right torque in mixed cells (their Eq. 3-4 discussion).
- What it does not give: no energy theorem, no dissipation estimate. The abstract/text speaks of momentum conservation and stability, not energy. The coupled step is backward-Euler-like in the solid velocity, hence first-order.
- 2009 follow-up (Robinson-Mosher, Schroeder, Fedkiw): not opened.

### 2.3 Energy-stable partitioned/monolithic FSI (abstract level only)
- Bukac & Trenchea (APS DFD 2018 abstract, https://meetings-archive.aps.org/dfd/2018/d04/5/): partitioned sequence backward-Euler then forward-Euler that reproduces Crank-Nicolson; energy-estimate stability shown unconditional; second-order in time.
- Banks, Henshaw, Schwendeman, Tang, JCP 2017 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcp.2017.04.064): "added-mass partitioned" (AMP) scheme for rigid bodies in incompressible flow; second-order; stable without sub-iterations for light and zero-mass bodies; Robin condition on pressure includes the body's linear and angular acceleration. Closest published analogue of our rigid-cart coupling at second order.
- Other: Eulerian monolithic scheme with implicit Euler (arXiv 1607.08083): energy estimate gives stability, and the authors themselves say first order in time is "somewhat too diffusive". This is the only explicit statement I found that first-order implicit FSI time stepping is dissipative; a general fact (backward Euler damps), not a measurement for our case.
- None of these I read beyond the abstract.

### 2.4 **[mine]** Where a first-order implicit cart coupling loses energy, and a cheap test
Let M_t = M_cart + M_added, force F per step. Backward Euler: M_t (U_{n+1} - U_n) = F dt. Change of kinetic energy of the (cart + added mass) = M_t dU (U_n + U_{n+1})/2 = F dt U_mean. If the energy book credits the motor with F * U_{n+1} dt (end-of-step velocity, which is what "the cart's velocity at the end of the step enters the projection" suggests), the book shows an apparent loss of M_t dU^2/2 per step, i.e. about (1/2) M_t sum(dU^2), first order in dt. Crediting the work with the mean velocity removes this exactly for the cart part. For the fluid side the same argument applies to the impulse the pressure gives the cart. Test: recompute the book with W = F * (U_n + U_{n+1})/2 * dt and see how much of the "coupling" part of R moves into a named, measurable term. This is a bookkeeping check, not a scheme change. It is only a hypothesis until measured.

## 3. Q3. Under-resolved water: implicit vs explicit LES, 2D caveats

### 3.1 Boffetta & Ecke 2012, "Two-dimensional turbulence", Annu. Rev. Fluid Mech. 44:427-451
Link: https://courses.physics.ucsd.edu/2020/Winter/physics116/annurev-fluid-120710-101240.pdf (DOI 10.1146/annurev-fluid-120710-101240). Read (searched; the relevant sections 2-3 and 5 read).
- Eq. 1-2 (vorticity equation with linear friction alpha and forcing). Eq. 5-6: for zero friction and forcing, dE/dt = -2 nu Omega and d(Omega)/dt = -2 nu P (P palinstrophy). Because enstrophy is bounded, the viscous energy dissipation eps_nu -> 0 as nu -> 0: no finite dissipation anomaly in 2D, unlike 3D. Energy is not dissipated by viscosity; it goes to large scales (inverse cascade) and is removed by friction at large scales (eps_alpha). Enstrophy goes to small scales (direct cascade) and is dissipated by viscosity there.
- Inverse cascade: E(k) = C eps_alpha^{2/3} k^{-5/3} (their Eq. 12), measured C between 3 and 7, about 6.0 +/- 0.4 and 6.5 +/- 1 in DNS (Fig. 5), versus about 1.5 in 3D **[mine, memory]**. Direct cascade: about k^-3 with logarithmic correction (I did not read the exact form).
- Many 2D DNS use hyperviscosity and hypofriction instead of Laplacian dissipation to widen the inertial range; the review warns that this "can seriously affect the statistics" at the transition to dissipative scales (citing Lamorgese et al., Frisch et al. 2008, Bos & Bertoglio 2009).
- Does not cover: LES or SGS modelling in 2D (I searched the text for it; nothing substantial), and no statement about ILES.

### 3.2 Grinstein, Margolin, Rider (eds.) 2007, "Implicit Large Eddy Simulation: Computing Turbulent Fluid Dynamics", Cambridge UP
Search-level only (https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/implicit-large-eddy-simulation/numerics-for-iles/0B76BB6FE15171412D33F1D0BCF80D54 and related chapter pages). Not read.
- Claim as given in the chapter summaries: with a non-oscillatory finite-volume scheme the truncation error acts as the subgrid model; modified-equation analysis shows leading truncation terms resemble mixed SGS models; the scheme must be built so these terms have the right SGS properties (not any scheme works). Consequence for our book: the dissipation is real in effect but unnamed; to call it a model term you have to measure it (energy lost with nu = 0), and what it removes in 2D is not the physical sink.

### 3.3 Smagorinsky 1963; Lilly 1967; dynamic models (not opened)
**[mine, from memory, unverified here]** nu_t = (C_s dx)^2 |S|, |S| = sqrt(2 S_ij S_ij); Lilly's isotropic-turbulence argument gives C_s = (1/pi)(3 C_K / 2)^(-3/4) = 0.17 for C_K = 1.5 (3D Kolmogorov constant); practical wall-bounded values are 0.1 to 0.17. Dynamic (Germano) models compute C_s locally; they can go negative (backscatter) and need clipping/averaging. These numbers are all unchecked by me.
**2D consequence (from Boffetta, section 3.1 above plus Lilly's argument):** the Lilly derivation assumes a forward energy cascade. In 2D the forward cascade is of enstrophy, the energy goes backward. A Smagorinsky term removes energy at the grid scale, which in 2D is the wrong budget line; it will damp the large-scale flow more than needed to remove enstrophy. A 2D-appropriate closure should be fitted to enstrophy removal (the Leith-type model is based on the vorticity gradient; I did not read a source for this, **[mine]**). So an explicit Smagorinsky term in 2D is a numerical regulariser, and its coefficient must be reported as a tuned model constant, not as physics.

## 4. Ranked recommendations for our solver

All expected effects are guesses until measured; none is taken from a paper.

1. **Audit the work definition in the book (cost: minutes; risk: none).** Credit the motor with F * mean velocity; separate "cart added-mass term" 1/2 M_t sum(dU^2) as its own line. Expected: part of the "coupling" share of R (about two thirds of 17 % in glycerol) becomes a named first-order bookkeeping term that goes to zero linearly with dt; compare with the measured 17 -> 15 % when dt halves (a pure first-order term would halve R's coupling part, which it does not fully do, so something else is also in play). See 2.4.
2. **Measure the advection loss on a clean problem (cost: small; risk: none).** Decaying Taylor-Green or Lamb-Oseen vortex with nu = 0, without cart, at the same grid and dt, with and without the limiter; also with and without projection. Gives nu_num and shows how much of R is the limiter. Needed before any change to advection, because the 1/3 share and the SL formula in 1.1 predict different things.
3. **Second-order cart coupling (cost: moderate; risk: moderate).** Options: Crank-Nicolson/midpoint on the cart (project with U_{n+1/2}, set U_{n+1} = 2U_{n+1/2} - U_n); or the Banks-Henshaw AMP-type Robin pressure condition (reference only, I have not read it in detail). Expected: coupling part of R falls from O(dt) to O(dt^2) in the smooth regime. Risk: Crank-Nicolson is not damped, so with large added-mass ratio (light cart in heavy fluid; glycerol is heavy) it can oscillate; keep a theta of about 0.55 to 0.6 to retain some damping and re-measure. First check how large M_added/M_cart is.
3b. **Do not substitute the Batty energy-minimisation impulse split for what you have if your cart cells are mixed.** Robinson-Mosher argue it gives the wrong impulse distribution; check your implementation uses a consistent weighting.
4. **Advection (cost: high; risk: moderate).** For glycerol and honey, a skew-symmetric/Morinishi-style second-order central staggered advection with RK3 (CFL below 1.7) would replace the limited MacCormack; expected effect: remove most of the advection third of R (about 5 of 17 percentage points), with grid-scale wiggles possible at cell Re 10 (damped here by the real viscosity: 4 nu/dx^2 is about 7 s^-1 for glycerol, **[mine]**). Not for water. Option in between: keep MacCormack but log energy lost where the limiter fires, as its own line (cost: low), so the loss is named without changing the scheme.
5. **Water: name the dissipation instead of calling it a remainder (cost: low to moderate; risk: honesty).** Two honest options. (a) Keep ILES and label the water remainder "implicit numerical regularisation of the grid scale (ILES)", measured as in recommendation 2 with nu = 0, and say that in 2D it is not physical energy dissipation (Boffetta eq. 5-6). (b) Add an explicit Smagorinsky term (C_s 0.1 to 0.17), store its dissipation at the line where it is computed (2 nu_t S_ij S_ij), and show it as its own book line; state that C_s is a tuned constant and that the inverse cascade makes a forward-energy-cascade closure only a stand-in in 2D. Also the physical sink below the grid (your drag heat) is the real energy sink in a thin-layer picture (Boffetta's friction alpha); keep it separate.
6. **Do not use hyperviscosity to get a smaller R in water** without saying so; Boffetta warns it distorts statistics at the dissipation transition.
