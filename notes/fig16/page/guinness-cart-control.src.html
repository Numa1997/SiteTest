Lecture note · Fig. 16 computed on Kaggle · control · fluid dynamics

Carrying a pint over a kerb

Numa Koudsie · first version, 14 November 2026; the full chapter, refereed as Fig. 12’s was, follows

Abstract. Fig. 12 showed that no controller can save the head of a pint on a one-wheel cart that meets a 30 mm kerb at 1.5 m/s: the road pushes back with up to 82 N, the motor can answer with at most 11.25 N, and the sudden loss of speed sets the stout swinging over the rim. Here the pint is clamped to the deck of a four-wheel cart whose struts carry actuators, and a control system tilts and lifts the deck so that the stout feels almost no sideways push. We derive what the stout feels on a tilting deck, build the control law (optimal state feedback, road preview and a Kalman filter on the glass’s level probes), and read the rides computed with Fig. 12’s own fluid solver: on the kerb the tilt falls from {{f12_kerb_tilt}} to {{f16_kerb_tilt}} mm and the head lost from {{f12_kerb_head}} to {{f16_kerb_head}} mL.

01Why Fig. 12 could not save the head

Walk over a kerb with a full pint in your hand and you do two things without thinking: you slow down before it, and you let your wrist give, so that the glass stays level while your body jolts.

Fig. 12’s cart could do neither well. Its glass stood on a body with one wheel and one motor. When the wheel climbed the 30 mm kerb at 1.5 m/s, the road pushed it back with up to 82 N within about 40 ms, while the motor, held back by its own back-EMF at that speed, could push forward with at most 11.25 N. The cart lost speed suddenly, the stout carried on and climbed the front wall, and with the best of its five controllers {{f12_kerb_head}} mL of the head broke over the rim (tilt {{f12_kerb_tilt}} mm). A controller can only choose what the motor does; it cannot remove the jolt, and it cannot tilt the glass.

So the new cart is built around the two things the hand does: it reads the road ahead, and it can tilt and lift the glass.

02The machine

The glass is clamped to a deck, {{Md}} kg with everything on it (the glass, battery, controller and scanner) plus the {{ml_g}} g of stout and head. The deck stands on four wheels of radius {{Rw_mm}} mm, two axles {{wb_mm}} mm apart. Each wheel hangs on a swing arm hinged under the deck; from the arm a coil spring over a damper rises to a tower on the deck ({{k}} N/m and {{c}} N s/m for each axle’s pair, so the deck alone would bounce at {{f_bounce}} Hz). At the top of each strut sits a voice-coil actuator, a coil in a magnet’s gap that pushes the deck up or down with a force set by its current, up to {{Umax}} N for each axle, and a load cell that weighs what the wheel carries. Two hub motors drive the rear wheels, {{b}} N per ampere each at the road, at most {{Imax}} A from a {{Vmax}} V battery. An inertial measurement unit (IMU) on the deck measures its tilt and acceleration; encoders measure the wheels’ turning; a road scanner on a boom measures the height of the road up to {{preview_s}} s ahead. Fig. 12’s two level probes on the glass’s wall measure the stout’s swing.

Everything that moves the deck therefore moves the glass: the deck heaves (moves up and down) and pitches (tilts nose up or down) as the wheels meet the road, and the actuators can make it heave and pitch on purpose.

03What the stout feels on a tilting deck

Fig. 12 computes the stout in the glass’s own frame, where it feels gravity minus the glass’s acceleration, the effective gravity \(\mathbf g_\text{eff} = \mathbf g - \mathbf a_S\). Its first mode of sloshing is driven by the part of \(\mathbf g_\text{eff}\) that lies across the glass, along the glass’s own forward axis. When the glass is tilted nose up by the angle \(\theta\), that axis is \(\mathbf e_x = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and the axis along the glass is \(\mathbf e_z = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\). With \(\mathbf g = (0, -g)\) and \(\mathbf a_S = (a_x, a_z)\),

\[ g_x = \mathbf g_\text{eff}\cdot\mathbf e_x = -(g + a_z)\sin\theta - a_x\cos\theta, \qquad g_z = \mathbf g_\text{eff}\cdot\mathbf e_z = -(g + a_z)\cos\theta + a_x\sin\theta . \tag{1} \]

The sideways push vanishes when \(g_x = 0\), that is when

\[ \tan\theta^* = -\frac{a_x}{g + a_z} . \tag{2} \]

This is the waiter’s trick: braking (\(a_x \lt 0\)) calls for the glass to tilt nose up, accelerating for nose down, by the angle at which the glass’s axis points along the effective gravity. A kerb that slows the cart at 10 m/s² would ask for 45°, which no deck can give, so the controller cannot simply follow (2); it must spread the jolt in time, lean partly, and use the road scanner to start before the wheel arrives. For the glass the relevant acceleration \(\mathbf a_S\) is that of the point S where the first mode lives, \({{hg_mm}}\) mm above the deck’s centre of mass: the deck’s own pitching adds \(-h\ddot\theta\) to \(a_x\) there, so a deck that tilts too quickly pushes the stout as hard as one that brakes.

Equation (1) is what the fluid solver receives at every step: Fig. 12’s code is unchanged, only the direction and size of its effective gravity now follow the deck. The rotation of the glass also adds forces that vary across it (\(\ddot\theta\,r\) and \(\dot\theta^2 r\) about S); they are left out, and section 7 says how large they are.

04The control system ★

The controller sees the cart through twelve numbers, the state: the error of the speed and its integral, the deck’s height and pitch and their rates, the two axles’ heights and rates, and the swing of the stout \(q\) (its height at the front wall) and its rate. It acts with three: the drive force of the hub motors and the forces of the front and rear actuators. Newton’s law for the deck, the axles and the cart, with the first mode of Fig. 12 (frequency {{f0}} Hz, damping ratio {{zeta}}, drive coefficient {{beta}}, as run 8 measured them), linearised about steady driving, gives

\[ \dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x + B\mathbf u + E\mathbf w , \tag{3} \]

where \(\mathbf w\) holds the road’s height and slope under the two axles. Sampled every {{Ts_ms}} ms, the controller minimises the cost \(\sum_k (\mathbf x_k^\top Q\mathbf x_k + \mathbf u_k^\top R\mathbf u_k)\), with each weight the inverse square of the largest acceptable value (Bryson’s rule): {{q_mm}} mm for the swing, {{th_deg}}° for the pitch, {{stroke_mm}} mm for the struts’ travel, 40 N for each force. The answer is the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) with preview,

\[ \mathbf u_k = -K\mathbf x_k - \sum_{j=0}^{N_p-1} K_j\,\mathbf w_{k+j}, \qquad K_j = (R + B_d^\top P B_d)^{-1}B_d^\top (A_\text{cl}^\top)^{j} P E_d , \tag{4} \]

where \(P\) solves the discrete Riccati equation, \(A_\text{cl} = A_d - B_dK\), and \(N_p = {{Np}}\) samples ({{preview_s}} s) of road come from the scanner. The first term is feedback: it answers what has already happened. The second is the feed-forward of a disturbance that is known in advance: each future piece of road is weighted by how much the closed loop will still remember of it when it arrives. The swing \(q\) and its rate are estimated by a Kalman filter that predicts them from the IMU’s measured acceleration and corrects them with Fig. 12’s two wall probes once per fluid step. The weighting was chosen in the notebook itself, among {{ncand}} candidates tried on the kerb with a few-mode model of the stout validated on Fig. 12’s stored rides; the one with the least head spilled was kept.

05How the rides were computed

Every ride was computed on Kaggle, a service that rents graphics cards, in the notebook fig-16-guinness-cart-with-control on a {{device}}: version {{nb_version}} (14 November: the gentle roads with control), version 4 (15 November: the kerb with control, the turning glass’s forces included) and version 7 (15 November: every road without control, with the volume correction). The stout is Fig. 12’s: about a million particles of stout on a 1.5 mm grid, the head of foam with its mass, the drops and lumps that leave over the rim; its code is unchanged. The cart and its controller are a new module, stepped every 0.1 ms inside each fluid step and pushed back by the stout’s change of momentum, as in Fig. 12. Before any ride the pint stood still for 2 s and had to stay flat and full (it did, to {{rest_max_mm}} mm). The books closed in every stored picture: no particle of stout was lost, and the head in the glass plus the head broken off equals the head served to {{head_err}} mL. A step took about {{ms_step}} ms; the four rides and the rest took {{gpu_min}} minutes of the card.

Without control the deck rocks on its soft struts, and a tilt of 9° alone lifts the stout 5.7 mm at the wall (R tan 9° with R = 36 mm), more than the 3.5 mm between the head and the rim. So the head goes over at the first bump and the stout follows. On the five bumps it is worse than a single jolt. The deck’s pitch and the stout’s swing are coupled into one natural mode at 3.85 Hz (damping ratio 0.33: the eigenvalues of the cart’s linear model, computed on Kaggle). The bumps, 0.29 m apart at 1.0 m/s, rock the deck 3.45 times a second, inside that mode’s band, so each bump adds to the rocking the last one left. With control the deck stays nearly level, and the glass feels almost no sideways push.

07Limits, and what comes next

The control system is at its edge on the kerb: the motors saturate and the actuators come close to theirs. The estimator’s states other than the swing are taken from ideal sensors. The carts are planar: both wheels of an axle meet the same road, with no roll and no yaw.

Corrected on 15 November 2026. Three faults of the first version were found and mended, and the rides computed again on Kaggle.

  • The turning glass. The stout was given only the effective gravity of one point of the glass. A glass that turns also pushes on the stout across its width: the Euler force (from the change of the turning rate), the centrifugal force and the Coriolis force. Without control the deck’s turning rate changes by up to 154 rad/s², which across a 0.1 m glass is about 15 m/s², more than g. All three forces are now in the fluid. With control they change the kerb’s tilt by less than the scatter between two seedings (0.8 mm rms against 0.75 mm).
  • The swelling stout. After violent splashing the particles of stout stayed spread apart, so a glass that had lost a fifth of its stout was drawn fuller than at the start (by 18 to 48 % in volume). A correction now draws drifted particles back to their resting spacing, but only while the whole pint has swollen by more than 4 %. It moves positions only, never velocities. The uncontrolled rides now keep their volume to within 1.5 %. On the five bumps this changed the stout spilled from 103 g to 68 g.
  • The pictures. The wheels looked as if they turned backwards. At 30 pictures a second five spokes are sampled too slowly, the wagon-wheel effect of films. The wheel is now drawn at the screen’s rate from the stored position, which is kinematics and not stored physics, and the new rides store 60 pictures a second.
